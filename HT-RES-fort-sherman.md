# Fort Sherman: the army post a city grew around — and the troops it sent into the mining war

Research draft for Dash QC • 2026-11-18 • local only, no upload or outreach.

**Trip gate:** Fort Sherman is an exterior, walk-the-grounds site on the open campus of North Idaho College — the chapel, officers' quarters, and powder magazine stand among college buildings and can be viewed from public campus paths in any season. The one interior with published hours is the Fort Sherman Chapel, owned by the Museum of North Idaho: per the museum's own visit page it opens **June through August only, Thursdays and Fridays, 11 a.m.–3 p.m., admission by donation** — in November it is closed for the season, and no entry should promise otherwise. The Museum of North Idaho itself (720 E. Young Ave.) runs winter hours October–April, Tuesday–Saturday 10 a.m.–4 p.m. Local time: Pacific. [F4]

## 1. The story

The post began as an inspection-tour decision. After the Nez Perce War of 1877, General William T. Sherman toured the Northwest posts and recommended a post on the north shore of Lake Coeur d'Alene. Camp Coeur d'Alene was established April 16, 1878. The Idaho State Historical Society's Reference Series No. 355 gives the post's three reasons for existing: "(1) keeping the peace in northern Idaho, (2) protecting railroad and telegraph crews, and (3) guarding the border with Canada." The name changed to Fort Coeur d'Alene on April 5, 1879, and to Fort Sherman on April 6, 1887. Sherman himself never commanded or served at the post — the name is an honor, not a posting; a persistent visitor-review misconception on that point is worth correcting in the entry rather than repeating. [F2, F6]

The fort was built on ground that was already a center of population. The University of Idaho's archaeological thesis on the post — produced in collaboration among the university, North Idaho College, and the Coeur d'Alene Tribe — states plainly that the fort stood "on the ancestral lands of the Coeur d'Alene tribe, the Schitsu'umsh, at Hnch'mqinkwe, their largest village in the region." The army's sawmill, built in 1878, was the first in northern Idaho; it cut the lumber for more than fifty military buildings on a reservation of roughly 640 acres. The city of Coeur d'Alene grew up beside the post from 1878 onward — the fort is not a ruin outside the city; it is the city's founding neighbor, and its grounds are now the college campus at the city's western edge. [F5, F6]

The post's hardest service was not against any tribe but in the Coeur d'Alene mining country. During the mining troubles of 1892, ISHS No. 355 records, "the troops received a call to restore order. The soldiers, under the command of Colonel William P. Carlin, left the fort on July 12, 1892. They were equipped in heavy marching gear which consisted of eight rations and 100 rounds of ammunition per man. They were transported on the Northern Pacific Railroad Company's steamer to Harrison and thence by rail to Wardner… Upon arrival the troops aided the civil authorities in making arrests and preserving martial law. In September of 1892, subposts were created at Wallace, Burke, Gem, and Wardner… The subposts were disbanded in November, 1892." A federal courts history of the era adds the chain of authority: at Governor Norman B. Willey's request, President Benjamin Harrison ordered the Fort Sherman troops in, martial law was declared, and union miners were arrested en masse. Four companies of the 4th Infantry from Fort Sherman marched alongside three companies of the 25th Infantry — Buffalo Soldiers — from Fort Missoula. The garrison of a peacekeeping post spent the summer of 1892 as an occupying force in the mining towns this app's Silver Valley entries describe from the other side. [F2, F7, F9]

The end came in stages, and the sources must be kept in their lanes. The command departed for the Spanish-American War on April 21, 1898, leaving the post nearly empty. Hawley's 1920 *History of Idaho* states that when martial law was declared again in Shoshone County in 1899, "troops from Fort Sherman were ordered into the Coeur d'Alene mining districts" — but the federal courts history says President McKinley sent the 1899 troops from Fort Boise and posts in Montana, Utah, Wyoming, and Washington, which fits a fort whose garrison had sailed in 1898 and whose last listed post commanders were lieutenants. Both accounts are printed in Section 5; the entry must not assert a Fort Sherman role in 1899 as settled fact. Hawley records that the troops were removed to Spokane in the fall of 1899 and the fort "formally abandoned in August, 1901"; abandonment orders had already been issued before the 1899 riots, and an Idaho Legislature petition to convert the post to a national soldiers' home was refused. [F2, F7, F8]

## 2. Then / now

Then, a fifty-building frontier post: commanding officer's and officers' quarters, barracks, hospital, library, guardhouse, chapel, sawmill, bakery, blacksmith and carpenter shops, stables, ice houses — the 2023 thesis inventories them from period records. Now, the same ground is a college campus and the Fort Grounds neighborhood, and the post survives as a scatter of individual buildings interpenetrated by dormitories, tennis courts, and private homes. The National Register nomination is candid that the district is five buildings, each ringed by a ten-foot boundary — an archipelago, not a preserved fort. There is no wall, no parade-ground reconstruction, and no single gate; the visitor experience is a walk between survivors, which the stop progression below is built to honor rather than disguise. [F1, F6]

## 3. Surviving features and access classification

- **Fort Sherman Chapel (c. 1880):** red clapboard, round-arched openings; the nomination notes War Department design documents dated 1879. Donated to the Museum of North Idaho in 1984 after a 1942 civic rescue by the Athletic Round Table. Interior open June–August, Thu–Fri 11–3, by donation (museum's own page). Exterior viewable year-round from Hubbard Street (332 Hubbard St.). The museum also rents it for events, so a closed door on a summer weekday may mean a wedding, not a closure. [F1, F4]
- **Officers' Quarters No. 1 (McHugh Building) and No. 2 (McCormac Building):** two-story frame residences of the 1880s; McHugh "little altered" per the nomination. In college use (reported as offices) — exterior viewing from Garden Avenue; no public interior. See the survivor-count disagreement in Section 5 before writing "only one quarters survives" or "two survive." [F1]
- **Powder Magazine:** one-story red brick, gable roof. The original magazine burned in 1885 and was rebuilt with bricks made on the fort grounds. It housed the Museum of North Idaho from the museum's opening on July 28, 1973; the museum has since moved to 720 E. Young Ave. Current interior use/access of the magazine: not stated on the museum's current visit page — treat as exterior-only until the museum confirms. [F1, F4]
- **The hay barn:** the fifth building in the original 1974 nomination; the later nomination text records it as lost. Do not list it as surviving. [F1]
- **North Idaho College campus grounds:** public college campus; the interpretive frame is self-guided. Museum-led walking tours of Fort Sherman have been offered seasonally (Saturday tours April–September per museum programming) — seasonal programming, not a standing schedule; verify for the travel date. [F4]

## 4. Trip-shaped stop progression

**1. Begin at the Museum of North Idaho (720 E. Young Ave.) if it is open.** Its exhibits cover the fort, steamboats, and the region, and its staff operate the chapel — the museum is the post's living interpreter, and checking the day's reality (chapel open? tour running?) happens here, not at a locked door. Winter hours Tue–Sat 10–4. [F4]

**2. Walk the campus to the Powder Magazine.** Brick, small, and rebuilt after an 1885 fire from bricks made on the grounds — the most purely functional survivor, and the building that spent 1973 onward as the region's museum. Exterior only unless the museum says otherwise. [F1]

**3. Officers' Row on Garden Avenue.** Read the quarters as domestic architecture, not military spectacle: verandahs and clapboard, officers' families, a garrison town's social top floor. Exterior only. [F1]

**4. End at the Chapel on Hubbard Street.** In summer open hours, go in: it served as church, school, library, and meeting hall — the post's all-purpose room — and it is the survivor closest to original appearance. Out of season, the stop still works from the steps: this is the building a civic club rescued in 1942 and a museum keeps now. [F1, F4]

**Strongest story moment:** standing between the chapel and the campus, deliver the 1892 turn. The soldiers who marched out of this quiet ground on July 12, 1892 — eight rations, a hundred rounds a man, by steamer and rail to Wardner — were sent to arrest the miners of Burke, Gem, and Wallace, towns the traveler can drive to in under an hour. Fort Sherman is the Silver Valley labor war's other endpoint; the entry should say so in ISHS's own flat words ("aided the civil authorities in making arrests and preserving martial law") and let the flatness carry the weight. [F2]

## 5. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Renaming date:** ISHS No. 355 — a state reference publication — gives Fort Sherman's name from April 6, 1887. Hawley's 1920 history says the name changed "after the death of Gen. Sherman in 1891," which cannot be squared with 1887 (Sherman died February 1891). Print 1887 as the documented date and Hawley's as the older history's error-prone alternative; do not silently merge them. [F2, F8]
- **Reservation size:** the University of Idaho thesis gives approximately 640 acres for the original military reservation; Hawley gives 1,000 acres; the Sherman Park nomination speaks of "more than eight hundred acres" of reservation land available after abandonment. Three figures, three scopes or sources. Print the disagreement; adopt none. [F6, F8]
- **How many buildings survive — four, or three?** The National Register record and the 2023 thesis count four: chapel, powder magazine, and TWO officers' quarters (McHugh and McCormac). Multiple local accounts, including a detailed Idaho Magazine walking narrative, say only three remain and call the Garden Avenue house "the only remaining one from Officer's Row." The likely reconciliation — one quarters altered, moved, or reclassified — is a hypothesis, not a finding. Field verification against the college's own building list is a release gate for any survivor count in public copy. [F1, F6]
- **1899 role disputed:** Hawley (1920) sends Fort Sherman troops into the 1899 martial law; the U.S. District Court's published history sends troops from Fort Boise and four other states' posts, and ISHS's own commander list shows the post run by lieutenants after the April 1898 departure for Cuba. Print both; assert neither. [F7, F8, F2]
- **Abandonment year:** Hawley: troops removed fall 1899, formal abandonment August 1901. The Register's period of significance for the post is commonly stated 1878–1900. "Closed 1900 / abandoned 1901" is a defensible pairing only if both halves are attributed. [F8]
- **Powder magazine's present use** is unstated in current operator material retrieved; the museum's move to Young Avenue is documented, the magazine's current interior status is not. Exterior-only until confirmed.
- **No decimal coordinates adopted.** The Register's county listing prints a district point derived from NRIS data, but no primary register document read in this pass prints coordinates; per the locator rule, the fields stay blank rather than inherit an aggregator's point.

## 6. Candidate physical finds and onsite questions

Proposed tasks for public campus paths only; not active challenges. Nothing requires entering college buildings or the chapel outside its posted hours.

1. **Count the survivors yourself:** the Register-era record describes a chapel with round-arched window and door openings contrasting with its angular red clapboard. From Hubbard Street, count the round arches on the chapel's street face and record whether any opening has been squared off or filled. Answer/key: blank until field check. [F1]
2. **Bricks made on the grounds:** at the Powder Magazine, examine the brickwork from the public path: is the brick uniform manufactured brick, or visibly varied in color and size as small-batch, on-site production would suggest? Record observations only — no touching loose material, no collecting. Answer/key: blank. [F1]
3. **Officers' Row arithmetic:** period records describe eight officers' homes along the north side of the parade ground on what is now Garden Avenue. From the public sidewalk, how many 1880s-form houses can you identify on that row today, and what stands where the others stood? This task directly field-tests the Section 5 survivor disagreement; it stays disabled until a checker walks it with the nomination in hand. Answer/key: blank. [F1, F6]
