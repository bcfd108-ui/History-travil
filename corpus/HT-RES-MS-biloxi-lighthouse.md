# Biloxi Lighthouse and the keepers who answered for the light

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP22 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
A small tower arrived at Biloxi in pieces. Congress funded a lighthouse in 1847. A Baltimore foundry won the contract for a bolted iron tower, a separate supplier provided the lamps and reflectors, and Henry Scoles supervised erection after shipment on the brig *General North* in 1848. Early keeper Marcellus J. Howard tended oil lamps supplied by Winslow Lewis. Brick inside the conical iron shell and a stair wound around a central post gave the keeper a way to reach the light in bad weather. The tower was built through several kinds of labor: casting, freight, assembly and years of keeping a light burning. [1, 3]

Howard’s successor, Mary Reynolds, put that work into words. In a letter to Governor John J. Pettus, reproduced in part in the National Register nomination, she wrote, “I ascended the Tower at and after the last destructive storm (1860)”. Reynolds was asking for control of the light back after, according to the nomination’s account of her complaint, local Home Guards took the key in June 1861 and oil disappeared. The episode records her account and request. The governor’s reply and whether she immediately recovered control are unverified. [3]

The appointment passed to Perry Younghans in 1866. Following his death Maria Younghans kept the light, and her daughter Miranda officially assisted and succeeded her. Miranda served into 1929. The Coast Guard assumed responsibility in 1939. Before automation, someone had to climb the stair, maintain the lighting apparatus and take responsibility for the signal in storms and ordinary darkness. The nomination relates the signal specifically to harbor-bound oyster, shrimp and lumber traffic. [1, 3]

## 3. Then and now
A 1901 photograph puts the tower on a working waterfront surrounded by outbuildings, houses and a timber edge. The surviving cast-iron tower now occupies the median of U.S. 90. Katrina wrecked glazing and electrical equipment and loosened interior brick. The city identifies contractor J.O. Collins for the restoration preceding the March 2010 reopening for tours. [1, 3, 4]

Across the highway, Biloxi opened its visitors center in 2011. It echoes the Dantzler House that stood on that site until Katrina. The city’s description identifies the familiar porches, large windows and staircase as features of the new building’s design. The visitor center is modern municipal interpretation and provides the public viewing boundary for this draft. [2]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The visitors center publishes weather-dependent tours and WLOX reported an October 2026 reopening. The city’s lighthouse page still says top tours are temporarily unavailable for upper decking repairs. The regular visitor center is verified. Scope the trip there while the climb conflict stands. [1, 2, 5]
- The nomination describes fifth-order power in its account of the early light. The city specifically records a fourth-order Fresnel lens installed in 1856 after the original nine lamps and reflectors. The 1848 and 1856 systems were different. Keep the original lamp array separate from the later lens and leave the nomination’s order wording unresolved. The nomination also records 53 feet from ground to light, while the city reports a 64-foot tower. These are different measurement scopes. [1, 3]
- The nomination prints excerpts of Reynolds’s letter. The original manuscript and full reply were not read. Two U.S. Coast Guard endpoints, its dedicated station page and the historic Mississippi lights PDF, returned HTTP 403. [3, 6, 7]
