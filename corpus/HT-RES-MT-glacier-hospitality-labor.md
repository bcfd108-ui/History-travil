## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** scenery is not the whole product of a resort hotel. Guests also encounter work: welcoming, feeding, cleaning, performing and sustaining a public place. A worker's account can restore the person who disappears behind the phrase “guest experience.”

**Named published primary voice:** Diane Sine speaks in the National Park Service's *Headwaters*, season 1, episode 4, **“Confluence | Many Glacier,”** in the transcript's **HOTEL** segment. The hosts describe her earlier work as a singing waitress. In her own account, hotel employment lasted four years before she became a ranger. [5]

Her short first-person statement is **“I was hooked on this place and I figured out how to become a park ranger.”** The transcript was read directly; audio was not listened to and no unverified audio timestamp is supplied. [5]

The account connects a service job to a later working identity rather than treating seasonal employment as an anonymous prelude to somebody else's vacation. It does not establish that every employee had the same experience. Her recollection of the hotel's musical hiring tradition remains attributed testimony, not a complete set of employment rules.

**Original visitor narration:** When a worker says a place changed her life, the building becomes more than a place where visitors slept. It becomes a workplace that could also shape a vocation. Ask what the hotel looks like from the other side of the table: not as a room you bought for a night, but as somewhere you returned to work. That question does not require entering the staff side of the building.

Sine also recalls family connections to the hotel. Those personal memories help explain attachment; they do not authorize visiting private or cultural locations, repeating a wedding route or looking for relatives. [5]

This is one named woman's recollection, not a comprehensive account of concession labor. Pay, workload, accommodation, recruitment, turnover, discrimination and differing employee experiences remain research questions. Neither nostalgia nor a scenic setting resolves them. The episode's wider historical commentary is not adopted wholesale, and its other outdoor narratives are not visitor instructions.

No Tribal history is reconstructed from hotel advertising or an employee's memories. A fuller account of the region and tourism would require relevant Tribal-published histories. This dossier stays with the supported hospitality-work voice and explicitly does not describe the land as empty before tourism.

## 3. Then / now
- **Then:** the worker's earlier hospitality job is recalled through a later published interview. It is not a contemporary payroll or employment contract.
- **Now:** Many Glacier Hotel remains a seasonal concession operation. Its operator dates construction to 1914–15 and describes later renovations; the present interior should not be assumed unchanged from either the original building or Sine's employment years. [1]
- **Now, a different institution:** Apgar is a park visitor center, not the hotel and not a verified exhibit about this particular worker. Do not replace one with the other on a map and claim an equivalent experience.

Original comparison prompt: what can a building photograph show about hospitality, and what needs an actual worker's voice? The point is to combine different kinds of evidence without pretending that either is complete.
