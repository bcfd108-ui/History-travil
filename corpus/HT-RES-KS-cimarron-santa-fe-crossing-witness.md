# Cimarron Crossing — A letter at the Arkansas

## 2. The story

**A road with a decision in it.** The Santa Fe Trail ran west along the Arkansas. At the middle ford, travelers could leave the river corridor for the Cimarron route, while the Mountain Route continued west. Louise Barry brought together guidebooks that called this ford different things. The term Cimarron Crossing appears regularly in her tables for the 1860s. This was a crossing of the Arkansas River near present Cimarron, Kansas. The Cimarron River was elsewhere on the route. [2,3]

**Who worked at the fork.** Brothers William and John Francis “Frank” Hartwell had tried a stage ranch farther east. William’s reminiscences, reproduced in Barry's study with editing, associate their move with a stage superintendent's need for a ranch at the crossing. The ranch opened in 1866. Its people supplied travelers and tended animals. The NPS reproduces the site's interpretive description of sod roofs, a corral and stables. What the traveler sees now is a municipal park and interpretation. The station’s dimensions and fabric come from the record. [2,3]

**A family has to be told.** Mabillion W. McGee wrote from Cimarron Crossing to Adam Hill on June 17, 1867, after learning about a fatal incident the day before from freighter Charley Parker. The letter’s physical observation was plain: “The river is very high.” His account of the violence was partly relayed from Parker. The letter names Curtis Hill and identifies other people as a Frenchman and Mexicans without supplying their full identities. Its list of absences shows where the traveler record goes silent. The NPS places the episode amid territorial expansion into Native homelands. The military and settler accounts do not supply the Native participants’ own voices. Quiet reflection leaves that evidentiary gap visible. [2,3]

## 3. Then / now

**Historic location.** NPS places the former ranch at the present park. The precise ford line, event locations and burials are separate historical questions. The present channel and public boundaries cannot establish them. [2,3]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Cimarron Crossing Park, City of Cimarron. Use its signed drive-up and the public short walk to available park interpretation. Stay within the municipal park and away from water and private land. The park is where the ranch story can be read. Its name does not authorize tracing the ford. [1,2]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Start with the public interpretive exhibit. Read the divergence of routes and why stage animals and people required supplies. [2,3]
2. From dry park ground, orient to the river if visible. Compare the landscape with the three historic distance tables without attempting their route. [2,3]
3. **Strongest story moment — The letter to Adam Hill.** Read the dated river observation and the notice to the family. Pause at the park. There is no reenactment, grave search or river access. [3]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The historic Fort Dodge-to-crossing figures Barry prints are about 25, 25.57 and 27 miles, with differing sources and precision. They are not modern navigation. [3]
- Hartwell’s later transcription of his brother’s gravestone gives July 17, 1867. Barry’s visit to the inscription and the published genealogy report July 18. The loss remains witness-only. [3]
- The McGee letter was read through the historical society’s published transcription. Original manuscript, a Tribe-published account of this specific incident, verified ford alignment and day-of park conditions remain outstanding. City office hours printed on the municipal page are not park hours. [1–3]
