## 2. The story
**Named archival worker evidence:** the museum identifies **Norma Stoddard** as a civilian telephone operator at the wartime base and publishes a photograph collection in her name. Its description says the collection records women, social interactions and life in the town and base. This is institutional attribution of a personal photographic record, not a retrieved interview or direct quotation by Stoddard. [2][4]

A second named household collection belongs to **Don and Eloise Hagedorn**. The institution states that they lived in Wendover while Don was stationed there and Eloise worked on the base in a civilian capacity. Her specific job title, dates and own written account are not given on the collection page. Do not label her a telephone operator simply because Stoddard was one. [5]

**Voice gate: GAPPED.** These are named personal collections, but no spoken interview, signed letter, diary passage or fully identified first-person caption by either woman was recovered. Photographs can preserve a participant's perspective without supplying words. This draft deliberately does not invent a quotation or call institutional description firsthand testimony. It remains a bounded photographic/community-evidence draft pending the requested verbal worker voice.

**Original interpretive angle:** a wartime installation was also a place where people went to work, formed friendships and kept photographs. The ordinary social record does not make war ordinary or erase its human consequences. It changes the scale at which a visitor asks questions: who performed civilian work, what did they choose to preserve, and which parts of their lives remain outside the record?

A named woman in a catalog is a stronger beginning than an anonymous symbol, but it is not the end of research. Employment status, collection ownership, photographic authorship and the identities of pictured people are different facts. Keep them separate.
