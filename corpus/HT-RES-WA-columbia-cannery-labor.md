## 2. The story
Start with a worker's world, not the brand on a can. **Daniel Stephan**, identified as a Chinook fisherman, recalled growing up near Pillar Rock in a **2011 interview**: **“I really absorbed the fishing life”**. The published excerpt places his father's engine work beside his own eagerness to fish. The accompanying account names **Martha Stephan** and her skill hanging and mending nets, learned from her father. It describes economic participation alongside exclusion, displacement and abuse, not a uniformly prosperous cannery community. [6]

These are not anonymous picturesque figures around a factory. Catching, maintaining boats and repairing gear supported the packing industry while extending family knowledge. Our interpretation is that a labor history confined to the cannery doorway misses much of the work. Daniel is a community/fishing voice, **not presented as a canning-line employee**, and Martha's account is mediated through the project's narrative rather than a newly checked full transcript.

Source authority matters: **Chinook Story** identifies itself as a collaboration between the **Chinook Indian Nation and Portland State University's History Department**. Its account says the Nation's Culture Committee reviewed pages and participated in supplying and revising content. [7] Thus this is a published Tribal-partner interpretation with an attributed community voice, not a claim that every sentence is a solely Tribal-authored official statement. No new interview or contact occurred.

The primary visual record brings the story inside a Washington plant. **John F. Ford's photograph of women labeling salmon cans at Megler Cannery, Brookfield, Washington**, is cataloged **OrHi 56468**, approximately **1901**. The custodian's explanatory text narrows the probable exposure to **1900–1902**. These are compatible levels of dating precision, not two verified exact dates. [8] The workers are unnamed in the retrieved description; do not assign identities, wages or family histories to them.

A second named community voice explains why this image matters. In the museum's 2026 exhibition announcement, collector and Chinook Observer publisher **Matt Winters** writes that labels can remain after canneries disappear, recalling **“places where hundreds of men and women once toiled, dreamed and died.”** [3] This is a modern published community reflection, not firsthand testimony from the photograph's subjects. It supplies the stop's strongest question: when a colorful brand survives better than the workers' names, what does the visitor choose to remember?

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Suggested museum-scale sequence, not a timed route or admission reservation:
1. **Orient at the public museum.** Confirm that today's open galleries include the relevant regional interpretation; do not enter closed rooms or follow a historical map beyond public areas.
2. **Read work as a network.** Use whichever fishing/community displays are actually available to distinguish harvesting, processing, maintenance and household labor. The questions are ours; no undocumented display is promised.
3. **Pause with the archival photograph.** On a personal device, compare the documented labeling task with any legitimately displayed packaging example. If none is present, the digital photograph alone is sufficient. Do not photograph exhibits without the museum's applicable permission.
4. **End with the people, not the logo.** Return to the attributed community voices in section 2. The strongest story moment is recognizing that a saleable image can outlast the names and conditions of the people whose work made it possible. This is interpretation, not a claim that the pictured women died at work.
