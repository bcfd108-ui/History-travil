# The freight depot, a Stovall cabin, and the people who recorded Muddy Waters

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP18*

## 2. Story

Clarksdale’s former railroad freight depot held materials being sent on elsewhere. Its current museum holds some of the Delta’s musical record. The museum began in 1979 with the Carnegie Public Library trustees and became a standalone institution in 1999. The state inventory and operator identify the structure as the Yazoo and Mississippi Valley Railroad’s former freight depot. That is the building on Blues Alley where the public visits today. [1–3]

A surviving part of a different building is inside. McKinley Morganfield, whose records became known under the name Muddy Waters, lived on Stovall Farms in the years the museum associates with sharecropping and tractor work. The operator says the preserved cabin remains came from Stovall and describes a 1941 recording made at the cabin. The Library of Congress fieldwork guide specifies McKinley “Muddy Waters” Morganfield and Henry “Son” Simms at Stovall on discs AFS 4769–4770, alongside a larger joint project involving the Library of Congress and Fisk University. The guide is a keyed reconstruction from records such as notebooks and discs. It ties the session to its collaborators and period documentation. The publicly searchable item for “Country blues” could not be retrieved through its original LOC endpoint during this pass and no audio wording is quoted. [4–6]

In Indianola in 1980, B.B. King explained to MDAH interviewer John Jones that “Muddy Waters would be one of the most influential” among the Mississippians he was discussing. That line is from the printed MDAH interview transcript. King’s recollection is evidence of what a younger musician heard and valued. Read it beside the Stovall recording records, which hold a much earlier and differently situated view of Morganfield’s work. The museum allows a visitor to set those kinds of evidence beside material that came from his household. [4–7]

## 3. Then and now

The former freight depot is the visitor’s historical building. The cabin fabric belongs to the separate Stovall Farms setting and is displayed within the modern museum. It carries a documented connection to Morganfield without re-creating the original farm, yard and approach outside the depot. [2–5]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Staffed interior.** Depot galleries and the operator-listed cabin exhibit, subject to current opening and exhibit changes. The operator prohibits ordinary photography and video in the museum. [1][2][4]
- **Public exterior.** The signed approach to the depot entrance. No tracks, former freight operations ground or farmland are included. [1–3]
- **Historical record only.** Stovall is named in the archival recording records and the museum’s object history. The original household site is outside this visitor progression. [4–6]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

Enter the public museum during ticketed hours. Begin with the depot building, then move to the cabin exhibit if open. **Strongest story moment — Two buildings and one recorded voice.** Read the cabin label and compare the material inside the freight depot with the job and address in the Stovall record. Keep the building you entered, the cabin material you can see, and the places where Morganfield’s music was recorded distinct as you read. [1–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- MDAH dates the freight depot circa 1910, while the museum gives 1918. No original construction contract, dated plan or opening notice was retrieved that resolves the mismatch. Use early twentieth century for the building. Both agree it is the former railroad freight depot. [2][3]
- The museum specifies a front-porch session. The LOC fieldwork guide identifies the dates and Stovall session records. The precise position of the microphones and individual surviving timbers remain unverified. [4][5]
- The LOC audio resource and collection-level finding-aid view returned HTTP 403. The downloadable field-trip manuscript guide was readable. A named early primary recording has been identified without quoting unheard audio. [5][6][8]
