## 2. The story
The landscape’s grain is visible. The work that kept a household and harvest functioning is easier to miss. This dossier pairs two named women’s records with a public domestic-history setting while preserving the differences among them.

**Ruth Blair (Wiley), recorded recollection:** Washington State University holds Whitman County Historical Society interviews from 1977 and 1981. This draft uses **Richard Hamm’s April 11, 1981 interview**, its 14-page transcript, not the unheard audio. Blair recalled coming from Kansas in 1908, helping with family responsibilities, and life around Palouse and Elberton. On printed page 8 she remembered the arrival of electricity but explicitly could not give its year. Her short response, **“It sure helped!”**, was checked visually in the original transcript scan. It captures change without inventing a precise electrification date. [2,3]

Pages 10–11 distinguish later freezer use from earlier cellar storage; the interviewer’s follow-up prevents a modern practice from being assigned to her first years in Washington. The discussion also links household production to trips into town. This is retrospective individual memory, not a census of all Palouse farms. [3]

**May Christine MacFarlane Hull, a contemporary visual statement:** Whitman County Heritage catalogs a 1910 photographic postcard showing Hull with a homemade **“VOTES FOR WOMEN.”** sign. The image itself was downloaded and visually checked. The repository identifies her as a mother of five at the time and a member of a homesteading household near Colfax. Household and agricultural life did not exclude a public political identity. The photograph proves neither the scale of her organizing nor how every neighboring household viewed suffrage. [4,5]

Original interpretive voice: *The harvest needed more than the people visible beside a machine. It needed food kept through winter, journeys into town and decisions made inside households. Those households also held opinions about who should have a voice beyond the farm gate.* This is our synthesis, not a quotation from Blair or Hull. No connection between the two women or the Perkins family is asserted.

**The anchor is deliberately a comparison, not a stand-in.** The society dates the Perkins Victorian house to 1887 and describes its later restoration. A preserved substantial town residence cannot represent every farm household, hired worker, tenant or family with fewer resources. Its domestic setting offers questions to carry into the records, not proof of how Blair’s or Hull’s home looked. [1]

This is a bounded settler-household and civic-change entry, not a complete history of the Palouse. Indigenous land relationships and displacement require Tribal-published sources before interpretation. The rural economy’s varied workers and unequal access to land also remain underrepresented here.
