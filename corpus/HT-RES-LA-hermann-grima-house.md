# Hermann-Grima House — a Federal mansion, its courtyard work yard, and the enslaved household of St. Louis Street
HT-RES-LA-hermann-grima-house · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The house was built as an American statement inside a French city. Samuel Hermann, born in Rödelheim, Germany in 1777, immigrated to Louisiana in 1804, married Marie Emeranthe Becnel of St. John the Baptist Parish in 1806, and made his money in New Orleans as a broker and agent for planters and merchants, expanding into mortgages, stocks and real estate. In 1831 the Hermanns bought a lot on St. Louis Street and hired William Brand of Virginia to build their residence. Brand gave them a Federal, or Georgian, house — symmetry, a wide central hall and an ornate doorway in the manner of the rest of the United States — hybridized for its place with the balconies and galleries of French Louisiana. It was one of the first examples of American architectural influence in the French Quarter, and one of the largest properties in it: main house, a separate quarters building with the open-hearth kitchen and workrooms below and rooms for enslaved people above, a courtyard that originally ran back to Conti Street, and, later, a stable. [1, 3, 9, 10]

The splendor and the labor were the same household, and the museum now documents the second as deliberately as the first. The operator's research, built from notarial acts, wills and inventories, conveyances, census, tax, succession, emancipation, sacramental and death records, has identified roughly seventy people enslaved at the Hermann-Grima House between its construction in 1831 and the end of the Civil War. Notarial records for people bought and sold by the Hermanns begin in 1813, before the house existed; an inventory taken when the Hermann family declared bankruptcy in 1842 listed nine adults — six men and three women — and six children, all by name, and a later inventory at Mrs. Hermann's death shows many of the same people still held by the family. For the Grima years, notarial records run from 1829 to 1857; in 1840, before the Grimas bought this house, their household at Toulouse and Bourbon held seventeen enslaved persons and one free woman of color, and the 1860 census lists six enslaved persons and seven free people described in the census language of the time living at the St. Louis Street house. In March 2021 the museum began a concerted project to assemble all of this into a public Database of Enslaved Individuals, following the model of Gwendolyn Midlo Hall's Afro-Louisiana database, and in October 2021 it launched its Urban Enslavement Tour on that research — a tour outside reviewers have singled out among the city's best. The names are recoverable because the paperwork of buying, selling, inheriting and counting people was thorough; the museum prints that plainly, and so does this dossier. The parlors were furnished by the work done across the courtyard, in the kitchen, the ironing room, the washroom and the wine room, by people who slept above their work. [5, 6, 11]

The Hermanns did not keep the house long. The crash of the English cotton market in 1837 set off a worldwide panic; Hermann's fortune failed, the bankruptcy inventory followed in 1842, and the house passed to Felix Grima — born in New Orleans in 1798, a judge and scholar, who moved in with his wife, his widowed mother and his unmarried sister, and whose family then held the house for generations. The Grimas added the stable and carriage house in the 1870s — the operator identifies it as the only remaining original, intact stable in the French Quarter, with its brick floors, wrought-iron hay racks and cypress stalls surviving. Felix Grima's own book collection and family portraits by Jacques Amans, alongside Hermann portraits by Jean Joseph Vaudechamp, remain in the museum's collection, much of it donated by descendants. The last Grima resident sold the house in 1921. [3, 9, 10]

Its rescuer was a women's organization with a mission older than the museum idea. The Christian Woman's Exchange — chartered in 1881 as the first association organized and chartered in New Orleans by women for women, now The Woman's Exchange — acquired the property on January 3, 1924, from the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, which had acquired it from the Grima family. The Exchange ran it for decades as a boarding house for single women, with a tea room and consignment shop, its Exchange Shop mission — helping women support themselves by selling their work — continuing in the stable building today. Around 1963 the Exchange began to consider the house itself as its purpose; the open-hearth kitchen was restored first, in 1967; and in 1972 the organization formally changed its purpose to developing and maintaining the Hermann-Grima House by collecting, preserving and interpreting life in New Orleans from 1830 to 1860. The principal rooms, the 1831 kitchen and the courtyard parterre opened to the public in 1971 after extensive study of the architecture, customs and furnishings of the period; the house was listed in the National Register that year and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1974. The Woman's Exchange still owns and operates it, together with the Gallier House on Royal Street. [3, 4, 9]

What the visitor is shown now is the whole working property, not only the front rooms. The operator describes one of the best-preserved Federal houses in the Quarter, parlors, dining room and bedrooms furnished with period pieces and family heirlooms, the quarters building with its workrooms and a restored bedroom for enslaved residents on the second floor, the courtyard with its 1831 flagstones and parterre beds, and a functional open-hearth kitchen — described by the operator as one of the few functional open-hearth kitchens in the state and the only working historic kitchen in the city — with cooking demonstrations in season. Read the two descriptions of the demonstration schedule in the gaps below before planning around one. [1, 3]

**Lore, kept separate.** Commercial ghost-tour companies market the Hermann-Grima House as haunted, telling of a couple in nineteenth-century dress said to linger in the house. That telling is lore: it is sourced here only to the tour companies that sell it, it is not part of the museum's documented record, and no documented event in the sources inspected supports it. The documented deaths, inventories and successions in the house's real record are in the story above and need no embellishment. [12]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** 820 St. Louis Street (818–820 in the National Register file), between Bourbon and Dauphine Streets in the French Quarter, New Orleans, Orleans Parish — a contributing property of the Vieux Carré Historic District as well as an individually listed landmark. [7, 8]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Hermann-Grima House, 820 St. Louis St., New Orleans, LA 70112, 504-274-0750 — guided-tour museum entry only, at the published tour times, through the Exchange Shop in the stable building. Stay with the tour group, as the operator requires; the courtyard and quarters are visited as part of the tour, not as open ground. [1, 2]

## 5. Public progression

Arrive for a timed tour — 10, 11, 1, 2 or 3, never Tuesday — and take the house in the order the operator has restored it to be read. Begin in the front rooms with the Hermann commission of 1831 and the Brand plan, then cross the courtyard deliberately: that crossing, from parlor to work yard, is the house's real floor plan. **Strongest story moment · The 1842 inventory.** Nine adults and six children, listed by name in a bankruptcy — the document that was meant to settle a merchant's debts is now, through the museum's database, one of the instruments that gives those fifteen people their names back in public. Spend the tour's time in the quarters building and the kitchen at least as attentively as in the parlors, and use the museum's free online Database of Enslaved Individuals before or after the visit to meet the household as individuals, not as a category. End at the Exchange Shop in the Grima stable, where the Woman's Exchange's 1881 mission — women selling their own work to support themselves — is still the business being done. [2, 3, 5, 6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **When did the Grimas buy the house — 1844 or 1850?** The operator's own pages disagree. Its Place page states the property "was purchased by Felix Grima in 1850," while the National Trust account and the Cultural Landscape Foundation both give Grima family ownership from 1844, family histories give the move-in as 1844, and the museum's enslaved-research page discusses the Grimas' 1840 household elsewhere in the Quarter, four years before "their purchase of the Hermann house." A six-year gap between a move-in/occupancy date and a recorded purchase date would explain the split, but no deed or notarial act reconciling the two dates was inspected in this pass. Both dates are printed; the story above uses the 1844 occupancy carried by the independent sources and flags the operator's 1850 here. [3, 9, 10]
- **Museum opening — 1971 or 1975?** The operator's Place page states the principal rooms, kitchen and parterre opened to the public in 1971, and its preservation history and the landscape record agree on a 1971 museum opening with National Register listing later that year. Secondary summaries, including the landmark's encyclopedia entry, state the house was restored and reopened as a museum in 1975 and that the Woman's Exchange ran the boarding house until 1975. The boarding-house end date and the museum opening may have overlapped by wing or by phase; no source inspected reconciles them. [3, 4, 9]
- **Cooking demonstration schedule.** The operator's house page offers cooking demonstrations "twice a month on Saturdays from November through April"; its Place page states demonstrations "on Thursdays between October and May." The two operator pages conflict on both day and season. Confirm the demonstration calendar directly with the museum before planning a visit around a firing of the hearth. [1, 3]
- **Sale out of the Hermann family.** Accounts compress the end differently: a fortune lost after the 1837 panic, a bankruptcy inventory in 1842, and a sale to Grima dated 1844 (or 1850, above). The sequence panic → bankruptcy → transfer is consistent across sources; the exact instrument and date of transfer were not inspected. [5, 6, 9]
- **The enslaved record is a record of paperwork.** The database's roughly seventy individuals are known from documents created by enslavers and institutions — notarial acts, inventories, censuses, sacramental and death records — which the museum itself describes as accurate but sometimes impersonal. Personal papers by the enslaved people of this house are not in the record inspected, a silence the sources attribute in part to laws forbidding enslaved people to read or write. Names and life details beyond the database entries remain open research, and the museum treats the database as a work in progress. [5, 11]
- **Boarding-house dates.** The Woman's Exchange acquisition is dated January 3, 1924 in its own history, from the SPCA; the chain SPCA → Exchange is consistent, but how long the SPCA itself held the property after the 1921 Grima sale is not detailed in the sources inspected. [4]
