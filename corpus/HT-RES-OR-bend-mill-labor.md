## 2. The story
**Named family/community voice: Sue Fountain.** Rebekah Averette's profile in the historical society's **July 2022 Homesteader, Volume 48, No. 7, page 4**, reports Fountain's memories as a child in a Brooks-Scanlon millwright's family. Her mother also worked in local businesses. The profile connects her recollection of neighborhood life with her wish that a changing Bend remember its mill-town roots. It also records her later work creating a small Brooks-Scanlon exhibit. [4]

This is **reported community testimony in a named institutional profile**, not a verbatim oral-history transcript. No invented direct quotation is supplied. Fountain's memoir *Too Cold to Snow* is identified by that profile, but **the memoir itself was not read**. The source satisfies the named family-memory lead while leaving a direct first-person passage and her father's employment record unresolved.

**Original interpretive voice:** *A mill town was not only a place that made lumber. It was a place where children went to school, parents held jobs, and families recognized the sounds and rhythms of a working neighborhood. When the machinery disappears from everyday view, the memory does not automatically disappear with it. Someone has to decide to carry it into the town's next chapter.* This is our interpretation, not a historical quotation or a claim that every household shared one experience.

The family angle is deliberately narrower than a complete lumber-industry history. It asks how a worker's child remembers place and later helps preserve it. It does not manufacture wage figures, union affiliations, injury history, company housing arrangements or a father’s name missing from the inspected passage.

A separate archival photograph shows a mill landscape, not a complete social story. Oregon Historical Society identifies a Kiser Photo Co. view of a sawmill and logs on the Deschutes River, with an uncertain **1905–1925?** date range. It is not securely identified here as Fountain's father's workplace. [5]

This entry does not use a settler family profile to narrate Indigenous history. Broader land and cultural histories require relevant Tribal-published sources before expansion. A focused labor story is not a claim that Bend's history began with mills.

## 3. Then / now

- **A school becomes a museum:** the lawful visitor anchor is a civic-history institution. **Now:** it is not sold as a surviving mill floor or an immersive industrial interior. [2]
- **Past exhibit work versus present availability:** a dated profile documents a display project. **Now:** that does not establish the display's current installation or access. [4]

The entry is independent of any commercial Old Mill District visit. It neither assumes that redevelopment erased every trace nor promises that historical industrial remains can be entered.

6. **End at the school-turned-museum:** the strongest story moment is the movement from living in a working town to preserving its memory. Ask what can survive in a community even when its dominant workplace changes.
