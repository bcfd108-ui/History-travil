# Boise · The Cyrus Jacobs/Uberuaga Boardinghouse

History Travel · Idaho · Draft for Dash QC · Desk review 18 November 2026

## 2. The story

Florencio “Pancho” Aldape arrived in Boise in 1935. “I assumed the Capitol building was the church,” he recalled. At fourteen he was met by José Uberuaga, who put him in a taxi to the sheep camp where his father worked. Aldape later stayed at the Uberuaga boardinghouse while he attended Central School. Remember the schoolboy as well as the sheepherder. The same house could put a person on the road to work and give a child a place to come back to for school. [3]

The long front of 607 Grove belonged to another Boise first. Cyrus Jacobs had packed supplies to the Boise Basin. His mercantile grew out of a tent. He and Mary Ellen brought up their children here in a brick house that the 1972 nomination dates to about 1864. In 1878 an addition behind the street-facing rooms made a T of the building. The new part held a dining room, kitchen and two bedrooms. By the time Basque families rented it as a boardinghouse, the plan already joined the work of feeding people to the work of housing them. [2, 4]

The Galdos, Bicandi and Uberuaga families rented the house in succession. Boarding began in 1910. José and Hermenegilda Bernedo Uberuaga took the lease in 1917 and bought the property for $2,000 in 1928. José had worked on railroad construction and on Arrowrock Dam. At home the family provided meals, rooms and a place where boarders could speak Euskera. When José died in 1954, Hermenegilda kept the house in business until her death in 1969. In his 1972 nomination Arthur Hart named their son Joe P. Uberuaga as the owner. Decades later, Joseph **Manuel** Uberuaga described card games and the downstairs rooms for the museum’s oral-history project. The two Joes have distinct records. [2, 4, 5]

## 3. Then / now

A merchant’s family rooms became a rented boardinghouse, then a family-owned business and finally a restored house on the Basque Block. The 1972 form recorded a private residence. The museum now admits visitors on a limited schedule. The porch is a good place to read across these changes because the nomination records the front range, rear addition and connecting verandah separately. [1, 2, 4]

## 4. What’s still there

- **View from public ground** — the Grove Street front and roofed porch at 607. In the 1972 image, the gabled roof, chimney and lattice porch supports give the building a distinctive outline. [4, 6]
- **Public interior, conditional** — restored boardinghouse rooms during staffed admission. The modern museum entrance is at 611. The virtual tour supplies an interior view when the house door is shut. [1, 7]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Start at the museum desk at 611 and establish whether the house is staffed. On a closed day, start on the public Grove Street frontage with the virtual tour. [1, 7]
2. Look at 607 from across the street and open the 1972 photograph. Find the street-parallel roofline, then the turned corner of the porch. These belong to the same building whose ownership the nomination recorded. [4, 6]
3. **Strongest story moment** — in the downstairs rooms if admitted, or facing the house from the public walk, play the museum’s Joseph Manuel Uberuaga clip about playing cards. Then read Aldape’s account of living here to attend school. Each describes something a rent ledger would miss about a boardinghouse. [3, 5]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The museum’s Basque Block history gives 1864 for construction, while Hart wrote “about 1864.” Use circa 1864. The museum dates the boarding operation, the family lease and the purchase separately. A grant summary compresses the acquisition into the early 1900s. The operator’s chronology is the more specific account. The oral-history page has circulated with a daily-tour footer. Use the dedicated admissions page for current access. The clip titles and interview dates were read, the sound recordings were not transcribed here. [1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9]

## 7. Photo record

**Idaho State Historical Society, 1972.** The photograph and its reverse are in the National Register file. The reverse specifies a southwest view of the northeast front. **Photo Point — APPROXIMATE.** From the public Grove Street side northeast of the house, face southwest. Align the projecting porch corner with the long roof and the chimney behind it. High confidence in the side and bearing from the photographer’s label, moderate in the standing area, low in exact distance. The 1972 fence and trees are unreliable alignment points. [4, 6]

The museum’s house exhibit also locates collection items 2004.028.033, 2004.028.001 and 2012.008.001. Its surrounding text does not date each exposure or give a camera direction. No additional Photo Point assigned. [2]
