## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** the students were not asking to escape education. They wanted an education that did not decide their future before they had a chance to choose it.

**Paula Crisostomo**, a Lincoln High School participant, gave a published interview in **Pasadena on February 23, 2018**. She recalled being the eldest daughter among eight children, with household and sibling-care responsibilities, and learning from a mother active in community affairs. Encouragement from teacher **Sal Castro** and college students widened her sense of possibility. Her plain statement of the purpose was **“We were asking for better schools, a better education.”** [2, PDF pp.1–3]

This account was published through **Deborah Aschheim's Pasadena Time Travel** project, which collected participant interviews in 2017–18 and paired excerpts with drawings for a 2018 public-art project. [3] The relevant transcript was read, not an original sound recording. It is retrospective firsthand testimony, not a contemporaneous verbatim record of every meeting.

**Carlos Manuel Haro**, then a third-year UCLA student and Roosevelt High alumnus, later described the walkouts' significance in terms of students' self-worth and their resolve to write their own communities' histories. UCLA published his reflections in **March 2018**. [4] His perspective is both that of a participant generation and a later university researcher; it is not an independent measurement of every reform's effects.

**Original interpretation:** the strongest human thread is the movement from being judged by institutions to becoming an interpreter of one's own experience. The story is bigger than one teacher or one famous day, and smaller, more personal, than an undifferentiated crowd count.

## 3. Then / now

The Library of Congress research guide situates the walkouts in **March 1–8, 1968**, describing demands over curriculum, bilingual education and Mexican-American representation in school administration. [5] This is a later institutional synthesis, useful for orientation but not the students' original written demands.

The present museum advertises **LA Starts Here!** as its **first-floor permanent exhibition**, using personal narratives, photographs, objects and testimony to interpret Indigenous, Mexican and Mexican-American histories in Los Angeles. [6] That broad published scope supports the cultural anchor. It does **not** independently verify a current case, video or artifact specifically about Crisostomo or the 1968 walkouts.

LA Plaza also retains an **online exhibitions** page containing **¡Ya Basta! The East LA Walkouts and the Power of Protest**, including a news segment dated **September 30, 2018**. The page presents exhibitions previously shown. [7] Its continued existence online is not proof that the physical walkouts exhibition is installed now. This dossier therefore offers a thematic museum visit with remote testimony, not a guaranteed dedicated walkouts exhibition.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Published totals differ: the LOC guide gives roughly **15,000** participants, UCLA's 2018 account says nearly **20,000**, and the Los Angeles Conservancy says approximately **22,000**. [5][4][9] Definitions and counting methods were not reconciled. Use “thousands” in the visitor narrative; do not turn any estimate into an exact attendance fact.

Other chronology also needs care: LOC places a community board meeting on **March 28**, while the Conservancy describes a special board response meeting on **March 26**. [5][9] Original minutes were not retrieved, so this draft does not resolve those dates or construct a detailed day-by-day timeline from them.

No original student survey, complete contemporaneous demands document or district implementation audit was examined. No claim is made that every requested reform was achieved, or that one event alone produced later college-enrollment changes.

The museum's January 2027 holiday gate, exact accessible arrival arrangement, walkouts-specific installation, visitor-photography rules and individual research access remain gaps. Historic 2018 public-art installations and anniversary events must not be advertised as current attractions. [3][4]
