# 100 Men Hall and the work behind the music

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP24 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
A group of Black residents in Bay St. Louis organized mutual help before the standing hall existed. The older nomination names J. L. Collins, J. M. Larendo, Noel Perry and Robert Smith and dates the organization to 1888. The hall’s current operator and a 2024 Mississippi Senate resolution give 1894 and describe twelve men. The operator transcribes a charter purpose, “to assist its members when sick and bury its dead in a respectable manner”. It also reports that entertainments could replenish the treasury. An organization’s first meeting, charter and later recollection can leave different dates. The original incorporated document remains a gap. [3, 4, 7]

The building dates to 1922 in both accounts. Money also came from people seeking a room to dance or perform. The association’s rentals helped build audiences for musicians in a segregated world. The hall’s history names local performers including Harry Fairconnetue and later traveling acts, and the Senate resolution recognizes the hall as a stop on the Black performance circuit. The through line is an association providing care and a building where neighbors could support it. [3, 4, 7]

Lylie Nichols remembered the people who made a performance night work. In the Hall People Project she recalled, “My mom became the cook from them.” She had described her grandmother and Aunt Mercedes in aprons at the kitchen. As a child she saw a room of adults taking part in something she could watch from the door. Brian Keith Labat’s account remembers Joe Labat as a bandleader who hired musicians out of New Orleans. These are individually published recollections. The photographs made for those accounts are contemporary portraiture and are not used here. [5, 6]

The hall and organization changed hands. The operator records a 1982 transfer to Disabled American Veterans, a rescue after Katrina by Jesse and Kerrie Loya, and acquisition by Rachel Dangermond and Constantin “Tin” Dangermond in 2018. The current nonprofit paired new public programming with a project to publish local memories. The hall became a place where former participants could describe what it had meant to them. [3–6]

## 3. Then and now
The standing Union Street association building continues to house performances and gatherings. The documented transfer and restoration history helps distinguish the 1922 building from later work. The operator’s modern public sessions govern interior access. A club, rehearsal or private rental can be using the same rooms without granting public entry. [1–4, 7]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The older formal documentation gives 1888 for organization. The operator and 2024 legislative resolution give 1894. Both support construction of the standing hall in 1922. The reproduced charter wording is attributed to the operator’s publication, while original minutes, filing date and signatories remain unverified. [3, 4, 7]
- The operator’s visit page gives the location and links into its schedule. The public openings FAQ gives regular Thursday and event hours. Use the dated listing to avoid treating the hall as a daily museum. [1, 2]
- Most cited bookings rest on operator and legislative history. Exact performance dates, original tickets and the amount of original interior fabric after restoration remain open. [3, 4, 7]
