## 2. The story

A visitor first encounters substantial masonry. The harder story is that these walls were not merely scenery: they organized confinement and labor. The nomination describes local sandstone, prison labor, a fortified enclosure, separate women's accommodation and different forms of punishment space. Its architectural account is also a record of how a state made its authority physical. [2]

Construction began in 1870; incarceration here ran from 1872 to December 1973. [1] The National Register recognizes a district, not a single picturesque building, and identifies architecture and social history as its significance. It was listed July 17, 1974. [3]

**Short recorded voice, written rather than audio:** Alexander Nephi Stephens recorded on January 17, 1888 that punished prisoners had “no Bed to sleep on Nothing but stone flore”. The state interpretive plan reproduces that wording on printed page 40. Spelling retained; excerpt only. This is a diary account of others' treatment, not an independently corroborated finding about every punishment cell. [4]

For a later first-person voice, the operator publishes Melvin Aldous's recollections of incarceration in the 1960s, recorded March 31, 2023. Its description identifies work in the dining hall and chapel. The recording was located, not listened to or transcribed in this research: do not manufacture quotations or a timestamp. [5]

Interpretive recommendation: hold together the suffering of incarcerated people, the harm experienced by crime victims, the lives of staff and families, and the difference between a conviction record and a complete human biography. Neither a heroic escape story nor a notorious-criminal roster is sufficient.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** an operating prison whose form changed with penal practice. The 1973 nomination records fire damage to the original prison/chapel and dining-room roofs; its text anticipates closure and future interpretation. That is near-contemporary evidence, not a current condition survey. [2]

**Now:** a state historic site visited by choice. That difference is the essential comparison: today's visitor can leave. The state’s interpretive master plan is a proposed approach, not proof that every proposed exhibit has been installed. [4] The draft should not flatten a century of incarceration into one supposedly unchanged prison experience.

## 4. What's still there

- **Ticketed visitor route:** prison yard and open exhibits, subject to operator directions. Admission does not authorize every doorway. [1]
- **Historic fabric, current room access conditional:** sandstone enclosure, administration entrance, cell-house fabric and fire-damaged structures documented in the nomination. Use present signs/staff to determine which can be entered; this dossier does not certify their current condition. [2]
- **Restricted / not a hunt destination:** underground dungeon. The interpretive plan identifies its cells as inaccessible. Never enter a barred-off area to obtain a photograph or answer. [4]
- **Separate research access:** Idaho State Archives, 2205 Old Penitentiary Road, maintains historical records and images. Archive access is not included by implication in prison admission. [6]
- **Sensitive spaces:** execution and punishment interpretation should be optional for the visitor. Do not require distressing rooms as a condition of completing a game.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

This is a proposed sequence, not a surveyed accessible route:

1. At arrival, obtain the current visitor map and ask which spaces are closed. Select the stair-free alternative if needed.
2. Read the entrance as architecture: why make a prison look monumental? Compare the exterior impression with what the first interior interpretation explains.
3. Use one open cell-house exhibit to examine how an ordinary day was organized. Seek evidence about routine, work and relationships rather than collecting lurid facts.
4. Compare a labor/history exhibit with the stonework. Ask who made the place and under what constraints.
5. End at an authorized, quiet point on the visitor route, without obstructing others. Read the Stephens excerpt, then turn back toward the exit. **Strongest story moment: you can walk through that gate when you choose; the people described could not.** This is reflection, not simulated imprisonment. No reenactment, locked-door stunt or gallows pose.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The nomination's historical address is 2200 Warm Springs Avenue; use the operator's current visitor entrance, not that archival address. [1][3]
- The nomination's language of continuous use from 1870 differs from the operator's distinction between construction in 1870 and prisoners arriving in 1872. Preserve the distinction rather than treating construction as incarceration. [1][2]
- The diary quote was checked in the plan's extracted text, not against the original manuscript. PDF screenshot retrieval failed. Original handwriting and archival shelfmark remain QC work. [4]
- Aldous audio was not accessed successfully; the published description is not a transcript. [5]
- No same-day closure check by telephone, field visit, current room-by-room access survey, exact visitor-camera station or complete contemporary photo match.
- No claims that ghosts, sensational anecdotes or museum planning proposals establish historical facts.
