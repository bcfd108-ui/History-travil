## 2. The story
The City identifies **Gilman Blood** as a farmer and freighter. He arrived in 1871, filed to purchase Osage Trust Land, began farming in 1872 and hauled freight into what the City account calls Indian Territory while his apple trees matured. He brought back buffalo meat and hides for sale before turning to orchard work full-time. This is a named worker from a later museum synthesis, not his own testimony. [2]

A contemporary counterpoint: the City transcribes a *Wichita City Eagle* item dated May 17, 1882, boasting that a morning train from Wichita could reach St. Louis the next morning. Read it as newspaper promotion, not independently verified timing or a statement that wagons disappeared. The museum's saddle-and-harness interpretation explicitly describes animal-drawn service continuing after rail arrived. [2]

**Interpretive point:** Rail transformed Wichita's markets, but Blood's household still needed a team, a wagon and an income between planting and harvest. The two systems overlapped.

**Strongest story moment:** Freight work paid while the apple trees grew. The building visitors can see is a surviving household artifact, moved from its original ground. [2]

## 3. Then / now
- **Blood House:** Original 1871 hall-and-parlor house; moved to the museum in 2001. The City locates the farm historically near today's 63rd Street South and Broadway; that is context only, not a stop.
- **Grain transfer:** The museum's elevator was built in 1912 at Bentley and moved here; its accompanying scale house was built in 1987. Neither is an original 1870s Wichita freight structure.
- **Depot:** Museum's 1887 depot came from Anness and carries the Wichita & Southwestern name for interpretation. It is not the original Wichita depot. [2]
