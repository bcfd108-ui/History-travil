## 2. Story
**Cumberland Gap National Historical Park, Kentucky visitor center · Research draft · Fred**

The routes at the Gap long preceded the roads marked by land companies. In April 1750 Thomas Walker's party was looking for ways to move people, horses and baggage through the ridges. In the free University of Nebraska–Lincoln transcription, the journal records “a plain Indian Road” near the pass. He also saw marks on trees and noted a steep, rocky north slope. His record shows a man recognizing evidence of existing travel even while recording the country for a Virginia land enterprise. NPS distinguishes this earlier use from Richard Henderson's 1775 commission to Daniel Boone to mark a route for colonial settlement. That expansion changed whose claims the road was designed to serve. The visitor center is the place to read those accounts without visiting Indigenous or battle ground. [1, 3–4]

The same opening later had a federal paved road and heavy highway traffic. In the park's management plan, the Bureau of Public Roads built an Object Lesson Road in 1908. Engineers ultimately put through the Cumberland Gap highway tunnel, which opened October 18, 1996. The NPS and highway agencies could then remove traffic from the old roadbed and work on the pass's setting. The park's 2011 account gives Superintendent Mark Woods's explanation of the two aims: historic rehabilitation and traffic safety. The park archival description separates completion of the tunnel from the subsequent rehabilitation. [5–7]

## 3. Then and now
Firsthand route evidence comes from Walker's travel journal. Modern interpretation happens at a visitor center in Kentucky. The Gap itself lies in a multi-state park, so an account of the whole federal unit cannot be used as Kentucky-only visitation or a locator for one exhibit. Tunnel-era restoration created a modern historic landscape with portions interpreted as the Wilderness Road. [1, 3, 5–7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
- The Walker text is a university transcription. The marks he described supply evidence of travel, and the entry does not assign those marks to one named individual or Tribe. Recollections and documentation about whose route was used at particular points require separate records. [3–4]
- The plan compresses corridor work into language beginning “By 1996.” The tunnel's dated opening and the archive's later rehabilitation sequence establish two different events. The date of individual surface works remains open. [5–7]
- The federal unit's region-wide visitor counts do not identify visitors to the Kentucky exhibit, and no public-anchor coordinate pair was verified from two independent sources.
