# HT-RES-VT-hildene — The Ancestral Home Above the Battenkill

Division VT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Town: Manchester, Vermont

On a forested promontory above the Battenkill valley in Manchester, between the Taconic Mountains to the west and the Green Mountains to the east, stands the summer estate that Robert Todd Lincoln built for his family and called his ancestral home: a 412-acre property of mansion, formal garden, observatory, carriage barn, and working farm ground in the valley below. Robert was the eldest of Abraham and Mary Todd Lincoln's four sons and the only one to survive to maturity; the house he built here in 1905 stayed in Lincoln family hands until 1975 and is now preserved as a museum by the nonprofit Friends of Hildene. The name is built from two old English words — "hil," a hill, and "dene," a valley with a stream — for the ground itself: the house on the hill, the Battenkill running through the valley at its feet.

## 1. Trip gate

Hildene is operated by The Friends of Hildene, Inc., a nonprofit, at 1005 Hildene Road, Manchester, VT 05254; its published telephone number is 802-362-1788. A general admission ticket covers self-guided tours of Robert and Mary's home, the gardens, Hildene Farm in the valley, the Pullman railcar Sunbeam, and some twelve miles of walking trails.

The operator's own pages frame the visit as year-round. From Memorial Day through October 31 the estate is open daily, 10:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m.; from November 1 through the last Monday in May it is open Thursday through Monday on the same hours and closed Tuesdays and Wednesdays. It closes for Easter Sunday, Thanksgiving, and the days around Christmas. A guided house tour is offered as an add-on, daily at 11:00 a.m. and 1:00 p.m. and lasting about forty-five minutes, except that no guided tours run from September 15 through October 31; self-guided touring runs in every season, with docents posted at the sites. Admission is charged, at the prices the operator publishes; current prices and program schedules are as published at hildene.org.

The visitor boundary is the estate itself: mansion promontory and dene farm under one ticket, reached from Hildene Road; the village of Manchester around it is a living town, not part of the visit.

## 2. The story

Robert Todd Lincoln was born in Springfield, Illinois, in 1843, the first of Abraham and Mary Todd Lincoln's four sons. His brothers — Edward, William, and Thomas — all died young; Robert alone reached adulthood. Educated at Phillips Exeter Academy and Harvard College, he served at the end of the Civil War on General Ulysses S. Grant's staff, in the final Appomattox campaign, then built a law practice in Chicago. Public office followed: Secretary of War from 1881 to 1885, under Presidents Garfield and Arthur — the only Garfield cabinet member Arthur kept on — and then Minister to Great Britain under President Harrison. He had married Mary Harlan in 1868; their daughter Jessie, born in 1875, is the child whose work still shapes the estate's grounds.

Manchester entered his life during the war. Hildene's own history records that in the summer of 1864 the young Robert came to the Equinox Hotel in Manchester to meet his mother and his brother Tad, who had come up from Washington. He did not build here for another forty years. In between came the Pullman Company: Lincoln served it as counsel, and on the death of its founder, George Pullman, in 1897 he became its president — Hildene's own account dates the presidency 1897 to 1911, followed by the board chairmanship until 1922 and a directorship until his death. In those years the Pullman Company was, in Hildene's published description, the largest manufacturing corporation in the country.

At the turn of the century the Lincolns came back to Manchester — first to the Equinox House and rented village cottages, the Register documentation notes, drawn by the mountains, the quiet, the railroad, and the golf at the Ekwanok Country Club — and decided to build permanently. Robert purchased 392 acres to build what he would call his ancestral home. The Boston firm of Shepley, Rutan and Coolidge designed the house; the Boston builder Ira G. Hersey constructed it, with the carriage barn and outbuildings. The Register nomination dates the main dwelling to 1904; Hildene dates the family's move into the finished house to June 20, 1905. The twenty-four-room Georgian Revival mansion came equipped as a modern house: electricity, telephone service, hot-air heating, and an enunciator system by which the Lincolns could call their staff anywhere in the house. From 1905 to 1926, the Register documentation records, Lincoln spent from May through November at Hildene, conducting Pullman and personal business from it by telephone and telegraph.

The house grew once, significantly. In 1908 Lincoln extended the first floor westward by eighteen feet, filling in a porch to make an office for his secretaries, outfitted with a built-in filing system where he kept his business papers, his correspondence, and his papers concerning his father. The same year brought the Aeolian pipe organ, called for in the original designs, its console in the entry hall and its thousand pipes ranged on the staircase landing. Outside, the grounds took shape under Frederick Todd, an apprentice of Frederick Law Olmsted, who laid out the cutting and kitchen gardens, planted some three hundred varieties of trees and shrubs, and landscaped the reflecting pond where the grandchildren later sailed pond boats.

The formal garden was the daughter's gift. Jessie Lincoln designed it — the Register documentation dates the design to 1907 and calls it a present to her parents; Hildene's estate page dates it 1908 — as a parterre in the pattern of a stained-glass cathedral window, meant to be read from her mother's second-floor sitting room: privet hedges for the leading, beds of many-colored flowers for the glass. Its peonies were on their way before the garden was finished: Hildene's garden page prints Robert's directive of November 9, 1905, about a box addressed to him at the Manchester Depot express office from Paris, France — "It contains peony roots." More than a hundred peony plants grow there now, some heirloom stock; two, the cultivars 'Hildene' and 'Jessie Lincoln,' were designated previously unidentified cultivars by the American Peony Society.

Robert was a serious amateur astronomer, and the estate carries the proof: an observatory built in 1908 on the cliff northeast of the mansion, a small brick cylinder under a rotating dome. The farm below was no ornament either: the valley land was a working dairy farm when the family acquired it, its farmhouses and barns along River Road dating from the early nineteenth century. In 1909 the town voted to sell Lincoln the District No. 10 schoolhouse — one room, built in 1832, closed since 1892 — for fifty dollars; the Lincolns used it chiefly for storage.

Robert Todd Lincoln died in 1926, still a Pullman director. The estate descended through Jessie to her children. Her daughter Mary Lincoln Beckwith — Peggy — was the last Lincoln descendant to live at Hildene; she brought agriculture back to the dene, and a studio was built for her beside the mansion in the early 1930s. Peggy's will left Hildene to the First Church of Christ, Scientist, as a memorial to the Lincoln family; when she died in 1975 the Church took it, and exercised the right her will gave it to sell. The neighbors answered: community members formed The Friends of Hildene and, in 1978, after three years of negotiation, bought the property, restored the house and the formal garden, and opened Hildene to the public. The estate had entered the National Register the year before, on October 28, 1977. The direct line ended soon after: Peggy's brother, Robert Todd Lincoln Beckwith, died on December 24, 1985 — the last direct descendant of Abraham Lincoln, as the Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library records it.

## 3. Then / now

Then, Hildene was a working summer seat: the family in residence from May into the fall, secretaries in the west office filing the Pullman Company's business and the president's father's papers, staff summoned by the enunciator system, a dairy farm working the valley floor, and the master of the house walking out at night to his own observatory. It was a private place, and its pleasures were working ones: golf, gardens, a telescope, a farm.

Now, the same ground is a museum campus, and the change of use is the thing to read honestly. The house a visitor walks through is the family's own, furnished with Lincoln and Harlan family furniture and looking, in the operator's phrase, much as it did in Robert and Mary's day — Robert's study kept as it was in his lifetime. But the carriage barn where the visit begins is now the Welcome Center and Museum Store; the farm in the dene works again as a regenerative farm with livestock, a goat dairy, and school programs; and the estate's newest old thing, the Pullman car Sunbeam, is not a Lincoln survival at all but a 1903 car the museum searched the country for and restored, because Robert's company built its kind. The exclusiveness is gone; the work is still there, done now as education.

## 4. What's still there

The mansion stands at the high point of the ridge at the end of a mile-long tree-lined driveway, on a 412-acre property that held, at nomination, twenty-two structures and amenities. The house is a two-and-a-half-story center section under a hipped roof flanked by two-story hipped wings, built on brick foundations faced with marble and walled in a light yellow-brown stucco. The north front is bisected by the projecting entrance and its one-story porte-cochère, the main opening framed by a full entablature on fluted Ionic columns. Inside, the first floor keeps its formal entry foyer and the multi-spindled staircase rising to a balcony, the organ pipes encased at the balcony level; the library with its fireplace built into a mahogany panel wall; the dining room; Robert's study and the 1908 secretaries' office with its filing system; and a kitchen where a large wood- and coal-burning stove remains. The family bedrooms above are restored with the original furniture returned.

The formal garden lies off the south, rear elevation — about 150 feet by 70 feet in the Register documentation's measure — its privet hedges clipped back to the design after the Friends found them grown twice their planted height, restored in the 1980s from Jessie's original plans and purchase lists. Behind the carriage barn, the cutting and kitchen gardens of 1908 were restored in 1990. The observatory stands northeast of the mansion on its cliff over the valley, restored in the 1980s, its rotted canvas dome restored in 1981; the Register documentation records a telescope restoration in 2002 and notes the instrument's mechanical clock drive, one of few in the country. The carriage barn of 1905, two hundred yards southwest of the house, now holds the Welcome Center and the Museum Store.

Down in the dene, the farm buildings along River Road stand as the nomination found them, farmhouses and barns of the early nineteenth century with elements the documentation traces to the eighteenth. The 1832 schoolhouse — the Hollow School — is still on its original foundation and still used as a schoolhouse: area children attend programs in it, taking the names of actual students of 1862. The working farm around it runs today as Hildene Farm: Randall cattle, a rare Vermont heritage breed; fiber animals and heritage poultry; a Nubian goat dairy whose cheese is made on the property; an educational greenhouse built in 2015; and the wetlands along the Battenkill, crossed by a six-hundred-foot floating boardwalk. The Pullman car Sunbeam, a seventy-two-foot wooden private car of 1903, stands restored as an exhibit, carrying the museum's Many Voices exhibition on the Pullman porters. Some twelve miles of trails tie the promontory to the valley.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Begin where the museum begins: the carriage barn, now the Welcome Center. Then take the approach as it was designed — up the mile-long tree-lined drive to the promontory.

Take the house whole. If a guided tour is running (11:00 a.m. or 1:00 p.m., outside the mid-September-to-October gap), it starts outside the house and works through the center hall; on a self-guided pass, give the entry hall its time anyway — the staircase, the organ pipes in their cabinets at the balcony. Then the library and Robert's study, and the 1908 office beside it, where the filing system held the father's papers: the room where the estate's double life, summer house and Pullman headquarters, is easiest to read. Finish with the kitchen and its stove, and the restored family bedrooms above.

Come out the south side into the formal garden and read it as Jessie designed it to be read — as a window, leading and glass — keeping in mind that its true viewpoint is the second-floor sitting room above you. Take the cutting and kitchen gardens behind the carriage barn on the way down, and the short path northeast to the observatory on its cliff.

Give the Sunbeam its own stop: a 1903 wooden Pullman private car, restored after a nationwide search for the least-modified survivor of Robert's presidency, and with it the Many Voices exhibit on the porters who staffed such cars. Then descend to the dene for the farm — the goat dairy, the pastures, the 1832 schoolhouse — and close on the floating boardwalk across the wetlands.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The mansion's building date is stated two ways by the two primary authorities this dossier rests on. The 1977 National Register nomination says the main dwelling was built in 1904; the Register's own additional documentation heads its description "Main House (Building #1) 1905," and Hildene's pages call the house the 1905 summer home and date the family's move-in to June 20, 1905. Printed here as built 1904–1905, occupied from June 1905.

The land purchase is likewise not one clean date. Hildene's history page says Robert returned some forty years after 1864 and purchased 392 acres; its farm page dates a purchase of the dene dairy farm to 1902. The assembled estate is consistently given as 412 acres, the nomination's figure as well. No single purchase year is asserted here.

Robert's first Manchester visit is described inconsistently. Hildene's own history says the summer of 1864, when he came to the Equinox Hotel to meet his mother and his brother Tad. The Register's additional documentation says he "first visited Manchester with his mother and father during Civil War" — a version that sits poorly with the operator's own account. Section 2 follows Hildene's account and prints the conflict rather than resolving it.

The Pullman presidency is dated from two different years. Hildene's Sunbeam page states that on George Pullman's death in 1897 Robert became president, held the post until 1911, then chaired the board until 1922. The Register's additional documentation makes him the company's special counsel from 1894 to 1897 and "President of the Pullman Palace Car Company from 1901–1911." The start year is printed as the two sources give it, unresolved.

The formal garden carries two dates: the Register documentation dates Jessie's design to 1907; Hildene's estate page says 1908. Hildene's garden page adds that Robert's peony-roots letter is dated November 9, 1905 — the plantings were in motion before either date. Both dates print where the garden is described.

The observatory's telescope has two incompatible records. The 1977 nomination states that the telescope was removed in the late 1920s and donated to the Burr and Burton Seminary in Manchester. The Register's additional documentation, describing the observatory's restoration, records that "in 2002, Hildene restored the telescope" and describes its mechanical clock drive. Whether the present instrument is the returned original or a replacement is stated in neither document, and is not asserted here.

The small building east of the mansion is dated two ways: the 1977 nomination describes a studio built for Mrs. Beckwith in the early 1930s; the additional documentation lists the Annex as circa 1908, on photographic evidence. Section 4 does not date the building.

The Register's location data does not match the operator's address: the Park Service's asset record carries the property at "820 Hildene Rd.," while the operator publishes, and §10 prints, 1005 Hildene Road. And the Register's additional documentation gives the property's geography as UTM boundary references in Zone 18 — five corner points for a 412-acre parcel — which fix the estate's bounds but no point for the house. No latitude or longitude is therefore printed in §10; aggregator and map-site points were not used.
