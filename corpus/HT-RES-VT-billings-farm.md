# HT-RES-VT-billings-farm — The Farm That Planted Its Hills Back

Division VT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Town: Woodstock, Vermont

On the north bank of the Ottauquechee River, a short walk from Woodstock's village green, stands a dairy farm that has worked continuously since 1871 and now works in public: Billings Farm & Museum, a working Jersey dairy and a museum of Vermont rural life on the same ground. Frederick Billings, the railroad lawyer who bought the old Marsh farm in 1869, built it as a demonstration — a scientifically managed dairy on the lowland and, on the stripped slopes of Mount Tom across the road, one of the first programs of scientific forest management in the United States. The farm passed through his widow and daughters to his granddaughter Mary French Rockefeller, and in 1983 the Woodstock Foundation opened it to the public without stopping the milking. Across Route 12, the mansion and reforested mountain the Rockefellers gave to the nation in 1992 form Marsh-Billings-Rockefeller National Historical Park. It is conservation ground on both sides of the road: the farm shows the working half of the idea, the park the woodland half.

## 1. Trip gate

Billings Farm & Museum is owned and operated by The Woodstock Foundation, Inc., a nonprofit founded by Laurance and Mary Rockefeller in 1968, at 69 Old River Road, Woodstock, VT 05091, just north of the village on Vermont Route 12; its published telephone number is (802) 457-2355. It is a ticketed site combining a working dairy farm — barns, pastures, gardens, and a living herd — with museum exhibitions and a restored historic farmhouse, all on one walkable farmstead.

The farm's published seasons run daily, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., from late May through October 31, and on weekends and select days, 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., from November through May; admission is charged, and current hours, prices, and the day's program schedule are as the farm publishes them at billingsfarm.org. Daily programs run through the season — milkings, animal encounters, garden and craft work — and the calendar carries seasonal events from Thanksgiving at the Farm to Woodstock's Wassail Weekend.

The visitor boundary is the farmstead itself. Marsh-Billings-Rockefeller National Historical Park stands directly across Route 12 and is a separate, federal site with its own tours and hours, though the two share a parking area at the farm. The village of Woodstock is a short walk south and is not part of either ticket.

## 2. The story

The farm's first famous name is not Billings. The land was the farm of the Marsh family of Woodstock, and the brick mansion across the road was built in 1805–07 for Charles Marsh, Sr. His son George Perkins Marsh, born in Woodstock in 1801, grew up roaming these woods and pastures, and in 1864, after a career as lawyer, Vermont fish commissioner, and diplomat in Turkey and Italy, he published *Man and Nature*, the book the National Park Service calls a founding text of the American conservation movement — a warning, drawn from landscapes he had seen deforested on three continents, that stripping forest cover brings eroded soil, silted rivers, and ruined farmland. By then Vermont had run the experiment on itself: the Park Service's forest history records that as much as 80 percent of the state's original forest had been cleared, much of it for sheep pasture, and that erosion and flooding on the bare hills were driving farm families out of the state.

Frederick Billings read the book and then bought the author's boyhood ground. Billings was born in Royalton, Vermont, in 1823 and moved to Woodstock in 1835; he made his fortune in San Francisco as a land-claims lawyer during the Gold Rush and then in railroads, rising to head the Northern Pacific Railroad and direct its transcontinental completion. He returned east having read *Man and Nature* on its publication in 1864, made Woodstock his home again in 1865, and in 1869 bought the farm that had once belonged to the Marsh family, renovated the Federal-style brick mansion, and began buying up additional land. What he found, in the Park Service's summary, was barren hills, silted rivers, and a devastated countryside; what he set out to build was a template — farming, timber harvest, and recreation carried on, in the Park Service's phrase, without ruining the land.

The demonstration had two halves. On the uplands he reversed the clearing: Billings planted trees by the thousands to reforest Mount Tom, using progressive forestry methods and hardy European species that would thrive in the Vermont climate — Norway spruce became his signature tree — and laid out a network of carriage roads and trails, more than twenty miles of them, with scenic vistas, so the public could ride through a managed forest and see it working. The Park Service now calls the result the oldest professionally managed forest in the United States. On the lowland along the river he built the other half: the Billings Farm, established in 1871 and run on modern scientific principles as a model other farmers could copy, around a scientifically managed herd of Jersey cows. At the railroad he had already practiced the same ethic, promoting the protection and reclamation of railroad lands through tree planting; in 1881 he left the Northern Pacific to devote himself full-time to his Woodstock projects, and he served as a leading member of Vermont's forestry commission as the state began its own reforestation work along the lines he and his manager had proven.

The farm's working character was set by the manager. In 1884 Billings hired George Aitken, a native Scot and accomplished farmer, who managed the Billings Farm from 1884 until his death in 1910 and served as forest manager as well. Aitken imported cows from the Isle of Jersey, along with hundreds of Southdown sheep, Berkshire hogs, and other livestock, and built the herd's genetics into the farm's reputation. By 1890, the year Frederick Billings died, the farm had grown to nearly 1,000 acres and was widely acknowledged for the superior genetics of its premier Jersey herd and for an extensive butter-making operation producing 5,000 pounds of high-quality butter annually. The capstone of the herd's early years came three years after Billings's death: at the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893, the Billings Farm took top honors in the dairy division, and its heifer Lily Garfield was designated "Champion Heifer of the World."

Billings built for permanence in the farmyard as well as the herd book. In 1890 he built the farm manager's house — the 1890 Farm Manager's House that stands at the center of the museum today — for Aitken and his family: living quarters, the manager's office, and a creamery built into the basement, a house designed to keep a first-rate manager on the farm for years. Farm managers lived in it, modernizing as they went, for nearly a century.

After Billings's death the estate stayed in the family's hands and under its women's management. His widow, Julia Parmly Billings, managed the property until her death in 1914, carrying out many of the improvements to the house and gardens; the 1893 Chicago triumph fell in her tenure. Their daughters — first Laura, and later Mary and Elizabeth — took up management after 1914. Julia's granddaughter Mary French married Laurance Spelman Rockefeller in 1934, and the couple inherited the farm and properties in the 1950s. Through the middle of the century the dairy, the one part of the original farm still in operation, was carried on as a commercial dairy from 1948, held through an entity owned by the Billings descendants; by the 1970s it was losing money. In 1974 Laurance Rockefeller proposed purchasing Billings Farm, Inc., and the family stockholders agreed. The Rockefellers rehabilitated the dairy — in the mid-1970s the farm resumed breeding championship-caliber Jerseys, and a string of regional show winnings through the 1970s and 1980s culminated when Billings Top Rosanne took top honors in both American and Canadian competition as the finest Jersey in North America.

The Rockefellers then split the estate into its two public halves. Laurance and Mary had founded The Woodstock Foundation, Inc., in 1968; in 1983 the Foundation opened the farm to the public as Billings Farm & Museum, a working dairy farm and museum of agricultural and rural life, with the 1890 Farm Manager's House at its center — its modern accretions were removed in 1987 so the house could be restored to its 1890 appearance. In 1992 the Rockefellers donated the mansion and the surrounding forest — some 550 acres of the hillside Billings had replanted — to the federal government, and Marsh-Billings National Historical Park was established on August 26, 1992, as Vermont's first national park; renamed Marsh-Billings-Rockefeller National Historical Park in 1998 and opened to the public that year, it remains the only national park unit dedicated to the history of conservation and the evolving practice of land stewardship. The two institutions work in partnership across the road from each other, and the pairing is the point: Marsh wrote the idea, Billings practiced it on a farm and a forest, and both halves of his ground are still being worked — one by farmers, one by foresters.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the farm was an argument. Vermont's hills stood bare from clearing, its sheep economy had collapsed, and its farm families were leaving; Billings's answer was a farm run as a published experiment — imported Jerseys, selective breeding, a creamery, a paid professional manager in a purpose-built house — set in a valley whose mountain he was visibly planting back into forest, carriage roads and all, so neighbors could ride up and look at the future he was selling.

Now, the farm is the same argument with the public let in. The dairy never stopped: the herd in the barns is a working Jersey herd whose milk goes to farmstead cheese, the fields are hayed, the draft horses plow and harrow, and the manager's house is furnished back to 1890 around the one piece that never left, George Aitken's stand-up desk. What changed is the audience — the farm is a nonprofit's educational institution now, its barns carrying exhibitions alongside the milking. Across the road, the mountain Billings planted is a national park's managed woodland, still harvested, still replanted; the two properties together read as one estate interrupted by a state highway.

## 4. What's still there

The working farmstead stands on the river flat where Billings built it: barns and a silo behind the 1890 Farm Manager's House, pastures, gardens, and the lane connecting barn to pasture. The farm the museum describes is a living herd — Jersey cows known for their lineage, Southdown sheep reflecting Vermont's sheep-farming century, Angora goats, heritage chickens in a mobile pasture coop, and draft horses that still work the fields and draw wagon and sleigh rides. The farmstead cheeses are made from the herd's own milk, and the farm's present practice runs to rotational grazing, no-till seeding, riparian tree planting, and pollinator-friendly fields.

The 1890 Farm Manager's House is the museum's centerpiece building: built by Frederick Billings in 1890 for George Aitken, lived in by farm managers until the museum opened in 1983, and restored in 1987 to its 1890 appearance, fully furnished, with the manager's office and the basement creamery showing how farm business, family life, and butter-making fitted into one house. Of its furnishings, the park's published guide notes, only Aitken's stand-up desk in the office is original to the house.

Across Route 12, the park holds the estate's other half: the Marsh-Billings mansion of 1805–07, boyhood home of George Perkins Marsh and later the Billings and Rockefeller home, a National Historic Landmark since 1967; and Mount Tom's 550 acres of managed woodland, carrying Billings's plantations of Norway spruce, Eastern white pine, Scotch pine, red pine, and European larch, his carriage roads and trails still the way in. The farm's grounds add an heirloom and dye garden complex, an orchard of more than fifty apple varieties on the hillside, and a one-mile walking path through the Great Meadow along the Ottauquechee River.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Start at the visitor center by the parking area on the farm side of Route 12 and take the working farm first, while the day's farm work is in front of you. Walk the barns and meet the herd: the Jersey cows are the farm's thesis, and the daily milking is the demonstration Billings designed the place to give. Watch for the draft horses at field work and the smaller stock — Southdown sheep, Angora goats, chickens — that fill out the nineteenth-century farm economy the museum teaches.

Then take the 1890 Farm Manager's House slowly: office first, with Aitken's own desk, then the family rooms, then the basement creamery, reading the house as the machine it was — the room where the herd book was kept, the room where the butter was made, the house a railroad fortune built to hold a good manager. The museum exhibitions in the barns carry Vermont farm life outward from this one farmstead.

Give the grounds their time: the gardens, the orchard hillside, and the river path through the Great Meadow, which reads the farm from its pasture side with the planted mountain in view. Then cross Route 12 to the park for the other half of the story — the mansion where Marsh grew up and Billings and the Rockefellers lived, and the carriage roads up through the plantations on Mount Tom. Finish back in the village, a short walk south.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The size of Billings's holdings is stated at two scales by the primary authorities, and the difference is one of scope, printed here unresolved. The farm's own published history describes the model farm as a 270-acre farm that had grown to nearly 1,000 acres by 1890. A National Register nomination for a neighboring Woodstock farm, describing the Billings estate as a whole, records Billings increasing his land holdings from 250 acres to almost 2,000 acres — a figure that sweeps in the Mount Tom forest land as well as the farm. Neither figure is asserted here for the other's subject: the farm's growth is given on the farm's own account, and the estate total on the nomination's.

Two dates anchor the farm and must not be merged: Billings bought the Marsh property in 1869, and the Billings Farm as an organized model operation is dated by both the farm and the Park Service to 1871. This dossier keeps both, in their places.

The herd's modern show record rests on the farm's own published history: the mid-1970s return to championship breeding and Billings Top Rosanne's honors are stated as the farm publishes them; no independent show record was fetched in this pass.

The 1890 Farm Manager's House is firmly dated by every authority consulted, but its architect is named in only one: the Society of Architectural Historians' Archipedia entry attributes the design to Frederick W. Stickney. No primary record fetched in this pass confirms the attribution, and it is therefore not asserted in the text above.
