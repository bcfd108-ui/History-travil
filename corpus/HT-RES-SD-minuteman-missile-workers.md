## 2. The story
**Named firsthand voice actually read:** Wendy McNiel's February 2003 oral-history excerpts, published by NPS [5]. Her career included Ellsworth service February 1991–December 1992. Her account calls the work an “invisible thankless job.” She explicitly widens recognition beyond officers to facility managers, security personnel and chefs. This is a retrospective worker's testimony, not a contemporaneous diary or a universal account of every employee. Only the social-history excerpt is used; technical discussion is excluded.

**Original interpretive voice:** A place remembered through large machines can make ordinary labor disappear. The useful human turn is away from equipment and toward the people whose work seldom became a public story. A meal, a long assignment, a profession chosen and a career remembered belong in history too. Listening does not require endorsing every policy that shaped the job. Neither admiration nor criticism should erase the worker.

The wider NPS oral-history roster includes Don Paulsen, whose cooperative-management background connects the story to civilian employment, and peace-movement participant John LaForge [6]. Their biographies establish additional voices exist, not what their unread full interviews prove. Do not turn biographical listings into invented quotations or balanced testimony that has not actually been heard.

### Who gets remembered

The park’s museum collection gives another view of ordinary work. It includes seating and items for maintaining living quarters, and it has grown through material preserved by civilian contractors and former staff. McNiel’s interview names people who might have disappeared behind the job titles. Read the published worker excerpt before the exhibits. Then consider which jobs leave an object when staff go home, and which depend on somebody telling the story afterward. [5,7,9]

The NPS exhibit material situates the history in a civilian landscape, and its interview catalog names communications co-op manager Don Paulsen alongside military employees and peace advocates. The catalog identifies his work and the subject of the interview. It supplies no words that can safely be quoted as his testimony. The desk visit ends with those missing voices in view, preserving the distinction between people who served on base and neighbors who lived and worked nearby. [6,7]

## 3. Then / now
Then: a woman recalled her early-1990s employment from a 2003 interview [5]. Now: NPS presents selected memories to a public audience. The interview date, period recalled, and modern web publication are different evidentiary layers. The narrative is about work and remembrance, not a reconstruction of procedures.

The center's official exhibit description includes home-front responses, workers' daily lives and civilian perspectives [7]. That is evidence of an interpretive program, not an inventory confirming every screen is functioning today. Historic photographs and recreated settings do not confer access to the places depicted.

## 4. What's still there
The documented public resource is a visitor-center exhibition, with oral-history interpretation and material on workers and households [7]. This draft does not inventory historic hardware or provide installation locations. Treat any displayed uniform or personal object as an exhibit whose label must establish its provenance, not as proof of ownership by the named narrator.

Staff spaces, active installations, private ranches and any unofficial sites are outside the visitor boundary. No fence approach, geographic scavenger search, access shortcut or historic field itinerary is supplied. The public museum can tell this labor story without a second site.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Proposed interpretive sequence, not an operator tour or tested indoor route:**

1. Before departure, read McNiel's short published testimony [5], then check the seasonal gate [1–4].
2. At the authorized visitor-center entrance, orient to the available public exhibits. Allow a modest indoor visit rather than building a day around an unbooked tour; no duration is represented as measured.
3. In the publicly available worker/home-front interpretation [7], attend to names, jobs and perspective rather than machinery. Read only exhibits open to visitors; do not seek technical files or staff areas.
4. End with the labor named by McNiel but easily overlooked: the people supporting other people. **Strongest story landing:** public memory becomes more honest when the worker is no longer invisible.

If conditions close the center, complete the same reflection with the published excerpt. No detour substitutes for a closed gate. No quiz, role-play, weapon simulation or disaster scoring.
