## 2. The story

**Named firsthand worker: Joe A. Sandoval**, in the Union Label and Service Trades Department, AFL-CIO's published *Walk in My Shoes* feature, December 21, 2015. His first-person recollection describes 43 years in a Pueblo steel mill's wire department and retirement in 2003. He remembers collecting his father after work as a teenager and the different languages and moods of people ending their shifts. This is a published retrospective worker account with editorial introduction, not an unedited recorded interview; the full book *Hearts of Steel* was not retrieved. [5]

Short primary voice: **“I knew it had been a good choice.”** [5]

**Original interpretation:** The story need not begin with the size of the machinery. Begin with recognition: a child knows which tired face in a crowd belongs to his father. Later he has his own working life to remember. Pride and hardship can coexist without cancelling each other. The museum can provide historical context while a worker's memory restores the person to the picture.

This is one man's perspective, not a verdict that every employee shared his satisfaction. A union publisher preserves a valuable labor voice and also has an institutional viewpoint. Do not turn affectionate memory into a comprehensive account of wages, discrimination, safety or industrial relations.

## 3. Then / now

Then: work shaped family time and belonging. Now: a public museum and historical archives allow that relationship to be interpreted without visiting an active workplace.

The museum occupies CF&I's **1901 medical dispensary** and opened in 2007. Its operator presents labor and immigration among its interpretive themes. The building's former care function provides a useful contrast to a machine-centered history, but does not prove that a named worker received treatment here. [6]

The source publication date is not the date of childhood memories. Present-day ownership, employment conditions and the status of former access features are outside this historical dossier. No past workplace description becomes current access advice.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

One indoor anchor; **45–60 minutes is an editorial planning estimate**, not an operator tour duration or booking.

1. **Before departure:** verify the museum's actual date-specific opening and admission. If closed or holiday hours remain unresolved, use the published reading at home rather than an industrial-site substitute. [1–2]
2. **Public admission:** enter only the museum. Orient to the difference between an interpretive institution and the neighboring work environment.
3. **History lens:** look at the public displays available that day for how they represent workers and households. Do not promise particular cases or request entry to staff areas. [6]
4. **Personal lens:** read the named worker's short recollection, identifying it as memory rather than a complete institutional history. [5]
5. **Strongest landing:** ask, “What would a child notice about a parent's working day that a company ledger would miss?” The ending is about recognition and perspective, not finding a historic work entrance.

The archive is an optional later research project subject to its own appointment, lead time and fees, not an extra room on this visit. No walking/driving chain, factory tour, industrial overlook or operational demonstration is offered.
