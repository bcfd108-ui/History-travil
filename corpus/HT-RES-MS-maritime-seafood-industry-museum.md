# Biloxi’s seafood industry, from the vessel to the picking table

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP22 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
A tall room at the modern Biloxi museum holds *Nydia*, a wooden sloop from the late nineteenth century. The Biloxi Historical Society’s notes on an 1897 newspaper account name William Nels Johnson as builder and describe the boat’s oak frames and cypress planking. The city gives the broader late-1800s date and Back Bay origin. A late-century yacht could take a builder’s skill to a paying owner in another city. The same town’s yards also made boats for companies that caught and packed food. [2, 3, 10]

In February 1911, National Child Labor Committee photographer Lewis Wickes Hine made a portrait of Marie Kriss. The committee caption identifies her as seven years old, places her at Biloxi Canning Company and says she shucked oysters and picked shrimp between bouts of watching a baby. It records 25 cents on some days. These are the committee’s recorded details. The photograph shows a girl standing beside a timber building. It cannot independently establish the hours she worked or what she was paid. A separate Barataria Canning Company caption makes a parent audible. Hine records CoRa Croslen saying, “I’m learnin’ her the trade.” The caption labels the child Alma and spells the child’s surname differently from the adult’s. [4, 5]

The canning business made possible some enterprises that outlasted a season or a shift. The museum documents Mary Skrmetti Anticich’s path to owning and managing a packing operation. Its account identifies public advocacy by an operator, including her position on a closed shrimping season in 1932. It also recounts a Pascagoula builder’s willingness to supply boats after banks declined her. The story is the museum’s historical account and the original financing record is open. [9]

Leroy Duvall spoke from the boat-owning side of the later industry. In his 2008 oral history, recorded in Biloxi, he said, “For 30 years I was a commercial fisherman in the shrimping industry in Biloxi.” He describes selling the boats around 1992 and families who worked in fishing in his neighborhood. Duvall was describing his own career and memory of a community, a different kind of testimony from Hine’s reform-era notes about a child. [6]

## 3. Then and now
The present building replaces a museum that operated in a former Coast Guard facility. Katrina destroyed the earlier museum building in 2005. A dated 2014 city notice documents the public reopening at Point Cadet. The modern atrium holds a surviving nineteenth-century vessel. The galleries give a place to interpret Biloxi’s industrial workforce. The Hine portrait records the historical labor subject at a separate, unverified industrial location. [2–5, 7]

## 5. Public progression
Enter at the museum’s staffed admissions door. Start at *Nydia* and look at how the vessel’s length and hull shape govern the room around it. Continue to the commercial fishing gallery if open. End at **Marie’s Twenty-Five Cents**, reading the Hine caption alongside the work on display. The gallery stop brings the child’s labor back into a story often told through boats and the people who owned them. [1–6, 10]
