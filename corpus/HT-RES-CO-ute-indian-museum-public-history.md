## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A museum can display history, but it can also expose who has been allowed to tell it. Here, the strongest question is not what disappeared. It is how living peoples describe themselves now.

**Named Tribal-published voice:** In its June 23, 2017 issue, *The Southern Ute Drum* reported the museum's reopening. It identified **Manual Heart**, a former chairman of the **Ute Mountain Ute Tribe**, and printed his statement: **“This building is here to tell people who we are and where we come from,”** as reported in the article. The spelling of his first name follows that issue. This is a published quotation reporting his address, not an audio recording heard in this research. The PDF's column extraction is disordered, but the named attribution and quoted sentence are identifiable in its reopening coverage. [3]

The same report distinguishes a June 9 Tribal opening and dedication from the June 10 public opening. It identifies **Betsy Chapoose**, then Northern Ute Cultural Rights and Protection Director, emphasizing continuing collaboration. These are historical roles and statements from 2017, not assertions about present officeholders or current visitor programming. [3]

**Separate sovereign account:** The Southern Ute Indian Tribe's own history describes dispossession and contested agreements, including its account of the 1873 Brunot agreement and 1874 ratification. Read that as the Tribe's published historical framing, not as an interchangeable account authored by every Ute government. This dossier does not adjudicate treaty rights or translate that history into directions to historic ground. [4]

**Keep the governments distinct:** The Southern Ute Indian Tribe, Ute Mountain Ute Tribe and Ute Indian Tribe of the Uintah and Ouray Reservation are distinct sovereign communities. Shared Ute history does not make one speaker a spokesperson for all three. The operator describes continuing work with representatives of all three on the museum and its exhibits; that is evidence of collaboration, not proof that every object, label or visitor activity carries universal agreement. [1,4]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Heart's sentence shifts the visitor's task from collecting facts about a supposedly vanished people to listening for authorship and continuity. The encounter should make room for both forced loss and present agency. It should not cast a museum visit as access to cultural knowledge that has not been offered publicly.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Suggested interpretive progression, not a surveyed floor plan:**

1. **Before entry:** check the operator's date-specific gate and read the three governments' names. Establish that the visitor is entering a public museum, not receiving a general invitation to community or cultural places.
2. **Orientation:** within open public galleries, look for a statement identifying who made the interpretation. If it is absent or unclear, record that uncertainty rather than assigning authorship.
3. **History in context:** read publicly offered accounts of dispossession alongside the Southern Ute Tribe's separately published account. Keep each author's identity visible; agreement or difference should not be invented.
4. **Continuing life:** if current displays offer contemporary voices, attend to how speakers identify themselves. Do not assume a historical photograph or object represents every living Ute person.
5. **Strongest story moment:** finish with Heart's 2017 sentence and ask whether the visit changed who you understood to be speaking. The ending is an act of listening, not a collectible answer or a cultural performance expected from staff.

**On-site question, only if staff are available and welcome it:** How does the museum identify the different Tribal voices and updates in its current public interpretation?

If closed, use the sources in the same sequence as a reading session. No substitute outdoor or private-ground route is proposed. Where interpretation addresses loss, the activity remains quiet reflection, with no timing, scoring or hunt.
