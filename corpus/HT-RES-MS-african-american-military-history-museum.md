# The people who worked the East Sixth Street USO in Hattiesburg

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP24 · Labeled research draft · Quiet reflection.*

## 2. Story
Hattiesburg’s East Sixth Street USO opened on March 22, 1942, to serve Black soldiers coming from Camp Shelby. It stood near the Black business district around Mobile Street. The military and city that received those soldiers were segregated. A separate club offered ordinary things soldiers needed: a place to write, bathe, make a call, find a room, or meet someone away from training. Its existence records those needs and the segregated system that made this particular institution necessary. [2, 3]

People from the neighborhood built a program around that room. The nomination identifies David W. White’s advisory committee and Garnett Jones as building chairman. The committees included E. W. Hall, Collins Clark and Gaither Hardaway. Edwin W. Merrick was the first director. The register reproduces names and details from Polk directories and the club’s 1946 souvenir program. Those names show an operating organization with jobs extending from an admissions door to the snack bar and the floor. [2]

Iva Sandifer, a teacher who trained at Tuskegee Institute, and Cora Jones organized women’s volunteer work. Sandifer’s 1994 interview informs the nomination’s account. The cited 1946 program reports 344 local volunteers and 40,261 hours during 1942–46. It lists “Home Hospitality, Room Registry, and Sewing Service” among the work. The figures record the program’s own accounting rather than a count of unique soldiers. Church groups, local musicians and families also assisted. A unit passing through town could receive help from people who never wore a uniform. [2]

The present museum widened the story beyond this building and wartime years. Among its named subjects are Hattiesburg’s Ruth Bailey Earl, who served as an Army nurse in World War II, and Jesse Leroy Brown, whose naval service is separately documented by the Navy. Their displays give context to the club without implying that every service member represented served at this specific address. [4–6]

## 3. Then and now
The standing T-shaped frame building is the former USO. The modern museum opened in 2009 under the Hattiesburg Convention Commission. Its lobby and exhibit spaces reinterpret the USO and the longer history of Black military service. The current gallery access is independent of Camp Shelby’s gates and exhibits. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The state nomination draws on Sandifer’s original oral history and the 1946 souvenir program. Read its printed extracts as documentary transmission. The cited recording, full program and original volunteer logs were not retrieved. [2]
- The nomination’s 1942–46 activity figure counts attendance at group activities, potentially repeatedly. It cannot establish that many distinct soldiers used the building. [2]
- The museum and the registry refer to the same standing East Sixth Street building. The USO’s March 1942 opening and the museum’s 2009 opening describe different uses. Named claims about Army nurse Ruth Bailey Earl remain limited to the museum’s public record. [1–5]
