## 2. The story

Standing on Jefferson Street near the early Natchez Bluff settlement, **King's Tavern**—also known in historic preservation records as the **Bledsoe House**—is documented in its **1971 National Register nomination (`NRHP 71000444`)** as a two-and-a-half-story hewn **poplar and cypress** heavy-timber frame building set over a **brick basement**, traditionally dated to **ca. 1769** and confirmed in property/post evidence as standing **prior to 1789** during Spanish rule of the Natchez District. [3]

Early tradition links the building to the terminal end of the **Natchez Trace**, serving travelers and overland mail carriers before Natchez's formal 1789/1790 Spanish town grid was laid out (its facade sits slightly askew from the later Jefferson Street alignment). On **June 6, 1799**, shortly after the United States took control of Mississippi Territory, **Richard King** received a formal license to keep a **public house / tavern** at the property—giving the building its enduring name. [3]

In **1817**, **Samuel King** sold the property to **Charles B. Green**, who mortgaged it to the Bank of Mississippi in **1823**. Following foreclosure, it was acquired by **Elizabeth Morgan Postlethwaite**, passing to **Emily Postlethwaite** and then **Mrs. Mary Anne Bledsoe**, remaining in the **Bledsoe family for six generations** as a private residence ("Bledsoe House"). The **Pilgrimage Garden Club** later acquired the structure (`owner of record on May 6, 1971` when listed as `NRHP 71000444`) and restored it for historic dining and interpretation. [3]

### Haunted history / lore

King's Tavern carries a prominent Natchez ghost-tour reputation—including stories that **three skeletons and a jeweled dagger** were discovered walled inside a chimney during a **1932** (or 1970s, depending on the telling) renovation, and claims of an apparition named **"Madeline"** (variously described as a murdered tavern waitress or mistress of Richard King) alongside haunted rooms and an ancient Indian grave under the floor. [2]

Crucially, **none of the 1932/1970s chimney-skeleton, dagger, or "Madeline" claims appears in the 1971 National Register nomination (`71000444`)**, nor were contemporary 1932 coroner, police, or newspaper records locating such a discovery retrieved in primary sources read this run. The tavern's physical age (pre-1789) and Richard King's 1799 license are documented facts; the chimney-skeleton and "Madeline" stories are separated here strictly as **unverified twentieth-century commercial/tour folklore**. [2, 3]

## 4. What's still there

- **Public (Jefferson St. sidewalk):** Complete exterior of the pre-1789/1799 building—gable roof with dormers, two-tiered front gallery/porch, brick basement foundation, and heavy timber framing skewing off the later street grid. [3, 4]
- **Public (reservation-only):** Historic ground-floor/basement dining rooms and hearth spaces during **Regina's Kitchen** Tuesday/Thursday reservation dinners or scheduled cooking classes. [1, 2]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Construction date (`c. 1769` vs. `pre-1789`):** Local tradition and modern signage frequently cite **1769** ("oldest building in Natchez"); `NRHP 71000444` notes the 1769 tradition conservatively, establishing definitively only that the house stood **prior to 1789** and was licensed as a tavern by **Richard King on June 6, 1799**. [3]
- **1932 vs. 1970s skeleton story:** Commercial lore alternates between 1932 and the 1970s for the alleged chimney-skeleton discovery; no primary Natchez police, coroner, or newspaper confirmation was found. [2, 3]
- **Pilgrimage Garden Club acquisition & restoration dates:** The 1971 NRHP form lists Pilgrimage Garden Club as owner; exact post-1971 restaurant lease/transition dates to Regina's Kitchen were not traced from city business filings. [1, 3]
