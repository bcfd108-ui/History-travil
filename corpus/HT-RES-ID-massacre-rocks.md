## 2. The story
Begin with homeland, not a supposedly empty corridor. The **Shoshone-Bannock Tribes' own published history** describes ancestral lands across a broad western region and seasonal fishing, hunting and plant gathering. It also describes the reduction of the Fort Hall land base after the treaty period and the Tribes' continuing sovereign governments and services. This is a living community's account, not background scenery for emigrants. [4]

The **Northwestern Band of the Shoshone Nation's history** explains how introduced cattle and sheep depleted important plant-food areas and intensified hunger. Its account links Pocatello's band to the Oregon and California trails. This contextual voice is not represented here as a specific eyewitness account of the August 1862 encounters. [5]

NPS interprets the August 9–10 fighting as involving several wagon companies and Pocatello's fighters, in a wider cycle that included earlier attacks on Shoshone people and resistance to emigrant trespass. It reports deaths on both sides. Treat this as an agency synthesis, not a complete casualty roll or agreement among all descendants. [2]

A primary voice survives in transcription. ISHS Reference Series 234 reproduces **Henry M. Judson's August 10, 1862 diary**: **“Our camp is a sorry one today--scarcely a smile to be seen”**. The same entry describes sale of dead travelers' belongings to meet survivors' needs and the purchase of paper that allowed his diary to continue. Its cited manuscript is *Diary of 1862, Omaha to Oregon*, Nebraska State Historical Society **#358, MS953**. The manuscript itself was not inspected. [6]

Judson records what he saw alongside what others told him, including speculative attribution. His grief is evidence of human loss; it does not independently establish every number, motive or identity in his narrative. The original Indigenous participants' voices are not supplied by substituting an emigrant diary for them.

## 3. Then / now
The name compresses a dispersed series of encounters into one apparently exact place. ISHS locates the lava formation roughly two miles west of a ravine associated with an attack and describes events across miles of road. **The prominent rocks, the modern park and every encounter site are not interchangeable.** [6]

Today's public interpretation can help a visitor understand movement and loss without walking supposed combat ground. Register Rock is a separate inscription/picnic location, not proof of where someone died. A signature establishes an act of inscription only to the extent supported by its documentation, not automatically the writer's participation in the 1862 violence.

## 4. What's still there

**Public access rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance. These access classifications are separate from the locator record.
- **Park interpretation and trail remnants:** NPS identifies a paved approach near the end of Park Lane, northeast of the visitor center, to Oregon Trail ruts. The route is historically documented public interpretation; current surface safety is unverified. [2]
- **Register Rock:** IDPR identifies emigrant inscriptions and a picnic area approximately two miles from the park. Its present protections and posted boundaries govern viewing; no touching, tracing, rubbing, chalking or new marks. [1]
- **River and rock landscape:** observe only from open, designated park surfaces. No descent to water, climbing or scrambling is required for this dossier.
- **Specific encounter locations, graves and private parcels:** not visitor stops assigned here. Historical descriptions are not access permission.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Proposed interpretive order, conditional on posted access and conditions:
1. Begin at the public park orientation area. Establish which paths and facilities are open; shorten the visit if winter surfaces are unsafe.
2. Read the Tribal accounts before an emigrant-centered interpretive panel. Notice whether a panel explains whose homeland the trail crossed.
3. If safely open, view the trail remnants from the designated route. The point is the scale of repeated passage, not reenacting pursuit or combat.
4. Treat Register Rock as an optional, separately accessed public stop, not a shortcut through historic ground. Read inscriptions from the permitted boundary, without requiring a particular name or photograph.
5. Finish with a quiet pause in a lawful public place. **Strongest story moment:** set Judson's need for paper beside the missing named accounts of Indigenous people who suffered here. What survives in an archive shapes whose loss a visitor can hear.

Reflection question: **Whose names and experiences are preserved in what I have read, and whose absence should I acknowledge instead of filling it with a story?** No reenactment, casualty-count game, trophy photograph or collection task.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The two-day August 9–10 focus in NPS and ISHS's broader August 8–10 regional frame need not be competing dates for one event; they cover different geographic/event scopes. Neither establishes a single precise visitor battlefield pin. [2,6]

**Do not confuse this place with City of Rocks or the Almo massacre marker.** Idaho State University's historical overview discusses the Almo story separately and rejects it as unsupported by contemporary diary/newspaper evidence. An alarming marker elsewhere is not corroboration for this stop. [7]

A quoted diary contains hearsay and rumor as well as observation. No allegation of disguised or collaborating attackers is adopted here as independently proven. Exact casualty identities and totals require deeper manuscript comparison and Tribal scholarship.

The current park page includes an implausible distance of three miles west of Idaho Falls. That line is not used for navigation. Its printed street address and park-level coordinate are retained with limited scope. [1]

No specific Tribal eyewitness recording, current complete interpretive-panel transcription, archaeological encounter boundary or winter route inspection was obtained. Tribal institutional histories supply context, not blanket endorsement of this draft.
