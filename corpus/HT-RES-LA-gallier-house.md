# Gallier House — an architect's own house, its engineering experiments, and the enslaved household behind them on Royal Street
HT-RES-LA-gallier-house · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

James Gallier Jr. built this house as a demonstration of what he believed a modern New Orleans residence could be, and he built it for his own family. Construction ran from 1857 to 1860 at 1132 Royal Street, on ground that had formed part of the Ursuline convent lands from 1727 until 1825. Gallier — son of the Irish-born architect James Gallier Sr. and himself one of the city's most important mid-nineteenth-century architects, designer of the French Opera House among other notable buildings — designed the two-story stuccoed-brick townhouse for his wife, Josephine Aglaé Villavaso, and their four daughters. The National Register nomination, prepared by Paul Goeldner of the National Park Service's Historic Sites Survey on January 15, 1974, states the significance plainly: the house was built and decorated in 1857–60 by Gallier as his own residence and was occupied by his family into the twentieth century. [3–7, 9]

The plan is a deliberate hybrid, and the sources describe it the same way from different angles. The Society of Architectural Historians' Archipedia entry records a mildly Greek Revival facade softened by a two-story, rosette-patterned cast-iron gallery, the ground floor rusticated and painted to resemble granite, a Creole carriageway leading back to a courtyard combined with the American preference for a staircase in the side hall rather than on the rear gallery, a double parlor on the ground floor, and four bedrooms above. The operator's Place page adds the townhouse arrangement the visitor still walks: parlors, bedrooms and principal living spaces in front, and the dining room, pantries, kitchen and quarters for enslaved people in the back. The original cast-iron gate at the formal front entry, painted the “Paris Green” Gallier used often in his projects, was designed by Gallier specifically for this house. [3, 5, 9]

What made the house famous among architects was not its front but its systems. Gallier gave his own house indoor plumbing with hot and cold running water — the operator singles out a bathtub with hot and cold water at a time when most other houses had neither — a ventilation system in the master bedroom, and a double skylight, including a skylight in the upstairs library whose glass panes opened to let heat escape in summer. The Archipedia inventory of the house's amenities at completion runs longer: plumbing and hot and cold running water on the second floor, a copper bathtub, a cast-iron cooking range, closets, and a ventilation system throughout. The Cultural Landscape Foundation notes a point visitors miss if they expect a detached kitchen: unlike many houses of the period, the kitchen here is located inside the house rather than in an outbuilding, while the service areas and the quarters for enslaved people extend in a structure from the rear of the house. An exterior cistern now on the property, dated about 1850, was moved here from a St. James Parish plantation — a later arrival, not a Gallier original, and printed as such. [3–5]

The comfort those systems delivered was produced by other people's labor, and the operator says so in its own summary of the house: the comfortable lifestyle was made possible through the work of enslaved people, and later domestic servants, whose work and lives are also interpreted on tours. The rear structure containing service areas and quarters for enslaved residents survives intact and is part of the tour, as it is at the sister house on St. Louis Street. The record inspected in this pass names no individual enslaved person at Gallier House — a gap printed in §6 rather than filled — while the operator's wider research program at the two houses, built from notarial, census, tax, succession and sacramental records, is the route by which such names have been recovered at Hermann-Grima House. What can be said on this evidence is structural and plain: the architect's showcase house and the quarters behind it are one property, planned together, and the tour reads them together. [1, 4]

Gallier did not enjoy the house long. The Landmarks Commission marker at the house, transcribed in the marker record, states that he died here on May 16, 1868, and that the property was owned by his descendants until 1917; the nomination and the landscape record give the family's tenure more loosely as into, or until, the early twentieth century, after which the house became a rooming house. That looseness is printed in §6. Architect Samuel Wilson Jr. measured the house in 1957 — the same measured-drawing tradition, through the Historic American Buildings Survey generation of New Orleans preservation architects, that underpins so much of the Quarter's documentation. [4–7, 9]

Its museum life came through a private family's foundation, then a university, then a women's exchange. In the mid-1960s the Freeman family hired Koch and Wilson Architects to restore the house; the New Orleans Town Gardeners researched, designed and planted the garden in 1969; and on the creation of the nonprofit Ella West Freeman Foundation, the same architects were rehired in 1970 to transform the house into a house museum, which opened to the public in 1971. The restoration could be unusually exact because Gallier's own architectural drawings, an original manuscript plan and house records survived, and because the original household inventory guided the refurnishing — the operator stresses that the period decorative arts on display reflect the Galliers' own documented possessions and taste, not a generic Victorian interior. The house was listed in the National Register and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1974. In 1986 the Freeman Foundation donated the property to Tulane University, which in turn donated it to The Woman's Exchange in 1996. The Exchange — the same organization that owns Hermann-Grima House — operates the two houses together as Hermann-Grima + Gallier Historic Houses, and a combined ticket and a ten-minute walk join them into a single argument about the nineteenth-century Quarter: the Federal merchant's house of 1831 on St. Louis Street, and the architect's engineered townhouse of 1860 on Royal. [1, 4, 5, 8]

Seasonally, the operator dresses Gallier House as its occupants would have — summer dress in the hot months, Creole death and mourning interpretation in the fall, and a Creole Christmas at the holidays — programming carried in the operator's current listings and best confirmed against the visit page before a trip built around it. [1, 2]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** 1132 Royal Street, between Ursulines and Governor Nicholls Streets in the French Quarter, New Orleans, Orleans Parish — an individually listed National Historic Landmark and a contributing property of the Vieux Carré Historic District. The operator's own walking directions in §6 give the address as 1126 Royal Street; the building, the nomination and the operator's address block all give 1132. [2–6]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Gallier House, 1132 Royal St., New Orleans, LA 70116, 504-274-0748 — guided-tour museum entry only, at the published tour times. Stay with the tour group, as the operator requires; the courtyard, carriageway and rear quarters are visited as part of the tour, not as open ground. [1, 2]

## 5. Public progression

Arrive for a timed tour — 10:30, 11:30, 1:30, 2:30 or 3:30, never Tuesday — and read the house front to back, because that is its argument. Begin at the Paris Green gate and the granite-painted ground floor with the architect showing his city what he could do, then take the parlors as the family's documented rooms, furnished from their own inventory rather than from imagination. **Strongest story moment · Hot water, carried by a plan and by people.** The skylight that opens, the bath that runs hot and cold, and the range in an inside kitchen were engineering; the quarters at the back of the same L are who made the engineered comfort livable, day after day. Spend the tour's back half — carriageway, courtyard, service wing and quarters — at least as attentively as its front, and if the day allows, walk the ten minutes to Hermann-Grima House on the combined ticket and compare an 1831 Federal household's work yard with an 1860 architect's service wing. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **The operator's address disagrees with itself.** The operator's address block, the National Register nomination and the landmark record all give 1132 Royal Street. The operator's own walking directions on the same visit page twice direct visitors to “1126 Royal Street, between Ursulines and Governor Nicholls Streets.” The two numbers are printed here as found; 1132 is the listed, nominated address and the one used in this dossier's gate. [2–6]
- **When the Gallier family tenure ended.** The Landmarks Commission marker transcribed in the marker record gives a precise end — owned by Gallier's descendants until 1917. The nomination states only that the house was occupied by his family “into the 20th century,” and the landscape record says it remained in the family “until the early twentieth century,” when it became a rooming house. No deed or conveyance reconciling 1917 with the looser accounts was inspected in this pass. [4, 6, 7, 9]
- **No enslaved individual at Gallier House is named in the record inspected.** The operator interprets the lives and work of the enslaved people of this house on tours and documents the quarters as intact, but the pages and records inspected in this pass name no Gallier House enslaved resident, give no count, and cite no house-specific inventory for them, in contrast to the named, database-backed record published for Hermann-Grima House. The blank is left blank; the operator's enslaved-individuals research program is the stated route by which it may be filled. [1, 3, 4]
- **Gallier's death — date documented, cause not sourced here.** The marker record gives May 16, 1868, at this house. Secondary retellings attribute the death to yellow fever; no primary or institutional source inspected in this pass states a cause, so none is claimed in the story above. [9]
- **Kitchen placement, stated two ways without contradiction intended.** The landscape record emphasizes that the kitchen is inside the house rather than in an outbuilding, “unlike many houses of the period”; the operator's Place page places kitchen and quarters together in the back of the townhouse plan, with service areas and quarters extending in a rear structure. The two accounts describe the same L-shaped service arrangement at different resolutions; no plan was inspected in this pass to fix the kitchen's exact room. [3, 4]
