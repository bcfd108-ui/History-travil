# Fort Maurepas, the people left to keep it and the search for its ground

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP25 · Labeled research draft · NO-ANCHOR · Quiet reflection.*

## 2. Story
In 1699 a French expedition led by Pierre Le Moyne d’Iberville established a post in the Biloxi Bay area. The archaeological synthesis distinguishes anchorage, search for a colonial position and erection of the wooden fort. It describes the work of sailors, soldiers, laborers and Canadian participants. The fort was a small and supply-dependent undertaking. [2]

In a nineteenth-century printed English translation of an early French account, the narrator begins the departure episode, “When our fort was finished, M. D’Iberville returned to France”. The text places Sauvol in command in May 1699, Bienville as the king’s lieutenant and De Bordenac as chaplain. An editorial note preserves an accounting of trades including mechanics and masons. The translation transmits a colonial voice with its own spelling and viewpoint. The labor, relationships and knowledge of Indigenous communities around the bay are only incompletely known through the French papers and archaeology read here. [2, 3]

The garrison faced disease and shortages. The state archaeology report describes trade for supplies and the choice of a replacement colonial location on the Mobile River. Sauvol died in 1701. By early 1702 the new post was occupied. The state archaeologists distinguish this early episode from Vieux Biloxi, a later settlement and brief seat of French Louisiana in 1719–21. The two episodes are related and leave different historical questions. [2, 3]

## 3. Then and now
Today’s Fort Maurepas Park uses the fort’s name in a shore recreation setting. It reflects later public commemoration, including rebuilding the recreation facilities after Katrina. The historic wooden fort and its footprint have not been authenticated at the modern park. State archaeologists reviewed more than one historical location and found that the available material did not resolve the fort’s exact site. [1, 2, 4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- Heritage interpretation associates the later capital with the fort’s name. The state archaeology report distinguishes Fort Maurepas in 1699–1702 from Vieux Biloxi in 1719–21. The modern shore park is commemorative. [1, 2, 4]
- Archaeologists found chronologically mixed or insufficient material and could not examine some areas. Neither archaeological candidate is proof of a tourable fort ground. The contemporaneous plan cited by the state report was not independently verified as an archival image or as a map that can be aligned to modern controls. [2]
- The primary printed source is an English translation in an edited historical collection. The underlying French manuscript and a Tribe-published account addressing this exact colonial site were not recovered. Names and locations from the archaeology are intentionally omitted from travel routing. [2, 3]
