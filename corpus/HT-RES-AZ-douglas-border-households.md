## 2. The story
**Raúl Hector Castro** recalls his Douglas-area childhood in a **September 12, 2013** interview by **Pam Stevenson**, recorded by **Bill Stevenson**. The published transcript's opening pages describe a household whose father worked at a smelter and whose mother supported children through midwifery after his death. Castro remembers unequal school transportation and asks **“why do they ride, and we walk?”** [4, pp. 1–4]

The household-work passage and school recollection are directly read first-person testimony, not an invented representative family. The testimony is retrospective and the website transcript is edited. His broad claims about births attended, historical assistance eligibility and later discrimination are not adopted as independently established statistics or law. Only the opening four pages were reviewed; no claim is made to have listened to the recording or read the whole interview. [4]

A separate archive lead, **Francisco Escobar Sr.**, describes his parents coming from Sonora and family life in Douglas. The search-indexed transcript opening relates his father's long employment and the siblings' schooling. The original PDF did not download successfully, so this dossier does **not** treat that lead as a fully inspected second interview. [5,6]

The interpretive focus is ordinary responsibility: wage work, care work and children's education. These are not background decorations for a mining-town story. They are how the town was lived.

## 3. Then / now
The Douglas Oral History Project explains its purpose as preserving everyday working people's stories, including both discrimination and resilience. It places households and community memory at the center rather than treating only prominent buildings or employers as history. This is a present community project's statement of purpose, not direct testimony about every early family. [7]

Today's museum interprets local history through multiple collections. Its website describes school memorabilia and a smelter room, while another area tells the story of photographers **Marty Stolp and Edessa Lee**.
