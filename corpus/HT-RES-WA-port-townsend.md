## 2. The story
The most useful maritime story here is not that old boats survived unchanged. It is that knowledge keeps moving between workers, apprentices, institutions and generations.

**Named worker in a contemporary primary record:** the Coast Guard’s **Navigation and Vessel Inspection Circular 7-95**, dated **November 7, 1995**, acknowledges **Ernie Baird, Repairer, Port Townsend, Washington**, as a participant in the joint industry/Coast Guard working group. The acknowledgments, printed page A-1, establish his documented contribution to wooden-vessel inspection and repair guidance. This is stronger evidence of professional work than an attractive boat photograph. It does not make him sole author, or prove responsibility for every recommendation. The official text was read; no technical repair instructions are reproduced here. [5]

The Wooden Boat Festival’s award organization supplies biographical context: Baird joined Mark Burn’s Port Townsend Boatworks in 1978, later established his own company, and in 2004 transferred the business to seven employees who formed Haven Boatworks. That is the award institution’s retrospective account, not a recovered employment contract or first-person diary. It connects skilled work with succession without inviting visitors into the successor business. [6]

**A direct published working voice:** Northwest Maritime’s March 5, 2026 interview with **Joel Arrington, Boatshop Manager**, describes students building skills and then two Point Hudson Dories during a four-week unit. Arrington says, **“Each student starts to find their voice through the part of the project they take responsibility for.”** The interview text was retrieved directly after the web reader timed out. This is a contemporary instructor’s published account, not an interview conducted for this dossier, and the student work is not promised to be on display today. [7]

Original interpretive voice: *A waterfront stays alive when skill can pass from one pair of hands to another. A repairer’s name in a national working group and an instructor’s account of students finding their part in a build are two records of that transfer.* This wording is ours, not Baird’s or Arrington’s.

**Community beyond the shop:** the city library records that its maritime collection was established in 1993 with a federal library-services grant through the Washington State Library. Manuals and histories are another form of shared working infrastructure. This is not simply a town of boats; it is a town that preserves and circulates knowledge about them. The public library is a source resource, not an activated extra stop here. [8]

This entry concentrates on documented maritime labor and learning. It does not claim the town’s story begins with settlers or supply an unsourced Tribal narrative. A fuller account of S’Klallam and Chemakum history requires Tribal-published sources first and remains a distinct research need.

## 3. Then / now
- **1995:** Baird’s expertise appears in a national working-group acknowledgment. **Now:** the record lets a visitor recognize the importance of repair and judgment, not just famous launches. [5]
- **1978–2004:** the award biography describes a path from employment to business and employee succession. **Now:** a current yard is a workplace, not a historic-house attraction. [6]
- **2026:** Arrington describes supervised learning. **Now:** a visitor must not assume class participation, workshop entry or access to tools is included in public admission. [7]
- **Historic town:** the official Port Townsend Historic District nomination records a wider built environment. **Present visit:** a district boundary does not grant entry to its private buildings or establish the accessible museum doorway. [9]

Do not describe the town as frozen in a vanished Victorian era. The selected primary records deliberately extend the story into continuing skilled work.

**Indoor museum anchor with a conditional institutional companion, not a routed waterfront walk.** Suggested museum allowance: 45–75 minutes, an editorial estimate rather than an operator tour duration.

3. **Connect work to succession.** The award biography supplies a bounded example of workers becoming the next business, rather than a generic assertion that skills simply survived.

Restrict the visit to spaces staff identify as public; no promise of an open Boatshop or class observation. If unavailable, read Arrington’s interview remotely instead.
5. **End at the strongest story moment:** the instructor describes a learner accepting responsibility for part of a vessel. That carries the story from preservation of things to continuity of capability. Finish in a public interior, not at the edge of a working dock.
