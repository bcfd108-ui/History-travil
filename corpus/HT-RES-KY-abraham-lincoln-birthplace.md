## 2. Story
**Abraham Lincoln Birthplace, Sinking Spring Farm and Memorial · Research draft · Fred**

In December 1859 Abraham Lincoln told his political associate Jesse W. Fell that a biography should “be modest, and not to go beyond the materials.” The enclosed sketch begins by putting his birth on February 12, 1809 in Hardin County, Kentucky, and names the Hanks family on his mother's side. The Library of Congress supplies the letter and sketch and a typed transcription. The family had moved to Knob Creek by 1811. The landscape at Sinking Spring belongs to that early story. A later generation decided what kind of building should tell it. [2–4]

The people who turned the farm into a public memorial made consequential choices. Robert Collier and the Lincoln Farm Association raised donations for a memorial and John Russell Pope drew the design. A construction budget forced a smaller project. Theodore Roosevelt laid the cornerstone at the 1909 centennial. William Howard Taft dedicated the completed memorial in 1911. The founders enclosed a cabin against the formal stone interior. The 1916 statute authorizing federal acceptance used the term “the log cabin in which he was born,” as the NPS cultural landscape inventory records. That language describes what the transfer assumed. A later study using tree-ring samples dated the oldest sampled log to 1848, decades after the birth. The surviving building and log exhibit have strong evidence as a history of commemoration. [3–7]

## 3. Then and now
The farm and the stone memorial are different generations of evidence on the same site. Inside the memorial, NPS presents the old log structure as the symbolic birth cabin. Its public interpretation preserves the early-twentieth-century act of memorial-making and explains the limits of the claim the association made about the logs. [3–7]

## 4. Surviving features and access
- **Public interiors when open** — Birthplace visitor center and Memorial Building. The symbolic cabin is seen from the public interior boundary.
- **Public exterior** — Signed paved approach and memorial forecourt, subject to posted accessibility and weather notices.
- **Historic landscape** — Sinking Spring and the family farm context. Keep to signed routes and leave water and closed ground outside this entry.
- **Separate unit** — Knob Creek's grounds, tavern and associated cabin have distinct identities and separate visitor schedules. [1, 3–5]

## 5. Public trip progression
Begin at the visitor center. Read the opening of the Fell sketch before walking to the memorial. Finish at **The cabin inside the monument**, where the modest farm room the donors wanted remembered and the stone building they built to remember it occupy one view. If the memorial is closed, the public exhibits at the visitor center and the typed source carry the story. [1–7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
- Lincoln names Hardin County in 1859. The modern NPS and HABS records locate the site in LaRue County. The county difference is historical jurisdiction, not a second birthplace. [2–4, 8]
- The 1916 text and some older records describe the cabin as original or traditional. The park's tree-ring account contradicts that literal attribution; the cultural landscape article uses the weaker “not conclusively identified.” This entry calls it symbolic and dates the sampled wood after Lincoln's birth. The typed law and later science carry different kinds of evidence. [3–7]
- The complete laboratory sampling report was not retrieved. No second-source coordinate agreement was made for the present public entrance.
- LOC HABS frame KY-95 view 1's direct image record did not load in the first retrieval. The cataloged federal survey is a lead; no reproduced photo was selected. [8]
