## 2. The story

Begin with a sovereign people speaking in the present, rather than with a museum label placing them entirely in the past. The Eastern Shoshone government's history describes traditional homelands extending back beyond written treaty records. It distinguishes that homeland from the later reservation and describes successive reductions in the land base. It also identifies the Eastern Shoshone and Northern Arapaho as distinct peoples, not interchangeable names for one community. [5]

A primary historical text is available through the government's own publication of the **1868 Fort Bridger treaty**. Article 2 uses the phrase **“absolute and undisturbed use and occupation”** in describing the land set apart. Article 7, in the same document, imposes English schooling requirements. [6] Read the contrast carefully: promised protection of land and intrusive federal conditions coexist in one instrument. The wording is historical evidence, not this dossier's statement of present law or visitor access rights.

The Tribe-linked museum's mission centers self-determination, language, knowledge and intergenerational continuity. [7] Its team account identifies **Robyn Rofkar**, an enrolled Eastern Shoshone member, with work on the digitization of elders' words for the Shoshone Dictionary, and **Sierra Coando**, also an Eastern Shoshone member, with archives, collections and research work. [8] These are named people doing contemporary historical stewardship, not invented narrators or generic representatives.

**Named firsthand voice, publication distinction explicit:** in a 2015 interview published by Native-led ICT from the National Museum of the American Indian's interview series, **Darwin John St. Clair Jr.**, identified there as chairman, describes responsibility **“To protect and preserve our treaty, sovereignty, water, air, wildlife, culture, language, government, and assets.”** [9] This is a historical interview attribution, not a claim about today's officeholder. It is a Native-published firsthand statement, **not a Tribe-published individual quotation**. The latter requirement remains an explicit sourcing gap; the directly verified Tribal publications above provide institutional voice and named biographies instead.

The story's central turn is that preservation is not only keeping old objects. It includes who controls interpretation, whose language continues, and who decides what can be shared. This is an interpretive synthesis of the published materials, not permission to request restricted teachings or enter a community space.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a treaty recorded relations between governments, promises and coercive conditions. **Now:** the Eastern Shoshone government chooses to publish that text alongside its own history and language resources. [1,3,6]

The comparison is documentary, not a camera match. Do not turn treaty boundary descriptions into a walking route or a map of places to investigate. Do not treat the existence of digitized cultural material as a general license to reproduce it.

The proposed Hub's combination of archives and public interpretation also makes a useful distinction: an institution can have public-facing exhibits while holding other material under controlled access. Future public programming does not mean everything in its custody is public.

## 4. What's still there

The museum team's published work offers evidence of living language and archival stewardship. It does not prove that an archive reading room accepts walk-ins. [8]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- An existing Cultural Center and a proposed Cultural Hub are not the same operating facility. The design narrative refers to community planning and a concept; no opening receipt was found. [2]
- Current winter hours, public access arrangements and accessibility remain unverified. This is uncertainty, not a claim that the Center is permanently closed.
- The Tribe's treaty page prints a ratification date later than its printed proclamation date. Do not silently correct that anomaly or treat the transcription as a checked facsimile. The treaty's July 3, 1868 date is clearly printed; later-date reconciliation remains open. [6]
- No audio was listened to; no voice recording, pronunciation or transcript has been authenticated here.
- A named Tribe-published first-person statement was not retrieved. The government-linked newsletter archive was followed, but the November issue failed to fetch. This does not justify substituting an invented attribution. [10]
- The account is not a legal analysis of treaty rights and does not authorize hunting, fishing, travel across land or access to records.
