# Cades Cove, Great Smoky Mountains National Park · The mill that stayed and the house that moved

*On Old Ground · Tennessee · Tier 1 seeding · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
A family arriving with corn at John P. Cable’s mill around 1870 could leave with meal after paying the miller a share of the grain. The water that did the grinding had already required considerable human labor. Cable bought land near two creeks in 1868. A diversion brought water from Forge Creek into Mill Creek. A low dam, race and wooden flume delivered the water to the upper edge of the wheel. The water fell, the wheel turned and the stones ground. Sawing timber was another business at the site. [2, 5]

Rebecca “Becky” Cable belonged in both halves of this story. When her father died in 1891, she and her brothers James and Daniel inherited the gristmill together. Rebecca and Daniel had already bought the nearby store property from Leason Gregg and James Beals in 1887. Its ground floor once held merchandise, with living quarters upstairs. Rebecca later kept boarders, raised cattle and cared for her brother’s children. A frame house now displayed beside the mill tells part of that story. When Rebecca did this work, the house stood upstream on Forge Creek Road. [2, 5]

People were still making livelihoods here when Tennessee began buying ground for the national park. Some households sold willingly. John W. Oliver repeatedly challenged the acquisition of his property in court. A life lease let some residents remain in exchange for a lower payment and limits on how they used the land. Rebecca held one. The cove’s school closed in 1944 and its post office in 1947. Kristen Olson’s study of the park traces the decisions by which this former neighborhood became a place where visitors were shown a particular version of mountain life. [5–7]

## 3. Then and now
The wheel stands at the mill site. Much of the structure visitors see passed through the hands of Civilian Conservation Corps workers, who rebuilt it in 1935–36 using salvaged and newly cut timber. In 1956, park staff moved the Gregg–Cable house to this cluster without its kitchen wing, back porch or outbuildings. They also reconstructed a blacksmith shop. The resulting arrangement preserved individual buildings while changing what visitors could see of family businesses, roads and neighbors. [2, 5, 7]

## 5. Public progression
From the visitor-center lot, the right-hand exhibit path leads to the mill. Look first at the point where water arrives above the wheel. Then look at the house from the public exhibit area. **What land ownership looked like for Rebecca Cable** is the story to take away here. She held a share of the mill, bought and worked at a store, grazed cattle and later occupied property under a life lease. The path between the mill and the house is short today. The record restores some of the distance, work and changing rights that the path leaves out. [2, 5, 6]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The NPS visitor page describes one wheel serving grinding and sawing. The park’s technical inventory, citing the 1938 survey, describes a separate wheel for the sash saw. Both operations are documented. The inventory carries the more precise machinery attribution. [2, 5]
- The visitor interpretation dates the house to 1879. The technical inventory records the 1876 land purchase and construction narrative without establishing a completion date in that passage. The timber’s origin at Cable’s mill is described as probable. [2, 5]
- Cades Cove was part of a Cherokee hunting landscape before Euro-American settlement. A Cherokee-authored account specific to this mill parcel and a verified two-source entrance coordinate remain open. The lead sheet also named the Mountain Farm Museum, which the park places at Oconaluftee in North Carolina. This filing covers the Tennessee mill ground. [1, 9]
- Visitation signal for ordering the lead is **11,527,939 recreation visits to the entire national park in 2025**. A separate count for this mill was not established. [10]

## 7. Historical photograph
**Adopted for desk review.** The inspected black-and-white frame shows water moving over timber courses, the exposed log cribbing and loose stone where the two dams meet. It records the mill’s water supply in 1938. The mill building and the visitor path are outside the frame. The photograph index names Charles S. Grossman, dates this exposure and identifies the park’s original. The federal historical handbook identifies Grossman as the Park Service landscape architect doing this documentation.
