# HT-RES-NH-canterbury-shaker-village — Hands to Work, and Hearts to God

Division NH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a hillside in Canterbury, New Hampshire, stands one of the most intact of the Shaker villages: a community of the United Society of Believers gathered in 1792 on Benjamin Whitcher's farm, which grew to some 350 to 400 members in four families, built more than a hundred buildings, and held Shakers in residence for two hundred years, until the death of its last sister in 1992. Its 1792 Meeting House and 1793 Dwelling House survive among twenty-four Shaker-built structures on 694 acres, and the village is today Canterbury Shaker Village, a nonprofit museum and National Historic Landmark — the preserved home of a living religious community, not a reconstruction of one.

## 1. Trip gate

Canterbury Shaker Village is a nonprofit museum at 288 Shaker Road, Canterbury, NH 03224, in Merrimack County, about twelve miles northeast of Concord; phone 603-783-9511. The museum's admission page gives the present terms: adults $25; veterans, active duty, and students $22.50; members and visitors under 18 free, school tours excepted. Admission includes the day's guided tours, limited to 25 people; its principal tour, Shaker Stories, runs about sixty minutes, and the museum puts the average visit at two and a half to three hours.

The village keeps a season. The museum states that the village is currently closed for the winter season as this dossier is checked, with private tours available in the off-season by advance arrangement. In season the buildings open daily and individual buildings keep posted hours within the day, the schedule subject to change. The grounds are a separate matter: the museum's nature trails are free to explore daily from dawn to dusk. The museum offers no food service; the Village Store carries light snacks and beverages, and visitors are welcome to bring a picnic lunch.

The visitor boundary is that of a museum set inside a landmark district: the historic buildings are entered on the museum's ticket and schedule, while the surrounding 694 acres, under permanent conservation easement, stay open on the trails whether the buildings are open or not.


## 2. The story

The people who built this village called themselves the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing. The landmark nomination (Lisa Mausolf, August 1992) states the outline: founded in England, the society was brought to America in 1774 by Ann Lee — Mother Ann to the Believers — and its first American community was established in 1776 at Niskeyuna, now Watervliet, New York; the shaking and dancing of their worship made them "Shakers" to the world. The faith held to celibate, communal living, men and women separate but equal: property in common, confession, pacifism, and separate doors and stairs for brethren and sisters. Each family was led by two elders and two eldresses. The Millennial Laws of 1821 codified even their building: "Headings, mouldings and cornices, which are merely for fancy may not be made by Believers." Mother Ann's charge, as the nomination quotes it, was the whole rule of life: "Hands to work and hearts to God."

Shakers first reached Canterbury in 1783. Benjamin Whitcher, an early convert who had sheltered the local Believers, gave them his farm; Shaker families were living on it by 1787, 35 people by the 1790 census. In 1792 the first 43 Canterbury Shakers were "called to order." They raised the meetinghouse first — in reverent silence, with no talking louder than a whisper, its frame raised on May 9, 1792, and the building completed on September 20 — under Moses Johnson, sent from New Lebanon to build to its 1786 prototype. A tannery followed, and then the dwelling house, begun in 1793.

The community grew into four families: the Church Family, first in seniority, then the Second Family in 1800, the North Family in 1801, begun as a novitiate order, and the West Family in 1806. At its height in the 1840s, by the nomination's account, Canterbury held 350 to 400 believers, approximately 3,000 contiguous acres in Canterbury and another thousand in Concord and Loudon, and more than one hundred buildings.

Work was worship's other half, and the village sold what it made to the "world's people": the nomination lists herbs, seeds, medicines, dairy products, sewn and other goods. The garden seed business ran, in the published account of Canterbury's own Eldress Bertha Lindsay and Sister Lillian Phelps, from 1794 into the early 1890s — seeds in neatly labeled boxes and tinted paper packets, sorted and packed by the sisters in the Children's House loft. Herbs dried in the North Shop's attic lofts, especially those for the sarsaparilla distilled in the Syrup Shop. Elder Henry Blinn's printing office (1869–1877) made Canterbury the publishing center for the northern Shaker communities, the Park Service records. Canterbury's best-known invention was Brother David Parker's washing machine of 1858, designed and manufactured for sale here — one of the few Shaker patents. New technology was welcomed to the end: a generating plant in 1910, before the state capitol in Concord was electrified, and a prefabricated steel garage in 1923, the last building raised.

The decline was long, and the sisters carried it. The North Family closed in 1894 and the Second Family in 1916; when the Shaker society at Enfield, New Hampshire, disbanded in 1923, its sisters moved to Canterbury. The last brother, Irving Greenwood, died in 1938, and the nomination states plainly that the village's survival owed much to the strength of Canterbury's women's community. In 1947 the parent ministry itself, come from New Lebanon by way of Hancock, made Canterbury the seat of the Shaker Lead Ministry. In 1965 the Lead Ministry voted to close the covenant, admitting no new members. The last Shaker Eldress, Bertha Lindsay, died at Canterbury in 1990. Sister Ethel Hudson, the last Canterbury Shaker, died in the Dwelling House in September 1992 — the village's two-hundredth year, making Canterbury, in the nomination's words, the first Shaker village to reach its 200th anniversary with a Shaker in residence.

Preservation was arranged before the end. Shaker Village, Inc., a nonprofit, has owned and administered the site since 1969, taking the retained acreage by conveyance in 1972, the nomination records. The village was entered in the National Register of Historic Places on June 17, 1975, and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1993. The museum states the transition in one sentence: the village has operated exclusively as a museum since 1992, when Ethel Hudson died. The few remaining Shakers live at Sabbathday Lake, Maine.

## 3. Then / now

Then, this hillside was a working religious commonwealth. Four families of brethren and sisters — celibate, holding all things in common — rose, worshipped, and worked in more than a hundred buildings: meetinghouse and ministry shop on the Religious Row, dwellings and infirmary on the Residential Row, the brethren's and sisters' shops, the laundry with its steam drying room, the syrup shop distilling sarsaparilla, mills on nine man-made ponds, and the great Cow Barn, 200 feet long, on the farm row. The world's people came to the brick Trustees' Office on Shaker Road to trade, and no farther.

Now, the Church Family's core is what survives: twenty-four contributing buildings clustered on about thirteen acres, in their five rows, inside 694 acres of fields and woodlots. Of the other three families, only the North Family Trustees' Office and a Second Family shed remain, both on private property outside the museum. The Cow Barn burned in 1973; its granite foundation walls are the village's one ruin. What changed the place least is its fabric: the buildings were continually updated — plumbing, wiring, stoves, linoleum — and those accretions were kept, so the village a visitor walks is not restored to one chosen decade but preserved as two hundred years of Shaker life left it. The difference is the silence where the work was: the buildings are a museum's now, and the last resident's home, the Dwelling House, is an exhibit of the life she actually lived there until 1992.

## 4. What's still there

The nomination counts twenty-eight contributing resources, built between 1792 and 1923, with no noncontributing structures: twenty-four buildings, the Meeting House fence, the Shaker Road stone walls, the cemetery, and the Cow Barn foundation — all the Church Family's, in five rows west of Shaker Road. With the exception of the brick Trustees' Office, the pressed-metal Fire House and Power House, and the plank woodshed and cart shed, the buildings are sheathed in clapboards, white predominating, and almost all rest on granite block foundations.

The Meeting House (1792) is the first building the community erected and, the nomination judges, among the least changed structures in the village: two and a half stories under a gambrel roof, 34 by 44 feet, closely modeled on Moses Johnson's New Lebanon house of 1786, its interior woodwork originally painted dark blue. Its two original stairways, one for men and one for women, were removed in 1815; the village Ministry slept and dined in its upper floors until 1878, moving then to the Ministry Shop (1848) beside it.

The Dwelling House (1793) is the largest building in the village, dominated by the domed cupola that holds a Paul Revere bell — hung in the belfry added in 1832 to call the family to meals and meetings, and lowered five and a half feet in 1842 after the New Lebanon Ministry disapproved of its size. The T-shaped, three-story house of 56 rooms began at 32 by 42 feet and grew by additions in 1798, 1805–1807, 1814, and 1837, when the chapel wing was built and the attic was fitted with two under-eaves spaces, six closets, fourteen cupboards, and 101 drawers in symmetrical arrangement — storage the nomination calls the crowning achievement of Shaker architecture at Canterbury. It is, the nomination states, the only Shaker dwelling house among the surviving villages that includes portions of its original 18th-century structure — and it was Ethel Hudson's residence until her death.

The Residential Row also holds the Sisters' Shop (1817), center of the village's tailoring, with its tailoring counters and the Eldresses' Confession Room; the Children's House (1810), built where the Trustees' Office now stands and moved east of the road in 1831 or 1833, where garden seeds were sorted and packed in the loft; and the Infirmary (1811), built as a guest house, converted to an infirmary in 1849 — with the village's first water closet installed in 1852 — and considered one of the oldest and most intact medical facilities in the state. Facing the row across Shaker Road stands the Trustees' Office (1830–1832), the village's only brick building, 72 by 42 feet, built of bricks the Church Family made itself; it housed the Lead Ministry's office and, from October 1848, a United States post office. The Enfield House (1826), moved to its present site in 1918, took its name from the Enfield sisters who came when their community disbanded in 1923; its basement was the center of the poplar ware industry.

The Workshop Row holds the Brethren's Shop (1824); the North Shop (1841), among the most pristine structures in the village, fitted with hand looms, fine built-in drawers and cupboards, and attic lofts for drying herbs; the Syrup Shop, moved to its site in 1841 for distilling sarsaparilla syrup; the Laundry (1795 and later), grown from the 1795 spin shop, its 1908 steam drying room still to be seen; and the Carriage House (1825), once the Yellow Building, which today holds the museum's gift shop and exhibition area. The utility row keeps the Carpenter Shop (1806), Fire House (1908), Power House (1910), and Steel Garage (1923). The farm row holds the Horse Barn (1819), the School House (1823, jacked up in 1863 onto a new schoolroom built beneath it), the Cart Shed (1840), the Bee House (1837), the Woodshed (1861), and the granite foundation of the Cow Barn (1858), destroyed by fire in 1973.

Beyond the buildings: the dry stone walls along Shaker Road, laid in 1793 and 1797–98 and rebuilt since; the Shaker Cemetery, its wall dating to 1798, where the individual tombstones were removed in 1900 and a single granite monument, lettered simply "Shakers" and presented in 1904, stands at its center; and the mill landscape to the east — five of the Shakers' nine man-made ponds, with their dams, headraces, and mill foundations, on the museum's land.

The museum's own present count differs from the nomination's and is printed as its own: its admission page describes a campus of 25 original and 4 reconstructed Shaker buildings. The nomination's twenty-four contributing buildings are all original Shaker-built fabric of 1792–1923; which buildings the museum counts as its four reconstructions is not itemized in the sources consulted, and the difference is recorded in §6 rather than reconciled here.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Come up Shaker Road as the world's people did, and begin where they were received: the brick Trustees' Office, the one building raised to face the public road, where the community's business with the outside was done and its post office kept.

Then walk the lane the Shakers kept apart. Meeting House Lane leads south to the Religious Row: the Meeting House first, fenced and set back, the least changed building in the village — read its gambrel roof and plain face against everything the Millennial Laws forbade, and its two lost stairways, men's and women's, against the order of the worship inside. The Ministry Shop beside it housed the elders and eldresses who governed the village's spiritual life.

Cross to the Residential Row and take the Dwelling House as the village's center: the cupola and its Revere bell above, the communal kitchen, bakery, and dining room on the first floor, the separate staircases, and the attic's 101 drawers — order made visible. The Sisters' Shop and Children's House carry the women's and children's work of the village, tailoring counters and seed-packing loft; the Infirmary carries its care, pharmacy and patients' rooms.

Work north through the shops as the village worked: the Brethren's Shop and its printing office, the North Shop's looms and herb lofts, the Syrup Shop where sarsaparilla was distilled, and the Laundry — where David Parker's patented washing machine was born of communal need. The Fire House, Power House, and Steel Garage close the rows with the community's last chapter: automobile, electric plant, and prefabricated steel.

End outside the rows. The Horse Barn and the Cow Barn's granite foundation mark the farm the village stood on; the cemetery, with its single "Shakers" monument, holds the community itself. If the day allows, finish on the museum's trails, through the fields to the mill ponds — the water system the brethren engineered east of the village.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Canterbury's order among the Shaker communities is not stated the same way in the primary record. The landmark nomination states that Canterbury "was the fifth and dates to 1792." The museum's own history page states that the followers of Mother Ann Lee "formed their seventh community in Canterbury, NH." A third count, "sixth," appears in Harvard Magazine's account of the village. The three ordinals stand unresolved; §2 prints no ordinal.

Peak membership is likewise given two ways. The nomination states that at its height in the 1840s the community had a membership of 350 to 400 believers in four families, and §2 follows it. The museum's history page states that at its height in the 1850s, 300 people lived and worked at Canterbury. Both figures stand.

The death of the last brother is dated a year apart. The nomination states that Irving Greenwood, the last brother at Canterbury, died in 1938; Harvard Magazine's account, citing the village's unofficial historian and Greenwood's biographer, gives 1939. Both stand, unresolved.

The building counts differ by source and by date. The 1975 National Register nomination described a village of twenty-two buildings on roughly 420 acres. The 1992 landmark nomination counts twenty-four contributing buildings and twenty-eight contributing resources in total, on 694 acres. The museum today describes 25 original and 4 reconstructed Shaker buildings, and its village map material describes a village of 32 buildings. The sources do not state uniformly what each count includes, and the museum does not itemize which four buildings it counts as reconstructed. The counts are printed as their sources give them.

The closing of the covenant and the founding of the museum are separate events that secondary accounts sometimes merge. The nomination dates the Lead Ministry's vote to close the covenant to 1965, Shaker Village, Inc.'s ownership and administration of the site to 1969, and the conveyance of the retained acreage to 1972. All three dates are printed as the nomination gives them.

The Syrup Shop's age is unresolved in the nomination itself. Its resource list dates the building 1797/1841, and it was long believed the core was built in 1797 as a grain store; but the nomination's text reports that work exposing the sills and foundation suggests the central section may be Benjamin Whitcher's own pre-Shaker building, of 1775–1782 — possibly the earliest surviving fabric in the village, and possibly Whitcher's residence or his granary. The Bee House's original purpose is likewise disputed within the community's own record: Elder Henry Blinn's writings say it was built in 1837 as an apple-drying house; Irving Greenwood held it was built to dry lumber. The nomination prints both, and so does this dossier. The Children's House move is dated by the nomination to "1831 or 1833," and is left so here.

One further claim in circulation is not carried in §2: that the Canterbury washing machine was displayed at the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia and sold to hotels and institutions. It appears in Harvard Magazine's account of the village but in no primary source consulted in this pass, and it is recorded here rather than asserted.

The landmark designation is dated firmly to the year and less firmly to the day. The museum and the National Park Service state the designation year, 1993; the specific date of April 19, 1993 circulates from the landmark program's summary listing through secondary records and was verified in this pass only there, so the year is asserted and the day is not. The nomination's documentation checklist records the property as recorded by the Historic American Buildings Survey, but the survey number is blank in the digitized copy consulted, so no HABS number is claimed in §10. Finally, the nomination defines the property's geography by boundary, not by point: eight UTM references in Zone 19 mark the corners of the 694-acre district, and no coordinate is scoped to the village itself. On the rule this catalog follows, the coordinates in §10 are left blank, and none have been taken from aggregator or map sites.
