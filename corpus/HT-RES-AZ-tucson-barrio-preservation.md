## 2. The story

**A resident becomes a historian.** **Lydia R. Otero** describes being born and raised in Tucson and returning to lived experiences through archival research. Their own biography connects this work to historic preservation, urban renewal and displacement. [3]

**Firsthand place memory.** In their published account of urban displacement, Otero remembers growing up close to Interstate 10 and experiencing the separation of a working-class barrio from the rest of the city. Their short voice is **“The freeway became an obstacle as well as a barrier.”** [4] The passage was read on Otero's own website, not transcribed from audio.

**Two histories, not one demolition.** Otero distinguishes freeway expansion from the **Pueblo Center Redevelopment Project**, approved by Tucson voters **March 1, 1966**, which targeted eighty acres of predominantly Mexican American downtown. Their work examines residents' lives as well as the demolished built environment. [4]

**Original interpretation.** A building inventory can record what remains while missing what made a neighborhood usable: familiar connections, shared routines and access to ordinary destinations. Preservation can therefore require records of people and relationships, not just architectural survival. That is a lens for this entry, not a claim that all residents held the same view.

### What the buildings cannot show

In the PBS transcript of a filmed interview, Otero says, “My parents were both born in this Barrio here, sometimes referred to as Barrio Viejo.” They recall downtown streets where speaking Spanish was ordinary and where neighbors from several communities shared a language schools had discouraged. This is a recollection in a published program transcript. The earlier Archive Tucson walk-and-talk recording remains an unheard source in this dossier. The testimony explains why a building survey alone can miss what was lost. [4,5,8]

Tucson Museum of Art identifies María Cordova as one of the residents displaced during urban renewal. Its ongoing exhibit puts her in relation to North Meyer Avenue, businesses and other households. Read Otero’s memory beside the museum’s account of Cordova. A building can survive a project that ends someone’s ability to live there. The museum distinguishes its historic block in Barrio Presidio, which it calls La Calle, from the larger terrain of the barrios discussed by Otero. Carry those source labels through the visit. [8–9]
