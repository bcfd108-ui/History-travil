# HT-RES-NH-strawbery-banke — The Neighborhood That Refused the Bulldozer

Division NH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

In Portsmouth's South End, around the filled tidal inlet that was Puddle Dock, stands a whole working waterfront neighborhood that was scheduled for demolition and instead became a museum: some ten acres of narrow streets, gardens, and historic houses — most of them on the sites where they were built between the late seventeenth century and the twentieth — preserved by a citizens' corporation, Strawbery Banke, Inc., formed in 1958 and opened to the public in 1965. The neighborhood carries more than 350 years in one place: Abenaki ground, the first English settlement on this part of the coast, a colonial seaport's merchants' and mariners' houses, an immigrant quarter of Irish and Jewish families, a scrap-metal and tenement decline, and the preservation fight that saved it.

## 1. Trip gate

Strawbery Banke Museum is operated by Strawbery Banke, Inc., a nonprofit educational corporation, at 14 Hancock Street, Portsmouth, NH 03801, in the South End on the Piscataqua River; its published telephone number is 603-433-1100. It is a ticketed outdoor site: visitors walk a fenced, landscaped campus of historic streets and enter the houses from the neighborhood's own lanes.

The Museum's own pages frame the visit as year-round programming with the historic houses at the center. Its site posts daily hours that vary by season and program, with houses, exhibitions, and craft demonstrations on the main-season schedule and special programs carrying the rest of the year. Admission is charged; the Museum supports itself, in its own published description, by admissions, sales, contributions, and membership. Current hours, ticket prices, and which houses stand open on a given day are as the Museum publishes them at strawberybanke.org.

The visitor boundary is the museum campus itself: the district continues past the fence. Eight houses inside the National Register district were privately owned when the district was nominated and remain private; Prescott Park and the Piscataqua waterfront adjoin the campus along Marcy Street, and the South End beyond the gates is a living neighborhood, not part of the ticket.

## 2. The story

The ground is Abenaki ground first. The Museum's land acknowledgement states it plainly: the Museum is on the homelands of the Abenaki people, who have ongoing cultural and spiritual connections to this area. Its archaeologists have uncovered pottery, stone tools, and tent post holes on the site, and its history page records that for over 12,000 years the Abenaki visited the Seacoast seasonally for hunting, fishing, and food preparation.

English settlement followed the water. The Museum's history page dates the first settlement to 1623, when English colonists built along the Piscataqua River and the sheltered cove that is now Portsmouth Harbor, naming the settlement "Strawbery Banke," likely for the wild strawberries along the riverbanks. The National Register nomination traces the district's origin to 1630 and records that in that year or shortly thereafter the settlers built a communal dwelling known as the "Great House" approximately on the site of a present-day museum building, choosing the spot for its closeness to the Creek — the tidal inlet later called Puddle Dock — a sheltered haven for small vessels, and to the Great Field, naturally cleared land for immediate agriculture. The two datings stand side by side in §6.

For its first century the neighborhood stayed in few hands: the nomination records that the area remained with two or three landowners until about 1695, when they began laying out small houselots and the narrow streets that still grid the district. The oldest surviving house belongs to that laying-out: the Capt. John Sherburne House, which the nomination dates to c. 1695 and the Museum describes as built by John Sherburne between 1695 and 1698, the year of his death — a two-story, single-cell, chimney-bay house, and one of the last three wood-framed houses from the 1600s still standing in New Hampshire. Its early eighteenth-century household included John's widow Mary, their son Joseph and his family, and an enslaved man and woman whose names are unrecorded — a fact the Museum prints on the house's own page, as it does for other households across the neighborhood.

Through the eighteenth century Puddle Dock was a working port in miniature. The north–south streets terminated at public wharves on the inlet; cargoes moved from its wharves to large vessels moored in the main river by small boats that could enter the shallow tidal waterway. Merchants' houses and mariners' cottages rose side by side: the Chase House of 1762, among the grandest Georgian structures in the neighborhood; the William Pitt Tavern of 1766, whose long room hosted New Hampshire's first dramatic and operatic performances and the founding of the Grand Lodge of New Hampshire on July 8, 1789; and modest four-room houses for laborers and mariners.

The nineteenth century changed the neighborhood's people more than its fabric. As Portsmouth's maritime economy faded and prosperous families moved to newer streets, immigrants moved into the houses around the inlet: by mid-century a number of Irish families lived in the district, and by 1900, the nomination records, landowners were predominantly Jewish. The Shapiro House carries that chapter in furnished form: a house built in 1795, interpreted to 1919, home to Sheva and Avrum Shapiro and their daughter Mollie — Jewish immigrants from Anapol in Ukraine who reached Portsmouth by chain migration, five brothers and their families here by 1904, and helped found and lead Temple Israel. The inlet itself was filled by the city about 1899, after larger wharves on the main river had taken its trade and it had come to be regarded as a health hazard; tenements and junk dealers' sheds rose on the filled land, and by the 1930s much of the old ground had become scrap-metal yards.

Preservation was proposed once and shelved: in 1935 the Works Progress Administration proposed restoring the district's early houses and removing the tenements, but the program never passed the planning stage. When the end came, it came as clearance — in the mid-1950s the Portsmouth Housing Authority planned to demolish all the buildings in the Puddle Dock area to build garden-type apartments. The Museum records that in 1957 local advocates made the threatened Sherburne House the focal point of the public effort to save the neighborhood. Portsmouth's librarian and antiquarian Dorothy M. Vaughan led the citizens who opposed the Housing Authority — and then joined forces with it: Housing Authority officials eventually favored the preservation idea, the two groups won tentative federal approval in June 1958, and the citizens incorporated as Strawbery Banke, Inc., on November 12, 1958. The machinery followed: state zoning legislation in March 1959 permitting cities to preserve old buildings in a renewal area; final federal approval of the Marcy–Washington Streets Urban Renewal Project (NH-R-1) on July 23, 1960; $800,000 to the Housing Authority to acquire the properties; clearance by 1964; and title passing to Strawbery Banke, Inc., in September 1964. The architect William Graves Perry divided the site into the "Historic Area," where everything preserved was to have historic precedent, and an "Area to Receive Historic Buildings from Without," for Portsmouth structures moved in for their preservation. By 1965, in the Museum's own summary, the site was opened to the public as an outdoor living history museum, with only a handful of the historic houses open. The district was entered in the National Register on June 20, 1975, on a nomination by James L. Garvin, the Museum's research curator, which judged it one of the first districts in the nation where urban renewal was adapted toward preservation instead of demolition.

## 3. Then / now

Then, Puddle Dock was a neighborhood, not an exhibit: a tidal inlet ringed with wharves, merchants' Georgian houses and four-room mariners' cottages on streets as narrow as eight feet, and, after the inlet was filled about 1899, tenements, junk yards, and a corner store serving a crowded immigrant quarter. Its people changed — English settlers, merchant and mariner families, Irish households, Jewish landowners and shopkeepers, a Black family in a Washington Street third of a house in the 1930s — while its houses, mostly, stayed where they had been built.

Now, the same streets are a museum campus, and the change of use is the thing to read honestly. The houses a visitor enters are real survivors standing where the nomination found them in 1975, but they are furnished to chosen years — 1777 at the Pitt Tavern, 1818 at the Chase House, 1870 at the Goodwin Mansion, 1919 at the Shapiro House, 1943 at the Abbott Store, the 1950s in half of the Shapley-Pridham House — so the neighborhood reads as all its centuries at once, not as any single year. The filled inlet is the campus's open green: the 3.2 acres the nomination counts as the old waterway's footprint, excavated in 1966 and 1968 and proven one of the richest historic archaeology sites in northern New England. Around the fence, the South End remains lived in, and the district's private houses are homes, not exhibits.

## 4. What's still there

The district the nomination measured holds approximately 12.08 acres, a grid of five east–west and six north–south streets, with 34 dwelling houses, some five auxiliary structures, eight privately owned houses, and a total building count of 50; the median date of its houses was 1792. With two brick exceptions the houses are braced-frame wood construction, most of them two and a half stories, set directly on the street edge as they were built. Washington and Jefferson Streets keep sidewalks of Durham flagstone, quarried eleven miles upriver and laid in the years just before 1800. Because the district lay just south of the ground burned in Portsmouth's great fire of 1813, its eighteenth-century street plan and wood-frame stock survived where the rebuilt center of the city turned to brick.

The houses the Museum opens carry the whole span. Oldest is the Sherburne House (1695–1698, with its c. 1703 eastern portion), an exhibition of seventeenth-century house construction. The Chase House (1762) stands on its corner of Court and Washington as the neighborhood's grand Georgian merchant house. The William Pitt Tavern (1766), on its original Court Street ground, is interpreted to 1777. The Wheelwright House (1784) runs hearth-cooking demonstrations; the Rider-Wood House (1800), home of English immigrants John and Mary Rider, is furnished to the 1830s. The Shapiro House (1795, to 1919) and the Abbott House and Store (1720, to 1943) carry the immigrant and home-front centuries; the Thomas Bailey Aldrich Memorial (c. 1797), the boyhood home Aldrich set in *The Story of a Bad Boy*, was restored as a memorial in 1908 — among the first houses in America so preserved — and reopened by the Museum in the 1990s to its 1908 appearance.

The moved buildings stand apart, and the Museum's own house list says which they are. The Governor Goodwin Mansion (built 1811), home of Ichabod Goodwin, New Hampshire's governor at the opening of the Civil War, was moved from Islington Street by the State in 1963 to prevent its destruction, and deeded to Strawbery Banke in 1970; it is interpreted to 1870, its garden recreated from Sarah Parker Rice Goodwin's journal. Stoodley's Tavern (1761) was moved from Daniel Street in 1966 ahead of a federal building and post office, and now serves as the Museum's education center. The Dinsmore Shop (c. 1800), the cooper's demonstration shop, was moved to the site in 1970. Two further moves happened inside the neighborhood's own ground: the Penhallow-Cousins House (c. 1750) was moved to its present Washington Street location from nearby in 1862 and cut into a multi-family home, and the Walsh House (c. 1796) was shifted about 80 feet north onto a new foundation in 1969. Everything else a visitor enters stands where it was built.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Enter on Hancock Street and take the neighborhood as the Museum lays it out: streets first, houses second. Begin at the filled inlet — the open green at the campus's center — and read it as water: the wharves stood where the north–south streets end, and the houses near it faced the channel.

Take the seventeenth century at the Sherburne House, where the exhibition is the building itself — its frame, its chimney bay, and the c. 1703 parlor that carried Portsmouth's first Palladian cornice. Then work the merchant century: the Pitt Tavern for 1777 and the Chase House for the Georgian scale of the West India trade. Cross to the working houses — Wheelwright for the hearth, Rider-Wood for an immigrant widow's shopkeeping household, the Cotton Tenant Houses for the last building generation and its looms.

Then take the centuries most museums would have demolished: the Shapiro House furnished to 1919, the Abbott Store to 1943, and the Shapley-Pridham House split between the 1790s and the 1950s, the decade the bulldozers were stopped. Finish with the moved buildings, read as what they are: the Goodwin Mansion and Stoodley's Tavern on the ground set aside to receive rescued Portsmouth buildings, honestly labeled by the Museum's own list. If time remains, the Jones House's "People of the Dawnland" exhibition returns the walk to the ground's first people, and the gardens behind the houses close the circuit.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The first settlement of Portsmouth is dated two ways by the two primary authorities this dossier rests on. The Museum's own history page states that in 1623 English colonists established the settlement and named it Strawbery Banke. The National Register nomination traces the district's origin to 1630 and places the Great House "in that year or shortly thereafter." The discrepancy is printed unresolved: §2 gives both, and no founding date is asserted beyond what each source says.

The size of the ground is likewise stated two ways. The nomination fixes the historic district at approximately 12.08 acres, including streets and privately owned houses; the Museum describes its campus as "nearly 10 acres."

Building counts vary across the Museum's own publications and the nomination. The nomination counts 50 buildings in the district (34 dwellings, five auxiliary structures, eight private houses with ancillaries). Museum text of an earlier generation states that 32 buildings are on their original sites and four were moved to the site; the nomination records five buildings moved into the receiving area, and the Museum's current house list names three moved in from elsewhere in Portsmouth (Goodwin Mansion, 1963; Stoodley's Tavern, 1966; Dinsmore Shop, 1970), plus two moved within the neighborhood (Penhallow-Cousins, 1862; Walsh, 1969). The current Museum pages say only that most historic houses stand on their original sites. No single present-day count is asserted here; the moved/original line is stated per building, where the record states it.

The Sherburne House's date is a range in every primary source, printed here as one: the Museum gives construction between 1695 and 1698 by John Sherburne; the nomination calls it the Capt. John Sherburne House of c. 1695, with a 1695 portion and an eastern portion of c. 1703. No single year is asserted for the whole house.

The Register record itself spells the district two ways: the nomination's name is "Strawbery Banke Historic District," while the National Park Service's asset record titles it "Strawberry Banke Historic District." The nomination's spelling governs here.

The nomination's geographical data gives the district by UTM corner references in Zone 19, but the digits are not legible in the digitized copy consulted, and no other primary record fetched carries a point scoped to this property. No coordinates are therefore printed in §10; the points circulating on aggregator and map sites were not used. Finally, the nomination records Historic American Buildings Survey coverage of individual district houses (a 1934 survey; a July 1962 HABS "Photo-Data Project" covering many of the numbered houses), but no single HABS survey number for the district was verified in this pass, and none is claimed in §10.

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** Lee Wright's 2013 panorama across the museum green at Strawbery Banke, Portsmouth. The frame is a wide, low sweep of the campus taken from across the filled Puddle Dock inlet: a broad band of green lawn fills the foreground and middle distance — the museum green on the old waterway's footprint — and the historic houses are ranged along its far edge in a near-continuous street wall, gabled and chimneyed. The red Jones House stands prominent at the right, the house the Museum now uses for its "People of the Dawnland" exhibition; the remaining houses of the museum's northwestern portion carry the line to the left under a pale sky. The frame shows the museum as its makers made it: not a single monument but a neighborhood, read whole across its own green.
