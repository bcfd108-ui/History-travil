## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A trading post needed people who could feed travelers, build with earth and make different languages understandable. Their labor matters more to this stop than the silhouette of the walls.

**Named community record, honestly mediated:** NPS's Cook's Quarters interpretation identifies **Charlotte Green as enslaved**, rather than presenting her only as a celebrated cook. It quotes William Boggs's memory of another cook, “Old Bill,” from his 1844 stay, and Lewis Garrard's recollection of Charlotte as a source of news. These are outsiders' records about workers, not Charlotte's own surviving testimony. The room attribution itself is interpretive: the page calls the room's proximity to dining and kitchen areas a reason it is a plausible cook's space. Do not convert that reconstruction into a proven bedroom or Charlotte's personal possessions. [4]

**Named firsthand observation of skilled work:** NPS's Languages of the Fur Trade article transmits **Lieutenant James Abert's 1845 observation of interpreter William Guerrier**, whose long experience enabled communication with Cheyenne speakers. It also quotes **Lewis Garrard in 1846** describing interpreter **John Smith** as “adept in the knowledge of Cheyenne tongue.” That nine-word fragment can be read aloud with Garrard's name attached. The article cautions that Native listeners sometimes considered such interpretation poor. This is firsthand observer testimony quoted through NPS, not the original journal examined here and not the interpreters' self-assessment. [5]

**Tribal-published account:** The **Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes' own Language and Culture page** describes hide preparation and trade near and at Bent's Old Fort in the 1830s. Its separate Cheyenne and Arapaho history sections make the communities' continuing identities visible, while its current language program emphasizes preservation of both languages for future generations. This is a governmental community account, not an outside park writer's paraphrase presented as Tribal speech. [6]

Do not equate the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes with every Cheyenne or Arapaho government, including Northern Cheyenne or Northern Arapaho. Nor do their accounts represent Comanche, Kiowa, Ute or all Mexican workers. A multinational commercial setting is not evidence of equal power, free labor or universal agreement.

**Interpretive synthesis:** Trade depended on knowledge and work that a merchant-centered story can make invisible. The asymmetry in surviving records is itself part of the visit: named travelers get quoted; Charlotte is described by them. A responsible account does not repair that imbalance by inventing her words.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** written recollections preserve glimpses of cooking, news-sharing and interpreting, through observers with their own assumptions. [4,5]

**Now:** visitors encounter a twentieth-century reconstruction. NPS distinguishes the 1975–76 building project from authentic surviving nineteenth-century fabric. Its construction and present restrictions have their own history. An old-looking wall is not automatically original evidence. [1]

The useful comparison is between records and the choices made to interpret them, not a then/now photograph allegedly taken from the same spot. The Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes' continuing language work also prevents a false ending in which the communities disappear when the trading post closes. [6]

## 4. What's still there
- A separately identified public Information Center and a restricted reconstruction, with different access conditions. [1]
- The central plaza is a conditional public interpretation area, not blanket room access. NPS's plaza page places public restrooms and drinking water at the visitor parking area, not inside the restricted fort. [7]
- The online room and language interpretations remain readable even when physical access is unavailable. Neither page establishes a specific worker's original possessions on display today. [4,5]

No claim is made that a furnished space is currently accessible without a guide or that any particular tour includes the Cook's Quarters. No collecting, physical examination of historic fabric or exploration beyond open public areas.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
A suggested interpretive sequence, not a route or reservation:
1. **Begin at the public information facility, if open.** Establish today's permitted scope before building a plan around the reconstruction.
2. **Read the labor layer.** Put Charlotte Green and the named interpreters into the story before the merchant names. Identify whose testimony each passage actually preserves.
3. **Only if plaza access is scheduled and open:** compare the reconstruction's public interpretation with the documentary account. Closed rooms remain closed; do not seek an alternate entry.
4. **Read the Tribal account alongside it.** Notice that language is not only a nineteenth-century trading tool but a continuing community responsibility.
5. **Strongest ending, quiet reflection:** ask whose work made exchange possible and whose own words the surviving record withholds. End without reenactment or a scored challenge concerning enslavement or loss.

Optional question at ordinary public interpretation, if staffed: “Which details about workers come from contemporary records, and which are reconstruction choices?” No contact or special program request has been made.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Detailed winter hours came from NPS's indexed dynamic listing; direct text displayed the restriction notice but not the full hours table. No specific future plaza date was extracted. [2,3]
- The indexed replica schedule lists Tuesday/Wednesday closure, but also carried a relative “closed today” label. A crawler-relative label is not reliable evidence for November 19. Do not silently turn it into today's status.
- Original construction dates vary even across NPS summaries: one says built in 1833; the status page describes a start in 1833 and probable completion in spring 1835. Present the original as an early-1830s project unless a detailed chronology is separately resolved. [1,8]
- No Charlotte Green first-person account was located in this bounded pass. Do not call Garrard's recollection her voice.
- Abert and Garrard are read through modern NPS quotations, not original journal pages. No recording was played and no complete archival manuscript verified.
- Specific current exhibit contents, weather, entrance charge and individual accessibility needs remain trip-day checks. No unrestricted reopening date is established.
