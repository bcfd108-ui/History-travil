# Mississippi Governor’s Mansion and the work of keeping a public house

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March OP10 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

**Short exterior stop, with separately confirmed interior tours.** The public Capitol Street sidewalk at 316 East Capitol Street is the ordinary stop. MDAH lists reserved historic-interior tours Tuesday–Thursday at 9:30, 10 and 10:30 a.m. Requests must be made at least 72 hours ahead and confirmed by the curator. The mansion closes for tours Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year weeks. On November 21, 2026, the Thanksgiving closure rules out the three regular dates in the coming week. Follow signs, any temporary sidewalk closure and operator instructions. [1–3]

## 2. Story

The mansion was a state building before it was anyone’s home. Mississippi’s architectural inventory credits state architect William Nichols and gives 1839–42 as the construction span. Governor Tilghman Tucker and his family became its first occupants. Nichols also designed the Old Capitol, a separate public building. The two commissions gave a young state distinct settings for lawmaking and executive residence. The surviving HABS data sheet identifies brick walls and foundation, wood floors, and plaster inside. On the old form the architect’s entry is corrected to William Nichols and the builder field is empty. It is a record of the work as much as of the names that dropped out of the record. [2–5]

This was an occupied house whose exterior changed. The secretary of state’s published account says later yellow pressed brick was used to match the two-story annex and that it was painted white during Governor Paul B. Johnson Sr.’s term. In 1971 an inspection found the building unsafe for occupancy and Governor John Bell Williams left. Under Governor William Waller, public money supported a major restoration. Charlotte Capers coordinated the project and brought in architectural historian Charles E. Peterson and interiors specialist Edward Vason Jones. Lewis-Eaton Partnership worked as project architect. The old annex was replaced and the project was dedicated June 8, 1975. [3,6]

Waller’s Executive Order 122 describes the building as a “historical site, a cultural heritage and the Governor’s residence.” The order puts furniture, art, gifts and records alongside building work, giving those responsibilities to a newly created Fine Arts Commission working with Archives and History. It shows the choices involved in preserving a lived-in building: whose furnishings to accept, what their histories were, and which sections could serve an active household. Capers and the restoration specialists made decisions that still shape what a visitor sees. The contemporaneous order establishes the policy. The later Blue Book supplies the sequence and named project staff. [6–7]

## 3. Then and now

Read the historic house and the replacement family annex as parts of different building campaigns. The formal front and portico provide the exterior comparison with the measured HABS drawings. The restoration did not turn the whole site into a museum. It remains an active residence with an interpreted historic interior. The sidewalk view conveys the outward form of a state building. Interior furnishings and room arrangements require a confirmed tour. [1–6]

## 5. Public progression

In daylight, use the open Capitol Street sidewalk and compare the street-facing portico with the HABS record. A confirmed historic-house tour can supply the indoor continuation. **Story landing — A house the state had to make livable again.** The historic front and the records of 1970s reconstruction make visible the compromise between preservation and a working residence. [1–7]

## 6. Uncertainty and disputes

MDAH identifies an 1842 completion and occupancy. The old HABS form gives 1842–44 and the catalog descriptors give 1844. Use MDAH for the visitor chronology and retain the HABS discrepancy. The sources consulted do not establish the names and status of the initial builders, particular members of the domestic workforce, or the exact version of every historic finish. The 1936 HABS photograph is verified below. A current matched image and public modern camera point remain open. [2–7]
