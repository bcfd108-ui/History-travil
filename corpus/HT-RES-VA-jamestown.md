# HT-RES-VA-jamestown — The fort everyone said the river had taken, found again beside its own church tower
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A low island in the James River where 104 English settlers built a triangular wooden fort in 1607, where an elected assembly first met in a church in 1619, and where the fort itself was believed drowned for two centuries — until archaeologists dug beside the one brick tower still standing and found it in the ground.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is an island, not a single building: Historic Jamestowne, on Jamestown Island in the James River, James City County, Virginia — the original site of James Fort and the 17th-century town of Jamestown, jointly administered by the National Park Service and Preservation Virginia (through its Jamestown Rediscovery project) as part of Colonial National Historical Park. Two operators means two admissions, and both operators' own pages state the terms the same way. The Historic Jamestowne visit guide states: general admission is $15.00 per adult (ages 16 & older) for the National Park Service portion and $15.00 per adult for the Jamestown Rediscovery (Preservation Virginia) portion; children ages 15 & under are free at the National Park Service portion, children ages 6–15 are $5 at the Preservation Virginia portion, and children 5 and under are free site-wide. The NPS fees page states the same split from its side: the standard entrance pass is $15.00, with an additional $15 fee required to access Preservation Virginia resources, which that page names as the James Fort Site and the Archaearium. The visit guide states the hours: Entrance Gate 8:45am–4:30pm; Visitor Center Ticketing 9:00am–4:30pm; Memorial Church 9:00am–5:00pm; Archaearium and Museum Store 9:30am–5:00pm; Island Loop Drive 8:45am–sunset. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966, as Jamestown National Historic Site (NRHP reference no. 66000840, per the NPS's own National Register record). This entry is not Jamestown Settlement, the separate state living-history museum nearby; nothing from that site is used here.
## 2. The story
In June 1606, King James I granted a charter to a group of London entrepreneurs, the Virginia Company, to establish an English settlement in the Chesapeake region. In December of that year, 104 settlers sailed from London with Company instructions to build a secure settlement, find gold, and seek a water route to the Pacific. The NPS's short history gives the sailing date as December 6, 1606, on three ships — the *Susan Constant*, the *Godspeed*, and the *Discovery*.

The settlers first made landfall at Cape Henry, at the mouth of the Chesapeake Bay, in April 1607, then traveled up the James River to an island some 60 miles from the bay's mouth and established the settlement named for their king. Historic Jamestowne's own history page dates the landing on Jamestown Island to **May 14, 1607**; the NPS short history says the settlers picked Jamestown on **May 13** — the one-day disagreement is printed, not resolved, in §6 below.

The island met the Virginia Company's own criteria: it was defensible, deep water lay close to shore, and Spanish ships could not fire point-blank into a fort built there — the point Historic Jamestowne's archaeology has since confirmed about the fort's siting. Within days of landing, the colonists were attacked by Powhatan Indians, and the newcomers spent the next few weeks, in the contemporary phrase the site's history preserves, working to *"beare and plant palisadoes"* for a wooden fort. The NPS short history dates the fort's completion to June 15, 1607: triangle-shaped, with a bulwark at each corner holding four or five pieces of artillery. Three contemporary accounts and a sketch agree, in Historic Jamestowne's telling, that the walls formed a triangle around a storehouse, a church, and a number of houses.

The fort burned accidentally in 1608 and was rebuilt. The winter of 1609–10 — the Starving Time — nearly ended the colony: Historic Jamestowne's history records that about 300 settlers were crowded into James Fort when the Powhatan set up a siege, and only 60 survived to the next spring. What saved the enterprise commercially was tobacco, the long-awaited cash crop the Virginia Company had wanted from its investment.

On **July 30, 1619**, the first representative assembly in English North America convened in the Jamestown church, in response to Virginia Company orders *"to establish one equal and uniform government over all Virginia"* and to provide *"just laws for the happy guiding and governing of the people there inhabiting."* A few weeks later came the first recorded arrival of Africans in English America — Historic Jamestowne's history marks it as the beginning of *de facto* slavery in the colony. The NPS's park overview also names Jamestown as the focal point of Bacon's Rebellion in 1676, when the town — including its church — was burned.

As the colony matured in the 1620s, residents began moving slightly east of the old three-sided fort into "New Towne," where, in the NPS Colonial overview's description, colonists constructed a statehouse, church, roadway, workshops, dwellings, warehouses, wharves, and taverns, and Jamestown took the shape of an established town. Jamestown served as the seat of colonial government in Virginia for 92 years, from 1607 until the capital moved to Williamsburg in 1699, after the statehouse burned in 1698. Written references to the original fort then disappeared as the town faded.

What happened next is the entry's second story, and it is an argument about evidence. By the later 19th century, James River erosion had eaten away the island's western shore, and the common belief — stated plainly in Historic Jamestowne's own history — was that the site of 1607 James Fort lay completely underwater. The one piece of the 17th-century town still standing above ground was the brick church tower, which the NPS Colonial overview identifies as the only original 17th-century structure still at Jamestown.

William Kelso, who had visited Jamestown in 1963 as a graduate student at the College of William & Mary, doubted the drowned-fort account. His reasoning, as Jamestown Rediscovery's own project history tells it, ran through the church: colony secretary William Strachey had written that a "pretty chapel" stood in the midst of the fort, and on the principle of once sacred ground, always sacred ground, Kelso theorized that a later church would have been built in the same place — near the fort's center — making the standing brick tower his primary evidence for where the fort had been. Soil stains found by NPS archaeologists near a Civil War earthwork by the tower, suggesting palisade remnants, pointed the same way. After ten years of negotiation with Preservation Virginia, which owned the land, Kelso launched the Jamestown Rediscovery project in **1994**, aiming to find the earliest fortified town and share the discovery in time — the team hoped — for Jamestown's 400th anniversary in 2007. Kelso began excavations on **April 4, 1994**, digging between the church tower and the James River. Within three archaeological seasons, the team had proved that the remains of James Fort existed on dry land near the church tower. The fort had not drowned. It had been waiting under the grass, next to the one building that had never left.

The island's modern keeping is shared. Historic Jamestowne's history records that the remaining acreage was acquired by the National Park Service in 1934 and made part of Colonial National Historical Park, and that today Jamestown is jointly operated by Preservation Virginia and the NPS. The National Register listing followed on October 15, 1966.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a triangular wooden fort of palisades, bulwarks, a storehouse, a church, and houses, completed by June 15, 1607, on a low island in the James River. **Now:** the fort's lines recovered by archaeology since 1994 — postholes, palisade traces, and foundations in open ground beside the church tower, on the James Fort Site the Preservation Virginia admission covers.

**Then:** a town that grew east of the fort into New Towne, with a statehouse, workshops, dwellings, warehouses, wharves, and taverns, serving as Virginia's capital for 92 years. **Now:** ruins and archaeological remains along the New Towne walking tour — the once-bustling town reduced to foundations in open ground.

**Then:** the fort believed, for roughly two centuries, to lie completely underwater, eaten by the river. **Now:** the fort proven on dry land by the Jamestown Rediscovery excavations begun April 4, 1994 — the older assumption and the newer evidence are both part of what the visitor is standing on, and §6 keeps them separate.

**Then:** a brick church tower standing alone after the town faded. **Now:** the same tower, the only original 17th-century structure still there in the NPS overview's words, with the Memorial Church of 1907 behind it, built over the foundations of the earlier churches.

## 4. What's still there
- The James Fort site itself, excavated since 1994: palisade lines, foundations, and archaeological features in open ground between the church tower and the James River (Jamestown Rediscovery project history)
- The 17th-century brick church tower — the only original 17th-century structure still at Jamestown, per the NPS Colonial overview
- The Memorial Church (1907), which the NPS park page states sits on the very site of the 1617 church — the ground where the General Assembly convened on July 30, 1619
- The Tercentenary Monument: a 103-foot shaft of New Hampshire granite erected by the United States in 1907 for the settlement's 300th anniversary (NPS Historical Handbook: Jamestown)
- The New Towne ruins and archaeological remains — statehouse, dwellings, workshops, and taverns — along the New Towne walking tour (NPS Colonial overview)
- The Archaearium, the archaeology museum holding the Jamestown Rediscovery finds (NPS fees page; hours on the visit guide)
- The 1608 Glasshouse and the Island Loop Drive, both listed with hours on the Historic Jamestowne visit guide

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Visitor Center:** Start where both operators meet the visitor — ticketing for both portions is sold here (9:00am–4:30pm, per the visit guide), and the split admission is settled before the ground is.
2. **At the church ground:** The brick tower and the Memorial Church behind it — the tower Kelso used as his evidence, and the church site where the assembly of July 30, 1619, convened.
3. **On the fort ground:** The James Fort Site between the church tower and the river, where the April 4, 1994, excavations began and the palisade lines were found in the soil.
4. **In New Towne:** East of the fort, along the walking tour of ruins and remains — the town Jamestown became after the fort.
5. **At the Archaearium:** The finds themselves — the museum the Preservation Virginia portion of admission covers, where the archaeology that reversed the drowned-fort story is the exhibit.
6. **Around the island:** The Island Loop Drive, for the river, the marsh, and the reason the island was chosen — and nearly lost — in the first place.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The landing date disagrees by one day between the site's two operators, and both versions print. Historic Jamestowne's history page states that the Virginia Company settlers landed on Jamestown Island on **May 14, 1607**. The National Park Service's short history states that on **May 13** the settlers picked Jamestown for their settlement, and the NPS Colonial overview likewise puts the move up the James River by May 13, 1607. This draft prints both dates as their sources carry them and does not reconcile them.

The head counts also differ across the operators' pages, and the difference is printed rather than smoothed. The NPS Colonial overview states that 144 Englishmen made landfall at Cape Henry in April 1607. Historic Jamestowne's history and the NPS short history both put the sailing and arriving party at 104 settlers. The figures may count different legs of the same voyage — landfall party against the full company — but neither source consulted explains the difference, so none is invented here.

The church tower's own building history is carried differently by two federal documentation projects, and both are printed. The NPS Historical Handbook for Jamestown states that the Old Church Tower is believed to have been part of the first brick church built about 1639. The Carnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South's inventory notes for Frances Benjamin Johnston's Jamestown Church photographs state that the Old Church Tower belongs to the fourth church, that this church was burned in 1676 by Nathaniel Bacon and repaired in 1686, and that the present building behind the tower was erected in 1907. The two accounts are not reconciled in the sources consulted; the visitor sees one tower either way, but which church it belonged to is a live documentation difference, not a settled fact this draft will pick between.

Older secondary tellings of Jamestown sometimes repeat claims the current operator sources do not carry — among them precise details of the 1619 African arrivals and of individual settlers' actions in the fort's first years. Where Historic Jamestowne's and the NPS's current pages state a fact, this draft uses it; where they do not, the claim is left out rather than repeated on older authority. The Rediscovery findings have limits this draft respects: the project history states that within three seasons the team had proved the fort's remains existed on dry land near the church tower; it does not state that the whole fort survives, and no percentage of survival is printed here.

The NPS National Register record for Jamestown National Historic Site (66000840) carries no latitude or longitude. The coordinates printed in §10 come instead from a separate NPS record — the NPGallery photo asset record for views of the site — and are scoped there as that record scopes them; they are not taken from any lead-list source.
