## 2. The story
**Original interpretive frame:** A cattle town was a transfer system, not just a scene for gunfighters. Herds reached a market; freight workers kept the surrounding economy supplied; railway connections carried goods onward. The useful story is the labor between those stages, not a reenactment of violence.

Robert M. Wright's *Dodge City, the Cowboy Capital* is a participant-merchant's retrospective account, published in the 1913 second edition. Its cattle chapter connects incoming herds with sale and transportation through the Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe town. His phrase **“all they were out were the men's wages”** exposes the employer's viewpoint: wages appear as a cost, while workers largely disappear from his account. This is primary evidence of Wright's perspective, not an impartial measure of conditions or profit. [3–4]

Named workers emerge in Ida Ellen Rath's 1961 biography: **Fred Bond**, foreman of Charles Rath's railway-contract work, and **Joseph Minor**, taking charge of the bull train between Dodge City and the Panhandle in the passage dated March 29, 1881. These are ox-freight operations, not passenger trains. Charles Rath is the contractor/operator, not a substitute for the workers' voices. The chapter also describes return freight and cites the *Ford County Globe*. It is a later compilation incorporating historical newspaper material; original newspaper images and Bond's or Minor's own testimony were not read. [5–6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Suggested single-anchor visit, **45–75 minutes as an editorial estimate**, not an operator duration:
1. Read the short Wright and Rath context before arrival, or at home if the museum is closed.
2. Enter through museum admission. Orient to the distinction between collection, reconstruction and historic location.
3. Within open public exhibits, consider how merchandise and transportation supported cattle commerce. Do not promise a named worker panel or particular artifact.
4. End with the strongest story moment: put **Bond and Minor's names beside Wright's wage-cost sentence**. The town's prosperity depended on work that its celebratory histories often reduced to an expense. This is an interpretive comparison, not a quiz or a claim that the museum makes this argument.

No scavenging at graves, no violence scoring, no reenactment requirement. Any encounter with burial or death interpretation is reflection-only. No outdoor detour is necessary to complete the visit.
