# Split Rock Lighthouse — Fuel, supplies and the work of keeping a light
Research draft · Minnesota OP01 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

**Getting supplies up the cliff.** Clarence Young, son of keeper Orren P. Young, remembered that larger boats could approach the old hoist “only when the water was perfectly calm.” This recollection is reproduced from the Quinn Evans report in a University of Maryland thesis. The original interview was not retrieved. The same thesis describes how the hoist made the delivery crew dependent on water and weather and why the district pursued a tramway. There is a second record of the labor in the first keeper’s own account book. The MNHS finding aid identifies an 1910–1913 volume covering oil, wicks, chimneys and how long the light burned each night. The ledger pages were not accessible online in the collection records consulted. [3–4]

## 3 · Then / now

**Then — from hoist to beam.** The lighthouse service built a station that had to operate seasonally even when its shore access was difficult. Construction followed the 1905 storm and congressional appropriation in 1907. The 1910 light used a third-order Fresnel lens and a pressurized-kerosene lamp. Fuel, deliveries and staff were the physical conditions of keeping a regular signal. MNHS records later electrification, retirement of the fog signal in 1961 and station decommissioning in 1969. [3–7]

**Now — a complete station with divided public access.** The historic tower, fog signal building and men’s working and residential landscape survive under operator interpretation. MNHS describes the present beacon as an event or maintenance light, outside active navigation. The historic-site pass, public program windows and DNR state park access each have separate terms. [1–2,5–9]

## 4 · What’s still there

- **Exterior only during open grounds admission.** Tower exterior and views of historic station buildings from signed paths. This stop uses the short ordinary visitor approach. [1,9]
- **Public interior when the applicable ticket or tour permits it.** Lighthouse and house access varies with the posted program. Visitor center and fog-signal interpretation follow site hours and operator limits. [1,5]
- **Not accessible in this progression.** Cliff edges, historic hoist ground, other closed places, and exposure on ice or rough shoreline. A navigation-aid visit is separate from remembrance of the loss of the Edmund Fitzgerald and other ships. [1–2,6,9]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Historic location.** The Split Rock light station with its cliff-top working compound. The historical loading point was part of the original supply system. [3–4,7]
2. **Lawful anchor.** Use the signed public MNHS visitor approach. Stay on its open grounds and choose an appropriate tour if available. The ordinary short grounds path is the route. Read remotely during complete closures or unsafe conditions. [1–2,9]
3. **Strongest story moment — A calm-water delivery.** End where the tower and lake can be seen from the open ordinary paths. Read Clarence Young’s words, then look at the height between the water and the light. [3–5]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

MNHS’s beacon interpretation marks July 31 as first lighting, while its archives finding aid dates the beginning of operation August 1, 1910. The thesis distinguishes switching on the light from the August 16 first fog signal; the first keeper’s daily page is still needed. The finding aid says the station closed in 1968; the timeline gives 1969 decommissioning. The causal or administrative difference remains open. The Coast Guard archive item returned HTTP 403 on web retrieval. The older 1963 aerial PDF request timed out and no rights-bearing copy was obtained. Its record credits St. Paul Dispatch photographer Don Spavin. [3–4,6–8,10–11]
