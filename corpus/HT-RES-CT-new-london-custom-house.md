# HT-RES-CT-new-london-custom-house — New London: where commerce met the struggle for freedom
Division CT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

Built of granite between 1833 and 1835 to Robert Mills's design, New London's Custom House regulated the young nation's maritime trade — and in 1840 it was where the seized cargo of the Amistad was auctioned. The building still stands on Bank Street, now a maritime museum, its Greek Revival front facing the street it served.

U.S. Custom House, 150 Bank Street, New London, New London County, Connecticut

**Public access:** Exterior experience from the public sidewalk along Bank Street. The building houses a maritime museum operated by the New London Maritime Society; museum admission is not required for this entry. Confirm museum hours with the Society if you plan to go inside.

## The story

The Custom House served the federal government's regulation of maritime commerce. Ships arriving in New London brought goods, passengers, and revenue through a port connected to international trade, and the customs service — collector, inspectors, duties — ran through this granite building. Its architect, Robert Mills, is the architect associated with the Washington Monument.

The building also became part of the Amistad affair. After the schooner was seized in 1839 and brought into New London's orbit, its cargo was subsequently auctioned at the Custom House in 1840 — the commerce of the port and the human story of the captives meeting in one room. The captives' own case was decided in the courts; the sale of the cargo is the Custom House's part of the record.

Today the building houses a maritime museum operated by the New London Maritime Society. Its surviving architecture connects visitors to the commercial and human stories of Connecticut's waterfront.

## Then / now

- 1833–1835: Custom House constructed to Robert Mills's design.
- 1839: the Amistad seized and brought to the New London area.
- 1840: the schooner's cargo auctioned at the Custom House.
- Today: maritime museum of the New London Maritime Society.

## What's still there

The granite façade and Greek Revival entrance on Bank Street, the Maritime Society's sign over the portico, and the museum within. The exterior is the stop: federal architecture built for the working waterfront.

## The stop, step by step

Stand on the Bank Street sidewalk facing number 150. Locate the granite façade and the Greek Revival entrance. Read the building as a working federal office of the sailing era — then, if you go in, the museum carries the port's story from there.

## Honest gaps & disagreements

The Amistad association is stated at the level the record supports: the cargo auction of 1840. The captives were held and tried elsewhere in Connecticut and the case decided in Washington; retellings sometimes move those events to this building, and this entry does not. Construction dates are given as the 1833–1835 range the sources support.
