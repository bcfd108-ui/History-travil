# HT-RES — Celilo Falls / Wy-am: the falls submerged, the people remain
Research revision R01 • Checked 2026-11-19 • Research draft for Dash QC; not published or field verified.

## 1. Interpretive proposition
**Strongest moment, editorial recommendation:** read a named tribal witness before looking from Celilo Park toward the reservoir. The visitor should encounter the difference between a place's apparent quiet and its remembered sound, livelihood, and obligations. This is not a hunt for a vanished people, a game on a sacred site, or a debate in which a visitor decides whose identity is authentic.

Public-facing draft line: *The water changed in a day. The relationship to this place did not end.* That sentence is original editorial synthesis, not a tribal quotation.

## 2. Narrative grounded in published sources
Celilo was a salmon fishery, exchange center, and lived community, not simply a waterfall that happened to have spectators. NPS describes fishing along this stretch for at least 10,000 years and links the fishery with a wide trade network. Treat that duration as an institutional interpretive statement rather than a newly established archaeological date. NPS also reports sonar evidence that the submerged horseshoe-shaped rock formation remains intact. It does not authorize boating over it, diving, or artifact searching. [C1]

The Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission describes a regulated communal fishery: elders and chiefs governed fishing, the First Salmon observance preceded the season, and catch supported people beyond the individual fisher. The economics cannot be separated from social duties. Compensation for flooded fishing sites was not a purchase extinguishing treaty fishing rights. Keep the flooded place and continuing rights conceptually separate. [C2]

On March 10, 1957, closure of The Dalles Dam's gates raised the reservoir over the falls. CRITFC's anniversary account gives 10 a.m. for closure and four and a half hours for silencing the falls eight miles upstream. Its focus is the loss of sound and fishery and the continuing people of Celilo Village. Do not render this as an abandoned ghost settlement. [C3]

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers describes continuing year-round and seasonal homes and the cumulative effects of navigation and dam projects. Its tribal-program account records a federal village built in 1948 with inadequate facilities, and redevelopment completed in 2009. The surviving community is not equivalent to the whole pre-inundation settlement; neither “nothing was lost” nor “everyone disappeared” is supportable. [C4]

### Published tribal voices, not invented testimony
CRITFC publishes these named recollections:
- **Geraldine Jim, Warm Springs:** “That was our life.” Her recollection connects the falls with her family fishing there.
- **Chief Johnny Jackson, Yakama River Chief:** “The power of Celilo Falls helped me learn to respect the river.”
These are individual attributed voices in a tribal commission publication, not statements purporting to speak for every tribal citizen. Preserve names and affiliations; do not turn the quotations into app-character dialogue. [C2]

CRITFC's 2016 remembrance expressly says residents still call Celilo Village home and continue cultural practices and connections. That institutional tribal perspective is central evidence of continuity, not an optional emotional sidebar to a dam-engineering narrative. [C3]

### Government narrative and disputed precision
The federal power-system handbook situates The Dalles project in navigation and power production, with construction during 1952–1957. These purposes explain a governmental program; they do not rebut tribal testimony of loss. They answer different historical questions. [C5]

A 2007 Congressional Record commemoration describes the inundation in six hours, whereas CRITFC states four and a half. Do not manufacture a false exact consensus. The shared date is March 10, 1957; interval differences may reflect endpoints or retelling, but that explanation is a hypothesis until compared with contemporaneous operational logs. For public copy use “within hours.” [C3, C6]

## 3. Then / now: evidence and restraint
- **Then:** photographed turbulent fishery and trading place. **Now:** reservoir landscape with a continuing tribal community, not visible exposed falls. Use an accurately dated archival photograph beside the public viewpoint, but never force an exact matching camera angle without survey evidence. [C1–C3]
- **Then:** fishery access submerged. **Now:** treaty/in-lieu access arrangements continue. CRITFC explains that its 31 designated sites serve fishers of the four member tribes; those are not a general tourism admission system. Public recreation park access is a different question. [C7]
- **Then:** a public memorial erected in 1957. **Now:** HMdb documents the Celilo Falls Fishing Grounds tablet near the park parking lot, with photographs from 2017. That verifies a historical inventory, not its condition today. [C8]

## 4. Runnable stop order and strongest on-site moment
**1. Approach Celilo Park in Oregon via I-84 Exit 97.** Use designated parking and posted public routes. NPS lists interpretive information, picnic tables, restrooms, a scenic viewpoint and wheelchair accessibility; no specific present-day route gradient, restroom opening, or unobstructed path has been independently checked. Suggested dwell 30–45 minutes is an editorial planning allowance. [C1]

**2. Begin at the public Celilo Falls Fishing Grounds marker near parking, if present and accessible.** Read what its short commemoration includes and what it cannot convey alone. Then open the published tribal recollections. The memorial does not replace living voices. Do not treat HMdb's nearby-marker distances as walkable connections across the Columbia: entries on the Washington bank are not a short pedestrian extension. [C8]

**3. Move only along an authorized public park path to a safe river view.** Pair the reservoir with one properly credited historic photograph. Listen briefly to the present environment without staging a ceremony or imitating tribal practice. No descent to fishing platforms, rocks, railroad tracks or private access roads. No photographing residents, fishers, homes, ceremonies or equipment as a challenge. This is the story's recommended emotional landing, not a claim to stand on the exact submerged falls.

**4. Leave via the public park route.** The village, longhouse and tribal fishing access are excluded from the route. If a boundary or closure is unclear, stop rather than infer permission from an online map. [C7]

### Current visitor-rule conflict: don't sell stale camping advice
NPS broadly advertises RV/tent camping, but the managing USACE district's current recreation listing restricts camping to March 16–October 31, with Celilo tent camping Friday and Saturday only; it also limits stays. November 19 is outside that season. Plan this as a day-use visit, not overnight lodging. A Corps system row labels Celilo Park “WA,” conflicting with NPS/manager Oregon location; do not use that metadata to route travelers. Manager rules and signs control. Full-page USACE retrieval later returned 403; earlier indexed manager text supplied the restrictions, so recheck current closures before departure. [C1, C9–C10]

## 6. Honest gaps and release gates
- Primary operating logs to resolve inundation timing; broader published tribal oral histories with narrator consent/reuse status.
- Exact pre-dam village and falls map layers, visibly distinguished from public routing; no invented footprints.
- Current marker/panel condition, accessible route, closures, amenities and daylight weather checks.
- No evidence collected here of current permission for community/ceremonial visits; none implied.
- A waterfall's submerged geology, a destroyed fishery, and a living community are three distinct facts. Do not simplify any one into disappearance of the other two.
