## 2. The story
**Del Martin and Phyllis Lyon**, interviewed by **Nan Boyd on December 2, 1992**, provide a firsthand account of organizational labor. The published transcript credits **Genevieve Maxwell** as transcriber. In the discussion around PDF pages 15–17, Lyon identifies Daughters of Bilitis as an organization primarily for lesbians. Later, around pages 47–48, they discuss work done by people reluctant to join formally and the anxiety surrounding membership lists. Lyon's concise remembered reassurance is **“Your name is safe.”** [3]

This is a published adult community account, not an identity inferred from photographs, appearance, neighborhood or archival association. Only selected organizing passages were read; original recording and consent paperwork were not inspected. The institution publicly supplies the transcript for research, which does not authorize broader republication. [3,4]

The story's center is the ordinary work behind a durable community record: making something that people could read, making room for participation, and carrying responsibility for people's trust. These are interpretive themes, not instructions to investigate who appeared on a historical mailing list.

A public archive is valuable partly because so much community work could otherwise vanish. But preservation and disclosure are not identical obligations. The strongest question is what a researcher owes to the people whose records make the story possible. Do not turn historical vulnerability into a list of names to locate today.

When Nan Boyd asked about the first issue of *The Ladder*, Phyllis Lyon answered, “October, 1956.” Del Martin agreed. Elsewhere in the same interview the two could not recall precisely how they first learned about another organization. The difference matters: oral history can give a firm date for a publication and still leave the human links that preceded it uncertain. The Society’s source set places the founding of the Daughters of Bilitis in 1955. The first issue followed the organizing. [3,6]

A decade later, the June 1966 cover featured Ernestine Eckstein, vice president of the New York chapter. Her interview ran inside. Seen beside the concerns about membership lists, the cover asks a different question. Some readers needed privacy to take part at all. Here is a participant represented by name and portrait in a publication made to travel. A visitor can read what Eckstein chose to put into public circulation without trying to identify a single subscriber. Consult the online exhibition if the issue is unavailable in the gallery. [3,7]
