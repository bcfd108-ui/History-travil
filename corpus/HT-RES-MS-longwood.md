# Longwood — who finished the rooms beneath the unfinished house

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP02 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Drive up to the ticketed Longwood tour at 140 Lower Woodville Road, Natchez. The individual listing offers hourly tours from 9 a.m. through 4 p.m. The same seller’s three-house ticket gives 3 p.m. as Longwood’s last start. Choose an earlier confirmed tour or verify the late departure when booking. Stay with the guide in rooms opened that day. Holiday and weather exceptions remain unverified. [1–2]

## 2. Story

In February 1860, before the Philadelphia carpenters arrived, Haller Nutt was planning the removal of an older house and the making of the new house’s bricks. In a letter to architect Samuel Sloan he proposed bringing men to clear the site and start the foundation. A second letter lists what came next, including “cut wood, prepare brick yard.” Those are Nutt’s words as transcribed in the National Park Service’s historical study. The letters preserve his instructions. They do not give the men’s accounts of the work. [3]

Nutt and his wife Julia commissioned an eight-sided house. Their wealth came from plantations in Louisiana and Mississippi where they held hundreds of people in slavery. Sloan sent masons and carpenters south, while a Philadelphia shop supplied millwork. Addison Hutton supervised on site, and George Henkels took the commission for doors and a furnishing scheme. When war broke out in 1861 most of the northern crew left. A tinner subsequently worked on the roof. Enslaved workers laid flooring and plastered walls below, making the part of the building where the family could actually live. Haller died in 1864. The living quarters meant to be temporary remained Julia’s home. [3–4]

## 3. Then and now

The dome makes the house look finished from a distance. Inside, the furnished lower rooms and unfinished structure above preserve two quite different outcomes of the same commission. The dome’s present finial is a 1993 reconstruction. A visitor seeing it today is looking at a later intervention on a nineteenth-century building. [1,4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Guided interior — the operator interprets furnished and unfinished space. Confirm which levels the guide opens. Exterior — the brick house, galleries and dome can be read from the tour approach. Documentary evidence — the surviving service buildings include a carriage house and a building identified as quarters for enslaved workers. Their survival establishes neither interior access nor permission to leave the tour. [1,4]

## 5. Public progression

Begin with the completed outline of the octagon. When the guide brings you to the family’s living rooms, carry the brickyard letter into the room with you. **Strongest story moment:** the finished plaster below the unfinished upper structure. The Philadelphia workers’ departure is visible in the upper floors. The work that made the lower floor habitable has its own history. [1,3–4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The NPS study counts eight rooms in the basement and the ticket operator describes nine on the ground floor. Neither source explains its counting convention. The original letters and an independent account by an enslaved worker were unavailable for inspection. No individual has been assigned a task from an unnamed group. Both last-tour times are printed above. [1–4]
