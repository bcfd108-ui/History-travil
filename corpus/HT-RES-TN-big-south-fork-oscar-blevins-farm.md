# Big South Fork, Tennessee · The unfinished cellar at the Oscar Blevins farm

*On Old Ground · Tennessee · Tier 1 seeding · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
Oscar Blevins stopped building a root cellar when he learned his farm might become part of a national park. The cellar remained unfinished. The National Park Service’s field inventory puts it among the farm’s surviving features, beside evidence of a family that had spent decades adapting its land. The investigators visited in July and December 1997, long after the family had moved. They could record the unfinished stonework. They could not recover every decision that led up to it. [4, 5]

The farm lay on the plateau along Old Leatherwood Ford Road in Fentress County. John “Jack” Blevins built the log house in 1879. When a frame dwelling went up in the 1950s, the family put the old house to work as a barn. A larger frame barn followed in the 1960s. Fields, an orchard, a spring, pasture fencing, a corn crib and the spaces between them all mattered to a household making a living here. The newer buildings are part of the working farm’s history. The change from a house to a barn helps explain why the older structure survived. [4–6]

Oscar and Martha Ermon Blevins lived here with their family. In 2022 the park announced a walk at which their son Lawrence was to speak at the farm about life there. That program identifies a firsthand witness and a promising source. It does not preserve his words. In the 1970s Congress authorized the recreation area. The federal inventory describes the family’s land as condemned for inclusion in the park and dates the move away to 1980. Local reporting says Oscar and Martha resettled in Jamestown. The original deed, acquisition award and family correspondence remain outstanding. [4–7]

The park also preserves evidence of industrial work. On the Tennessee side a section of today’s Rock Creek Loop follows the old Stearns logging railroad along Massey Branch. Across the state line, the Stearns Coal and Lumber Company operated its Blue Heron mines from 1937 to 1962. Former residents’ voices are part of that Kentucky interpretation. Linking those histories makes sense. Sending a traveler to a Tennessee trailhead to find Kentucky’s Mine 18 does not. [1, 2, 8]

## 3. Then and now
Where the family saw a home, farmyard and working fields, a visitor arrives on a blazed recreation trail. A nearby river once considered for damming was ultimately included in the recreation area. That preservation had different consequences for different people. The river remained free-flowing here, and the Blevins family left the farm. Its old house, newer house, outbuildings, field edges and interrupted cellar preserve both changes on the same ground. [4–6, 9]

## 5. Public progression
Use the signed Bandy Creek Trailhead for the Oscar Blevins loop. At the farm, look at the relationship between the log house and the later frame house before looking at the fields. A family adopted newer construction while keeping the older building in use. **The cellar Oscar stopped building** gives that sequence an ending. The documentary record carries this part of the story even when a visitor cannot see the cellar from the trail. Return on the marked loop. The Tennessee hike includes neither the Kentucky mine nor a cemetery visit. [1, 4–6, 8]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The NPS inventory and landscape account describe the 1879 builder as Oscar’s father. A local biographical account names John Blevins as Oscar’s father but calls the builder his uncle. Both sources identify the builder as John “Jack” Blevins. The kinship remains unresolved pending independent family or land records. [4–6]
- The inventory describes condemnation. The local account calls the 1980 transaction a federal purchase. An award or conveyance could clarify the procedure and timing. Neither account establishes the amount paid or the family’s own words about the terms. [4, 6]
- The 2022 program announcement names Lawrence Blevins. No recording or transcript of his remarks was established. No inference about what he said is printed. [7]
- Tier 1 ordering uses **774,353 recreation visits to the entire two-state recreation area in 2025**. Farm-trail attendance and a pair of independently matching trailhead coordinates remain open. [10]
