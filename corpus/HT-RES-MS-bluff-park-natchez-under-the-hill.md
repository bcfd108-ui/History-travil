# Bluff Park and the workers of Natchez Under-the-Hill

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP04 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Public overlook at Bluff Park, 101 South Broadway. Use the upper park. The landings and mill foundations are historical subjects, outside the visitor route. [1]

## 2. Story

From the bluff, the Mississippi can look like the edge of town. For the people who unloaded boats, moved logs and tended the saws, it was the way into work. At Upper Landing, Andrew Brown and later Rufus Learned operated a sawmill. At Middle Landing the Cozzens mill worked through much of the mid-nineteenth century. Enslaved African Americans supplied skilled labor in this river economy. After emancipation Black workers continued to make the lumber trade possible. The names of successive proprietors are much easier to recover than a shift of workers. [2]

## 3. Then and now

A 1999 archaeological investigation allows a more intimate account than a business directory. At Cozzens a brick foundation helped researchers establish how the machinery sat. Objects recovered from a filled well included machine components alongside evidence of food, clothing and medicine. The Upper Landing survey recovered much less material from the operating period. These two sets of findings support different levels of detail. [2]

## 4. Surviving features and access

In April 1972 a federal survey photographed a group of six surviving brick commercial buildings along Silver Street. The upper public park affords a view of the river and the height separating the town from its working waterfront. The 1972 survey cannot establish how many shop fronts are open today. [1,3]

## 5. Public progression

From the park overlook, read the height difference first, then the photograph. The camera was down on Silver Street. The masonry fronts, utility lines, parked cars and bridge truss belong to the 1972 street, after the early lumber trade. [3]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

Nineteenth-century travelers wrote about vice and disorder in the port. Michael Francis Beard traced how their accounts shaped its reputation. They are evidence for what travelers chose to describe. Cramer’s field results answer a different question about where people worked and what sustained them. Individual artifacts cannot securely be assigned to a named worker. [2,4]
