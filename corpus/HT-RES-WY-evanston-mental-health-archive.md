## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** Evanston's hospital history is also a history of work, care and voices that do not always agree. A building can carry community memory without telling us what every person inside experienced.

**Named firsthand worker voice:** in Eric Galatas' Wyoming News Service interview published March 11, 2024, retired employee **Vickie Fruits**, described as having worked there for 37 years, recalled women's work across nursing, food service, housekeeping, social work and medicine. Her short recorded-in-print recollection is: **“We had a dairy and garden. We pretty much supplied all of our own food.”** [3] These are her published words, not a recording heard for this dossier. Her account supplies a worker's perspective on a substantial workplace, not consent to narrate any patient's private experience.

For historical balance, Barbara Allen Bogart's 2014 account contrasts administrators' favorable reports with publicly raised complaints by former patient and journalist **E. T. Payton**. It also discusses patient labor serving institutional economic needs. [4] This dossier does not reproduce diagnoses, case files, graphic allegations or treatment procedures. The newspaper originals and Payton's own full text have not been inspected, so he is identified as a documented countervoice, not presented through an invented firsthand quotation.

**Original interpretive frame:** remembering the skill and commitment of workers need not erase patients' autonomy or criticism. Conversely, acknowledging institutional harm should not turn living people who need care into the subjects of a spectacle. Preserve these distinctions rather than forcing the material into a heroic or haunted-building story.

## 3. Then / now

The Department of Health's historical page records the territorial institution's origins and the 1923 adoption of the Wyoming State Hospital name. [5] That institutional chronology is not evidence of present conditions in every surviving historic building.

The current Department of Health page identifies a **104-bed acute psychiatric inpatient hospital**, with patient care and support services consolidated in a renovated facility. [2] That directly cautions against treating the 2024 news story's “mostly outpatient” description as current operational truth. Historical buildings, current care services and a preservation/reuse proposal are three different subjects.

The museum is another institution entirely: its public building is a former Carnegie library completed in 1906, expanded in 2008. [1] It offers a lawful, nonclinical setting for local-history inquiry. It is not a ward, admission office or patient-records service.

- **Published archival material:** the state historical account credits historical hospital images to the Uinta County Museum and Wyoming State Archives. [4] Custody attribution does not establish walk-in access to originals or reproduction permission.
- **Current healthcare institution, not a stop:** the Department of Health verifies ongoing inpatient services. [2] No patient, staff or facility photography or approach is proposed.
- **Historic-building survival/reuse status unresolved:** the 2024 preservation reporting describes plans and competing views, not completed demolition or an opened reuse project. [3] No current inventory was verified here.
- **Excluded entirely:** wards, service yards, closed buildings, private homes, burial locations and any route requiring healthcare access. No aerial or street-view reconnaissance of those areas is needed for this draft.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

A **single conditional museum visit**, with an archive-only alternative. Pacing is an editorial suggestion, not an operator program.

1. **Before arrival:** establish that the museum is open on the intended day and can offer relevant public interpretation. If not, retain the online reading instead of replacing it with a campus excursion.
2. **Begin with community work:** read Fruits' brief account. Notice how many kinds of labor sit behind the phrase “hospital staff.” Do not assign a worker's perspective to patients.
3. **Ask how the evidence was selected:** if staff can show public photographs or histories, distinguish caption, institutional report, newspaper allegation and personal recollection. Use only materials they make available for general research.
4. **End with the strongest story moment:** hold two ideas together: work remembered with pride, and the unresolved question of whose experience the archive leaves out. The original reflection prompt is: *What would a history of care need to preserve so that the people receiving it are not made invisible?*

Nobody is asked to disclose their own mental-health history. Visitors may stop reading or skip this entry. Quiet reflection completes the encounter; nothing needs to be found or “won.”

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The Department of Health history page says construction was completed in **1887**; Bogart's history describes construction beginning in **1888** and patients arriving in **1889**. [5][4] Do not silently combine these into a precise opening chronology without checking original appropriations and construction records.

The latest clinical operator page supersedes older general descriptions for the limited question of whether the hospital is active; it does not settle the condition or future of each old building. [2] This research has not verified demolition completion, a trade-school opening or public historic-campus tours. Nothing in the 2024 preservation discussion should be used as 2026 travel clearance. [3]

The worker interview is a published firsthand quotation inside reporting, not an oral-history archive item with a verified accession number. No audio was played. No independent patient-authored primary document was inspected. No confidential records were sought. The museum's ability to provide this specific subject remains a trip gate, not an assumed service.

The museum's website accessibility link does not by itself verify step-free physical entry, accessible toilets or winter surface conditions. Holiday opening remains unresolved; ordinary weekly hours are not a promise for Thanksgiving or Christmas.
