## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A business owner can finish a shift elsewhere and begin another workday behind their own counter. The label “entrepreneur” does not reveal all the labor.

**Named, actually read firsthand evidence:** journalist Dan Gunderson's January 2025 MPR report directly quotes **Fowzia Adde**, the Somali-born community organizer associated with Fargo's immigrant business economy. Her description connects paid production work to evening business labor. Her short personal statement is: **“I love working. It just gives me joy.”** This is published interview testimony embedded in journalism, not an independently heard recording or an unedited oral-history transcript. The article also records her mother's food-selling work in a refugee camp as Adde's own remembered family example. That episode is not relocated to Fargo. [4]

**Contemporaneous institutional record:** Northwest Area Foundation's March 10, 2020 account names **Haytham Al Saegh**, a Syrian immigrant who arrived in Fargo in 2009, and his FM International Foods enterprise. It describes Adde and IDC's business-planning and financing assistance. Adde is directly quoted describing her work; Al Saegh is the subject, not a speaker whose words were recovered. The foundation identifies itself as an IDC supporter, so this is an interested funder's account rather than independent evaluation. [5]

**Original interpretive voice:** The story is not simply that new arrivals opened shops. It is that an ordinary transaction rests on work the customer rarely sees: a job elsewhere, a plan prepared after hours, help making an institution understand an unfamiliar applicant. Read the named accounts as evidence of those relationships, not a universal success formula. A person's value does not depend on owning a business, and a business opening does not prove that every barrier disappeared.

## 3. Then / now
The dated 2020 account is a snapshot of immigrant enterprise and support work. The 2025 interview adds the organizer's own view of continuing labor and financing constraints. Neither source establishes the condition of a named business today. [4,5]

The archive is a separate institution through which Fargo's historical record can be studied, not the former workplace of these narrators and not a confirmed custodian of their papers. University promotion of public research does not establish a current exhibition on Adde or Al Saegh. [1]

This entry concerns recent history. Nineteenth-century immigrant labor, farm labor, domestic work and other Fargo communities remain separate research needs rather than being absorbed into one contemporary story.
