# Nebraska City river commerce — from warehouse to wagon

## 2. The story

### Where river freight changed vehicles

Alexander Majors recalled traveling up the Missouri with a quartermaster representative to choose a new supply base. They reached Plattsmouth and judged Nebraska City “the most available point upon the river for our business.” His 1893 memoir says he arranged for warehouses in anticipation of large shipments. The digitized printed memoir is a participant looking back decades later. It explains the choice of port from a contractor's point of view. The district nomination separately ties 1858 military contracts and local buildings to provisioning western forts. [3,4]

Think through the transfer. Supplies carried by river needed room to land, wait and be sorted. The onward journey took wagons, stock, and people assigned to move and manage the loads. A permanent office had a different task from a loading point. The surviving office lets a visitor begin with the administration that turned river arrivals into overland departures. It provides no basis for identifying the exact spot where a particular crate changed hands. [1,3–5]

On January 28, 1866, a correspondent identified as Stella described what the enterprise looked like from the street. In a letter to her younger sister, reproduced by the state historical society, she wrote of “the bellowing oxen–braying of mules.” Where Majors remembers choosing a port, Stella notices the sound and the teamsters’ work. Her surname, original manuscript and vantage are unverified here. [3,6]

### The names in the freight system

The nomination traces Calvin Chapman from arrival in 1860 to assistant wagonmaster two years later, then into local trade. That gives the employee listings in the museum a name and a timeline without pretending his workdays can be reconstructed from a preservation form. The operator describes galleries with employee names and an 1860 Freighters Bible signed by Majors. On a confirmed museum day, compare the founder's account of choosing a port with the museum's record of the people expected to make it work. [1,4]

### Read the scale carefully

The operator reports an 1859 investment of around $300,000 and a peak of 44 million pounds in 1865. The NPS visitor place page describes millions of tons over the course of the operations. One is a reported single year, the other an aggregate phrase. Neither page provides a ledger or common scope. Carry both attributions. The documents establish substantial activity and leave the precise annual and cumulative figures open. [1,5]

## 3. Then / now

The Missouri River freight base included an office and other buildings tied to the company and Army supply business. The operator places the museum at 407 North 14th Street. The district nomination identifies that address as site 8, the Martin–Russell, Majors, Waddell–Gant House. Its account says the Army built it in 1858 for Quartermaster J. G. Martin and the company bought it in 1859. The nearby Stevenson House is a separate nomination entry. [1,4,5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The operator publishes Arbor Day–June 30 and July 1–October 31 windows. The museum's November interior access is unverified. Library special hours and holdings remain to be confirmed. [1,2]
- NPS’s visitor page calls the building the company’s original 1858 headquarters. The district nomination says the government built 407 North 14th for Martin in 1858 and sold it to the firm in 1859. The founding year of the building is shared. Its initial owner and official use need the archival distinction. [1,4,5]
- The 1858 location and contract, the operator's 1859 investment, and the 1865 reported peak have distinct dates. The cumulative tonnage wording is attributed separately. No contemporary manifest or worker testimony from Chapman was retrieved. [1,3–5]
- No verified public staging for the historic river landing and no matched landing photo vantage. [4–6]
