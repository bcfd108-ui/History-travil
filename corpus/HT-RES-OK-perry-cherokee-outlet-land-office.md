# Perry — waiting to file at the land office

## 2 · The story

### Before the federal queue

The Cherokee Nation’s public history distinguishes the Cherokee Outlet from the narrow Kansas Cherokee Strip lost under the 1866 treaty. The Nation describes collecting grazing fees in the Outlet in 1878, securing a five-year lease at $100,000 annually in 1883, and renewing at $200,000 annually in 1888. This record places an existing Cherokee economy and government behind the subsequent opening. The Nation’s account dates the sale agreement to December 19, 1891 and describes subsequent federal approval in 1893. Its account of an Indian Claims Commission finding puts the negotiated sale in the context of federal pressure and undervaluation. The original judgment was not retrieved here. [3]

The federal grazing order illustrates that pressure. President Benjamin Harrison’s proclamation characterized the Nation’s grazing contracts as “wholly illegal and void” and ordered livestock removed by October 2, 1890. This is the federal government’s assertion. Read it alongside the Nation’s published account that the later Indian Claims Commission found Harrison lacked authority to void the lease. [3–4]

### Noon, then a week of paperwork

President Grover Cleveland’s August 19, 1893 proclamation and its accompanying directions prescribed certificates, the opening at noon on September 16, and ways of initiating a claim. A person could settle on a tract or file at the district office. The rules imposed different clocks: settlement first meant three months to file, while district-office entry first allowed six months to establish residence and begin improvements. The government also reserved an acre in the County P townsite for a land office. The text is a primary record of the federal procedure, including the actual affidavit forms. [5]

A photograph held by the National Archives shows a crowd at Perry a week later. Its original caption gives the day, time and reason: “9 o’clock A.M. waiting to file.” A row of wooden buildings runs beside people waiting in hats and work clothes. The caption fixes the moment at September 23. The picture shows people still waiting to put something on paper after the run had ended. It does not identify the individual applicants or tell us which obtained title. [6–7]

Perry’s historical entry connects the town’s early growth to the opening. An archival study of land-office administration distinguishes the office’s federal register and receiver from the applicants. Read the two records together: the claimants’ lives generated a stack of paperwork, and the paperwork was administered by people with authority to accept, reject or refer claims. [5,8–9]

## 3 · Then / now

- **Historic location.** The 1893 U.S. Land Office at Perry and the queue photographed September 23. The specific identified frontage, surveyed lot and survival of any wall or doorway in the frame remain unverified. [5–9]
- **Lawful physical anchor.** Cherokee Strip Museum and Rose Hill School, 2617 West Fir Street. Drive to signed public visitor parking. Use the ordinary staffed museum entrance and the building or grounds staff opens. The museum campus is separate from the historic land-office location. [1–2]
- **Quiet reading.** The museum is the public place to read the Nation’s account alongside the federal forms and the photograph. Loss of Cherokee land and revenue is quiet reflection. [1,3,5–6]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Start in the public museum. First read the Nation’s description of the lease payments, the federal grazing order and the subsequent sale. [1,3–4]
2. Read the federal certificate and district-office rules. Take the time to distinguish registration, choosing a tract, filing and later title. [5]
3. **Strongest story moment — Nine o’clock, one week later.** Open the National Archives frame at the museum’s public anchor. Read the caption, look at the people still waiting, then turn back to the Cherokee Nation’s record of what the transfer cost. [3,6]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The Nation-published narrative prints a grazing proclamation date of February 17, 1889. The dated text of Harrison’s Proclamation 296 at the American Presidency Project prints February 17, 1890 and an October 2, 1890 deadline. Underlying Indian Claims Commission opinion and Starr Roll payment pages were not retrieved. [3–4]
- The 1893 place is often called the Cherokee Strip, including in the archive series title and the museum’s name. Cherokee Nation distinguishes the Kansas strip and Cherokee Outlet. The dossier follows the Nation’s terminology for the land history and preserves each source title. [1,3,6]
- DocsTeach’s item opens and prints a public-domain mark. The NARA catalog link returned no readable item text in the web open. Gateway catalogs a Perry line photograph separately, but no identity match between versions is adopted. No identified claimant or matched present-day frontage was verified. [6–7,10]
