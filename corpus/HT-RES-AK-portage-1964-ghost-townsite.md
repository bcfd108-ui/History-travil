# HT-RES-AK-portage-1964-ghost-townsite — Portage: the town the 1964 earthquake lowered into the tide
Division AK seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

Portage was a small road-and-rail settlement at the head of Turnagain Arm. On March 27, 1964, the magnitude 9.2 earthquake dropped its ground by roughly two to two and a half meters; the tides came in over ground that had been dry, and the settlement was largely abandoned. Its remnants are read today from a signed roadside interpretive stop. This entry is reflection-first disaster ground.

## 1. Trip gate
Checked November 25, 2026. The Old Portage Townsite is documented by Alaska DOT&PF as an interpretive site at Seward Highway mile 80.2, directly across the highway from the Whittier train station. The agency's corridor plan says the earthquake remnants are visible there and calls for roadside interpretation of the 1964 earthquake and railroad history. The public visit is from the signed roadside interpretive ground; mudflats, submerged structures, railroad operating land and private remnants are outside the visitor boundary. The nearby Begich, Boggs Visitor Center is a separate seasonal public anchor with earthquake interpretation.

## 2. The story
Portage occupied low ground at the head of Turnagain Arm, where railroad, highway and tidal landscape met. On March 27, 1964, the magnitude 9.2 Alaska earthquake shook the settlement for roughly five and a half to six minutes. Buildings were cracked and foundations were disturbed, but the deeper change was vertical: regional tectonic movement and local compaction lowered the land by about two to two and a half meters. High tides could now cross ground that had been dry.

George Larson, a Portage resident quoted in the USGS community report, described violent motion, a broken gasoline-station chimney and fissures beneath buildings. The report found that most structures were not immediately beyond repair. Subsidence changed that verdict. Salt water returned with the tides, sediment accumulated and the settlement was largely abandoned.

The disaster also cut the transportation system that had helped create Portage. The highway and railroad were built across saturated alluvium and estuary mud. Earthquake-weakened deposits fissured, spread and settled under their embankments. Alaska's transportation history and its earthquake history are therefore the same story here: the corridor survived only by rebuilding on ground whose elevation and behavior had changed.

## 3. Then / now
Before the earthquake, Portage was a small road-and-rail settlement of about one hundred people. After the ground dropped, daily saltwater flooding killed trees, covered former upland with silt and left structures standing in an intertidal landscape. USGS research measured about eighteen square kilometers lowered into the intertidal zone and documented rapid natural sedimentation afterward.

Today, Portage is not a preserved town to enter. Remnant structures and the ghost forest are read from the highway corridor and interpretive ground. The settlement's disappearance should not be romanticized as an invitation to explore ruins. The visible lesson is that a coastline can move vertically in minutes and keep changing for decades.

## 4. What's still there
- Old townsite landscape — low, wet ground and the relationship between surviving remnants, tidal flats and the highway corridor from lawful roadside interpretation.
- Building remnants — a few deteriorating structures may remain visible from the road. Visibility varies with vegetation, sediment, tide and continued decay; no entry is authorized.
- Ghost forest — dead spruce trunks record saltwater intrusion following subsidence. They are landscape evidence, not a route onto the flats.
- Railroad/highway corridor — the rebuilt transportation lines show why Portage remained a junction after the settlement largely disappeared. Railroad property is operational and outside the visitor boundary.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Use the signed Portage townsite interpretive stop at mile 80.2. Read the scene in three layers: first the highway and railroad corridor; then the lowered, tidal landscape; finally the remaining structures and dead trees. Compare that view with the 1964 USGS image, which shows water surrounding buildings, cars and roads after the land had dropped. If more context is needed, continue to the separate Begich, Boggs Visitor Center during its operating season; its exhibits include the 1964 earthquake. Do not cross the highway outside a marked crossing, enter railroad land, approach the ruins or step onto mudflats.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Official sources use different rounded subsidence figures: the USGS photo record says six feet; the Alaska DOT&PF interpretive plan says about eight feet; later USGS studies describe about two meters and another technical account gives at least 2.4 meters. The differences reflect location, rounding and the division between regional and local subsidence. This entry prints the range rather than forcing one number.

The DOT&PF plan describes an interpretive-site proposal. It confirms the lawful public location and intended story, but not the present condition of every sign or pullout feature. A dated field check remains open.

The individual photographer and a separate accession number are not printed on the USGS media record. The record itself, its stable media slug, original asset name and item-level public-domain statement are preserved in §7.
