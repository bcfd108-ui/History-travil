## 2. Story

**Fort Matanzas National Monument · Reflection · Research draft · Fred**

A supply route explains this small tower. In June 1740 James Oglethorpe was trying to cut off Spanish St. Augustine. Governor Manuel de Montiano sought supplies from Cuba. Six Spanish vessels entered Matanzas Inlet on July 7, according to the NPS landscape inventory. The British withdrew from the inlet, the Spanish reached St. Augustine, and Oglethorpe abandoned the siege on July 20. The incident showed how an inlet well south of the Castillo could feed the town under pressure. [4]

The tower was an undertaking in wood before it was one in stone. The builders drove long piles into marsh and hauled coquina from near El Piñon. NPS puts the start of those works in fall 1740. As work continued in July 1741, a small Spanish galliot disrupted a British attack on a sloop, which had grounded on a sandbar. The NPS account records two Spanish crew killed and two wounded. A second British approach in September 1742 encountered the completed tower and withdrew. The deep story is the labor and the dependency on water, stores and navigation that gave a small building its importance. The present sources name Montiano and Oglethorpe but leave the construction crew largely unnamed. [4–5]

Montiano’s correspondence to Juan de Güemes y Horcasitas survives in a Georgia Historical Society letter book. The collection description is accessible. This draft uses the NPS inventory’s account of the supply request, with the actual letters still open for transcription and attribution. [6]

## 3. Then and now

The tower occupied Rattlesnake Island. The coquina field and square mass in Herbert Kahler’s 1934 photograph already stand behind open water. The NPS visitor center in use today was built in 1936. Treat that center as its own preservation-era structure and the present place to interpret the story. It sits across the water from the military ground. [2, 4, 7–8]

## 5. Public progression

Enter the visitor center from the signed public parking approach. Watch the short area-history film inside if available. Finish with **The Seven July Crossing**. Read the supply ships’ arrival as a change in what the people under siege could eat and how long they could hold out. The interpretation ends indoors. [2, 4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The HABS catalog assigns 1737–42 to initial construction. The NPS building account starts tower work in fall 1740, with completion by September 1742. Its 1565 framing refers to the earlier location history, not the age of the standing tower. [5, 7]
- The compendium governs area hours of 9–5:30. The operator’s conditions page gives the staffed building a 5 p.m. close. Use the building hours for this visit. [1, 3]
