# St. Louis — The garment workers’ winter
HT-RES-MO-st-louis-garment-workers · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The St. Louis District Council No. 4 of the United Garment Workers of America wrote to fellow unions on January 22, 1910. Published in the January 29 issue of *St. Louis Labor*, the letter described the fight with Marx & Haas as having begun September 13, 1909. By midwinter the local was asking for continued “moral and financial support.” The same page follows the appeal with contributions by organization and amount. Entries as varied as musicians, cigar makers, and milk wagon drivers show how lost pay in one industry could become a concern of workers across trades and cities. [3]

The union’s letter claimed a thousand people might be thrown out of work at the start of a severe winter. This is the union’s public characterization of the dispute. The listed donations can be read independently of that figure. They show the paper performing a second job besides reporting news by making help visible and inviting more of it. The original contract, factory payroll and company account are still open research. [3, 6, 7]

The block also opens a later window into how garments actually came to market. Anna Trefts Tibbe’s January 13, 1991 interview is indexed by the Missouri Historical Society. The archive describes work in design and mentions Alice Topp-Lee. Tibbe’s account of the downtown trade’s decline begins around the end of World War II as summarized in that catalog. The Saint Louis Fashion Fund describes a large production district still operating into the mid-1950s. A participant’s remembered turning point and a later estimate of industry output can both help explain how visible activity lasted after changes had begun. Tibbe belongs to a later generation than the workers in the lockout letter. Her recording has not been heard for this dossier. [4, 5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The National Register lists the Building at 1300 Washington Avenue at 1300–1310 and dates it to 1899. The garment district operated across multiple buildings. The street address anchors the district account, while room-level identification for the 1909–10 events and each person cited remains open. [2–5]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The public sidewalk corner of 13th Street and Washington Avenue is a short urban walk from lawful parking or transit. Use open sidewalks and marked crossings. The building's doors, loading areas, residences, and private interiors lie outside the walk. [1, 2]

Start at the open public corner. Read the width and height of the building before turning to the newspaper. Find a safe stopping position on the same sidewalk away from doors and travel lanes. Read the lockout letter and then its list of donations. **Strongest story moment · The donations after the letter.** A reader can follow support for garment workers across hundreds of miles on a single newspaper page. ## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The union named September 13, 1909 as the start of its fight and described the dispute as a lockout. An employer chronology or union payroll is still due. Keep “a thousand” attributed to the Council letter. [3, 6, 7]
- Tibbe’s cataloged recollection and the Fashion Fund’s mid-1950s narrative describe different measures of decline. Retrieving the full recording is needed before attributing an exact sentence or date to her. [4, 5]
- National Register metadata was retrieved. The linked NPGallery asset gave Internal Error on open. The Missouri State Parks candidate returned HTML in the local retrieval where a PDF was expected. Building tenant history, exact contemporary work floors, and item-level corroboration remain open. [2]
