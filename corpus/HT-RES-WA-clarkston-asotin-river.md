## 2. The story

An unstamped postcard turns Asotin from a panorama into somebody's home. Its writer identifies herself as **Mrs. G. Stonebraker**, and the University of Idaho's catalog transcribes her question: **“How is housekeeping? Do you make the office?”** She marks schools, a church, the courthouse and her own place. The photograph is approximately dated 1928; the writer's full given name and recipient are not established here. This is a primary household voice preserved through a repository transcription, not an interview or invented narration. [5]

The question is more useful than another explorer's arrival. Read it as an invitation to notice the ordinary institutions through which a town recognizes itself. A school is where children spend the day; a courthouse organizes civic business; a house is a point from which somebody writes to someone elsewhere. Those are this dossier's interpretive questions, not claims that a particular numbered building survives.

The museum itself adds a change of use. Visit Lewis Clark Valley describes its main building as a 1920 funeral parlor built for Henry J. Merchant Sr., later donated by the Merchant family for museum use. The building's public role links household loss, local service and the decision to preserve community memory. That chronology comes from a regional institutional description, not a deed or original construction file read here. [3]

This entry does not frame Asotin as empty before settler town life. A full account of the area's Indigenous continuity needs community-authorized sources and is not supplied by a postcard or tourism slogan. No Tribal story is reconstructed from secondhand place-name etymologies.
