## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A ranch household's labor does not end where the pasture ends. Clothes, meals, schooling and cash income belong in the same history as livestock.

**Named ranch account, with mediation explicit:** The New Mexico Farm & Ranch Heritage Museum publishes an abstract of **Janice Gnatkowski's** interviews on November 16, 2001, and January 4, 2002, recorded in Carrizozo for *Farm and Ranch Folks*. The record directly connects her family's ranch experience to Ancho in Lincoln County. It recalls her mother remaking hand-me-down clothes, a substantial household garden and Gnatkowski's own preference for outdoor ranch work as a girl. Later memories contrast domestic labor before and after automatic laundry appliances. These are the museum's summaries of her testimony, not verbatim words independently checked against a recording. [2]

**Named Capitan household voice:** Doris Cherry's profile **“Margaret Rench: a positive attitude,” The Ruidoso News, June 30, 1988, p. 11A**, gives a second, explicitly Capitan connection. It connects Rench to ranch life near Corona and household livelihood in Capitan, including cooking, laundry, construction and newspaper work. [3]

Rench's own directly attributed words about learning newspaper work were: **“Well, I learned to type in 1943 and I liked it.”** This short quotation was checked against the scanned page, not trusted to its damaged OCR. [3]

**What the pairing adds:** These are two different women's accounts, not a composite character. One offers a rancher's remembered household economy through an archival abstract; the other offers a contemporary newspaper profile and quoted recollection of earning a living in Capitan. Neither is a complete labor census or proof that every family shared the same experience.

Interpretive synthesis: the important movement is between categories often kept apart. Cooking, mending, making a building, teaching oneself a skill and keeping an enterprise alive all belong in the local economic story. This is not a nostalgia exercise that treats exhausting labor as effortless independence.
