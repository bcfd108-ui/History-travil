# Omaha Union Station — the red caps and the passenger hall

## 2. The story

### A porter remembers the station at work

“It was a very busy, active place,” Bob Fahey told Omaha writer Leo Adam Biga. Biga identifies him as a former red cap and station master who met his future wife there. The account comes from Biga's published 2011 interview, not an independently retrieved audio tape. The Durham's baggage-room history explains what the red caps did: they helped people check or collect luggage, moved it and escorted passengers to trains. A traveler walking across an imposing hall needed someone to know which door, which train, and what could be carried in the minutes available. [3,4]

### A building made for connections

The current station opened January 15, 1931, on the location of the 1899 depot. The National Register file identifies Gilbert Stanley Underwood and Peter Kiewit & Sons in the building's design and construction record. [11] The restored public Great Hall and the baggage-room story show different sides of the same system. The public finishes and waiting areas handled the pause before a train. The baggage facilities and red caps kept things and people moving between that hall and the train. [4–7]

Nebraska Public Media quotes Fahey giving 64 trains a day at the 1944 peak. Biga's account gives 10,000 people at a wartime daily peak and reports 64 trains and seven railroads. Treat trains, railroads and passengers as separate figures, which the sources present in different contexts. The batch did not retrieve a station log or a counted-day report. This remains a recollection alongside modern reporting, not a reconstructed timetable. [3,8]

### The traffic went away. The room survived.

Fahey remembered how sad it was to see the traffic dwindle, in the same interview. Union Station ceased passenger service in 1971. The railroad donated the building to Omaha in 1973 and the Western Heritage Museum opened there in 1975. A later restoration returned the Great Hall to museum use. It is now possible to occupy the room and read a worker's memories in it. The display trains and period scenes help interpret a system that no longer operates there in the same way. [3,5–9]

## 3. Then / now

The terminal once processed passengers and baggage. The historic station now houses the Durham Museum, including the restored Great Hall and lower-level exhibitions. Passenger service ended in 1971, followed by city ownership and a new museum use. Its 1971 National Register listing and 2016 National Historic Landmark designation are distinct milestones. [5–7,9]
