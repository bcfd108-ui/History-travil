# HT-RES-OH-oberlin-abolition-ground — A town that answered a kidnapping in daylight by emptying itself down the road to Wellington
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Oberlin was founded as a deliberate community — college, church, and town built together on antislavery ground — and on September 13, 1858, it acted as one body: when slave catchers seized John Price, who had lived and worked in Oberlin for about two years, and carried him eight miles south to a Wellington hotel, hundreds of Oberlinians, Black and white, students, professors, and townspeople, went down the road after him and brought him back. The rescue, the indictments, and the two Cleveland trials made Oberlin a national argument about whether a fugitive slave law could be enforced in a town that refused it. The ground is still legible: the 1842 First Church, the college square, the street where Price was hidden, and the monuments in its park.

## 2. The story
Oberlin was founded in **1833** by two Presbyterian ministers, John Jay Shipherd and Philo Penfield Stewart, as a colony and a college built together — the City of Oberlin's own history calls it a community conceived as integrated from the start. In **1835**, the college broke the American pattern and admitted students regardless of race, and pioneered co-education. Black and white children studied together in the town's one-room schoolhouse — built 1836–1837, still standing at the Heritage Center — in defiance of Ohio's Black Laws, as the Ohio marker on the grounds records.

The town's meeting house rose at its center. The First Church in Oberlin was erected in **1842** — the date on the church front, as the Historic American Buildings Survey's data pages record — a large brick auditorium church at the northwest corner of North Main and West Lorain Streets. The HABS record calls it the only remaining original building of the unique Congregational community of church, college, and town, and names its architect only as "perhaps Reverend Charles Finney" (see §6). Antislavery Oberlin assembled there, and it is where the rescuers came home to in 1859.

By the 1850s Oberlin was a key junction on the Underground Railroad — the Ohio History Connection's marker records at least five routes converging there — and its position was stated flatly by its own people: no fugitive was ever taken from Oberlin and returned to slavery, a record Professor James Harris Fairchild later observed had been kept without bloodshed. The legal ground shifted in **1858**, when a Democratic legislature repealed Ohio's protective law and left freedom seekers exposed to the federal Fugitive Slave Act of **1850** (City of Oberlin's history).

John Price had escaped enslavement in Kentucky in the mid-1850s and had been living and working in Oberlin for about two years. On **September 13, 1858**, slave catchers Anderson Jennings and Richard Mitchell, acting with federal marshal Jacob Lowe and his assistant Samuel Davis under a warrant, lured Price out of Oberlin with the promise of work, forced him into a carriage, and drove him eight miles south to Wellington to catch the 5:13 p.m. southbound train toward Columbus and Kentucky. News reached downtown Oberlin about 1 p.m. A crowd gathered in front of John Watson's grocery on South Main Street — Watson, a Black store owner who had pledged years before to aid any freedom seeker in distress, reached Wellington first. Behind him came the town: townspeople, students, and professors in wagons, buggies, and carriages, some on foot. The Oberlin Heritage Center puts the crowd around the Wadsworth Hotel, where the captors held Price, at between 200 and 500 men. Charles Langston, a Black schoolteacher, moved through the crowd keeping it calm and negotiated with the captors; John Anthony Copeland was among those who pressed the hotel. When the train arrived and negotiation failed, the crowd forced its way in, found Price, and put him in a buggy — driven, the trial record later established, by Simeon Bushnell — back to Oberlin.

Price was hidden first in the home of James Fitch, a known Underground Railroad agent, and then, because Fitch's house was too obvious, in Professor Fairchild's house on South Professor Street — Fairchild, the more conservative, law-abiding man, agreed to shelter him, and no search ever came there. What happened to Price afterward is undocumented; the Evans account, adapted from Kornblith and Lasser's *Elusive Utopia*, states it directly: he was never seen on Oberlin's streets again. The tradition that he was carried on to Canada is printed in §6 as tradition, not record.

The federal answer came on **December 6, 1858**, when a Cleveland grand jury indicted thirty-seven men — twenty-two from Oberlin or Russia Township, twelve from Wellington, one each from Pittsfield and Penfield, and Charles Langston, by then a Columbus resident with long Oberlin ties. Twelve were free Black men; five were never arrested. President James Buchanan personally pressed the prosecution. In response, Lorain County's grand jury indicted the capturing party for kidnapping under an Ohio statute of 1857 — the two prosecutions became each other's bargaining counter.

Only two men were tried. Simeon Bushnell's trial ran **April 5–15, 1859**; the prosecutor excluded antislavery sympathizers from the jury, and Bushnell was convicted. Charles Langston's followed, and the verdict, returned **May 10, 1859**, was guilty. Before sentencing, Langston addressed the court — a speech the Oberlin College Archives preserves — arguing he had not been tried by his peers and pledging to do for any seized man what he had done at Wellington, whatever the penalty. The sentences were light against the statute: Bushnell fined $600 and sixty days; Langston $100 and twenty days. The remaining rescuers, refusing bail in protest, waited in the Cuyahoga County Jail — eighty-three days, in the Evans brothers' case — published a newspaper, *The Rescuer*, from inside, and lost a habeas corpus petition in the Ohio Supreme Court, 3–2. Then federal prosecutors dropped the remaining charges as the state let the kidnapping case go, and the rescuers came home to a heroes' welcome, celebrated that evening at the First Church (the release date is disputed; see §6).

The story does not end at the jail door. In **1859**, John Mercer Langston — Charles's brother, an Oberlin graduate and the town's Black lawyer — recruited two young Oberlin men of color, John A. Copeland and Lewis Sheridan Leary, into John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry; both died there. Oberlin memorialized Copeland, Leary, and fellow raider Shields Green with a monument — the Ohio marker record calls it the only monument erected for any of the five African Americans who fought with Brown — standing today in Martin Luther King Jr. Park alongside the Rescue monument, dedicated in **1990** per the Oberlin College Archives. Both are Network to Freedom listings, as are the Heritage Center, the College Archives, and Westwood Cemetery.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** 1842, a new brick meeting house at Main and Lorain — the only building of the founding Congregational community that would survive, and the room where, in July 1859, the freed rescuers were welcomed home. **Now:** the same church, still an active congregation's house at the same corner, documented by the HABS in 1953 (§7) with tower, three front doors, and auditorium intact — and still the start point for the Freedom's Friends walk.

**Then:** September 13, 1858, about 1 p.m. — a crowd forming in front of John Watson's grocery on South Main Street, an eight-mile improvised procession south to Wellington, and a hidden man moved at night from Fitch's known agent's house to Fairchild's safer one on South Professor Street. **Now:** South Professor Street is the Heritage Center's own ground — the Monroe House (1866), the Little Red Schoolhouse (1836/1837), and the Jewett House (1884) stand there as its campus — and both monuments now stand together in Martin Luther King Jr. Park.

## 4. What's still there
- The First Church in Oberlin (erected 1842), northwest corner of North Main and West Lorain Streets — an active congregation, documented by HABS (OH-2117): 22 photographs, 8 drawings, 11 data pages at the Library of Congress
- The Oberlin-Wellington Rescue monument, dedicated 1990, in Martin Luther King Jr. Park — a National Park Service Network to Freedom site (Oberlin College Archives; NPS listings)
- The monument to John A. Copeland, Lewis Sheridan Leary, and Shields Green in the same park, per the Ohio marker record and the state's trail guide
- The Little Red Schoolhouse (1836/1837) at the Oberlin Heritage Center — the integrated one-room school of the Ohio marker record
- The Heritage Center campus on South Professor Street — the Monroe House (1866), home of abolitionist lecturer and Congressman James Monroe, who helped arrange Price's concealment; the Jewett House (1884); tours as stated in §1
- Tappan Square and the Oberlin College ground a block from First Church — the college that admitted students regardless of race from 1835
- Westwood Cemetery on Morgan Street — a Network to Freedom site holding the burials of hundreds involved in the fight to end slavery, per the state's trail guide

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The rescuers' release date does not agree across the sources, and all three print unresolved. The Wilson Bruce Evans Home Historical Society's account states the remaining rescuers returned to Oberlin on **July 6, 1859**, welcomed at the First Church that evening. The Oberlin College Archives holds a broadside calling a mass meeting to celebrate the release on **July 7, 1859**. *The Liberator* of July 22, 1859, reprinting the Cleveland *Leader*, states the rescuers "were all released from imprisonment at Cleveland, on the 9th instant," the government entering a *nolle prosequi*. July 6, 7, and 9 cannot all be the release day; the records checked do not settle whether the difference is discharge, departure, or homecoming, and §2 asserts no single date.

The Wellington crowd is an estimate with a wide mouth: the Oberlin Heritage Center gives "between 200 and 500 men." No tighter count exists in the sources checked; §2 carries the range. John Price's fate is a genuine gap: the Evans account states that what happened to him afterward is undocumented and that he was never seen on Oberlin's streets again. The commonly repeated statement that he was taken on to Canada appears in summary accounts (including reference works used only as leads) but is not documented in the primary and institutional sources checked; §2 prints the concealment and stops there.

The park holding the Rescue monument is named two ways: the Archives calls it "King Park"; the Ohio marker record and state trail guide call it "Martin Luther King Jr. Park" (the fuller form is used here). The First Church's architect is unsettled in the survey record itself — the HABS data pages say "Perhaps Reverend Charles Finney," the church "said to be modelled after the Broadway Tabernacle" — and §2 carries the "perhaps." The 1842 date rests on the HABS data page and the date on the church front; the HABS written history was prepared February 10, 1959, by Perry E. Borchers, from the survey's 1953 photographs.

Two pieces of the rescue's ground could not be verified standing, flagged rather than assumed: John Watson's grocery, and the Wadsworth Hotel in Wellington where Price was held. No coordinates print: no primary source checked verifies a point for this ground, and the HABS record locates the First Church by street corner only — §10's latitude and longitude are blank by rule. The entry carries no National Register number of its own: its federal recognition in the records checked is the Network to Freedom listing.
