## 2. Story
**Hensley Settlement, Cumberland Gap National Historical Park · Research draft · Fred**

The settlement sits apart from the story of travelers passing through the Gap. Its families needed to make a living in one place. Around 1900 Burton Hensley obtained mountain land and gave his daughter Nicey a share. NPS records that she received 21 acres, and that in 1903 she and Sherman Hensley moved with their family to the top of the mountain. Other families followed. Corn fed households and animals. Barns, smokehouses and granaries put different kinds of work a short distance from the family dwelling. Schooling and blacksmithing required separate places. In the 1924 Willie Gibbons house, preservation staff noted newspaper pasted inside, serving as “wallpaper and protection.” That small detail shows what a family could use, long before recreation brought visitors to the mountain. [3–4]

By the late 1940s families were moving away. NPS identifies Sherman as the last to leave, in 1951. In 1967–68 the Cumberland Gap Job Corps began making the buildings usable for interpretation. Former resident Jesse Gibbons directed work based on residents' accounts and his knowledge of construction. Some structures were rebuilt. Others kept variable quantities of original wood. The restoration itself has a history and identifiable workers. [3–5]

## 3. Then and now
The nomination describes families working pasture and corn land and a gradual return of woodland. Its reconstruction record differentiates surviving buildings from later work shaped by interviews. The public program today visits farm structures and a one-room schoolhouse in season, with the visitor center as the ordinary year-round interpretation anchor. [1–5]

## 4. Surviving features and access
- **Seasonal guided exterior** — Staff-led views of restored farm groups and paths. Ticket and confirmed reservation details apply.
- **Interior conditional** — Only spaces opened or indicated by NPS staff on the tour.
- **Off-season interpretation** — Middlesboro visitor center when open and NPS's remote record.
- **Protected ground** — Cemetery and any closed structures are outside the visit. No private or service-road access is used. [1–4]

## 5. Public trip progression
Use the NPS visitor center to confirm the tour. On the ranger-led program, read the barn and household structures as different jobs. End, if the tour reaches it, at **A room made of what people had**, using the schoolhouse or an operator-designated farmstead stop to consider how work, learning and improvised materials shared a ridge. The same reading can be done at the visitor center outside the tour season. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
- NPS's short account gives about 100 residents. The nomination estimates about 120 during the 1930s. These are approximate figures with different stated scope. No exact peak count is adopted. [3–4]
- The 1960s reconstruction means any claim for an original building part needs its individual preservation record. The nomination says roughly half the structures rebuilt at that time were totally reconstructed. [4–5]
- The underlying oral-history collection is identified, but individual interview audio or transcripts supporting a chosen exact quotation were not verified. No firsthand quotations are attributed to a resident based only on the park's summary. [5–6]
- No separate Hensley visitor count or two-source tour-anchor coordinates were verified.
