# Arkadelphia-area Ouachita saltmaking and Clark County Historical Museum
HT-RES-AR-arkadelphia-salt-and-river-workers · Arkansas OP05 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

On November 28, 1804, William Dunbar recorded speaking with an experienced Ouachita resident who had supplied himself with “salt by evaporation.” The printed transcription identifies him in that entry as an older Dutch hunter. The state encyclopedia identifies the man as Paltz. He gave the expedition a lead based on his own labor and joined George Hunter and others to examine the source the next day. That work depended on someone who already knew where brine could be found. [3, 5]

Dunbar logged the trial the next day. A party found brackish water below the surface and brought some to camp. He recorded that ten quarts yielded eight dry ounces of saline material. The expedition was comparing the brine with river water and registering the result of one evaporation. The number measures a particular field sample, with no evidence here of commercial output at the same spring. In the journal, the salt research appears among accounts of oars, poles, rain and difficult current, all of which governed the labor of the trip. [3, November 28–29, 1804]

The state encyclopedia’s Daleville history traces later salt manufacture and farming by John Hemphill’s family across the river from early Blakelytown. Its statewide industry history places Hemphill’s operation in 1811. Those accounts carry the story into a market economy in which preservation of meat mattered. They leave individual salt workers, fuel suppliers, hired or coerced labor, and transport accounts to be traced. Preserve Indigenous saltmaking as part of the earlier regional history while holding any more specific account for Tribal evidence. The town museum gives a place to read the record before the visitor reaches any ground that needs its own access decision. [1, 3, 5, 6, 8]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The historical deposits, experimental sampling place and later production site are documented in the Ouachita vicinity across multiple sources. The 1917 Missouri-Pacific depot is the public interpretive anchor and represents a later transport era. No exact spring, works parcel or legal approach has been established. [2–9]

## 5. Public progression

Use the street-side museum approach. Look for ordinary public county history displays if the museum is staffed. Bring the two dated journal entries for reading and allow the depot to mark a later kind of freight and travel. **Strongest story moment · Ten quarts to eight ounces.** A remembered spring became measurable only after someone knew where to dig, someone carried the water and someone boiled it down. Keep coercion and displaced histories to quiet reflection. [1–9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The city gives Friday 9 a.m.–2 p.m. and Saturday 10 a.m.–noon. A July 2023 local report gave Wed–Fri noon–5 p.m. and described a separate museum nonprofit, Clark County Arkansas Museum Inc., while the city still uses the historical association wording. Follow the city’s printed visit schedule and confirm through the museum when changes matter. [1, 7]
- The Emory/Ouachita Baptist manuscript catalog and its IIIF manifest resolved. The manifest exposed one canvas carrying the journal title. The manuscript leaf for November 28–29 was not located there. The quotations and quantity are from the transcribed journal. Paltz’s name is supplied separately by the state encyclopedia. [3–5]
- The later saltworks chronology gives a named operator, but the surviving records here do not join the experimental location and later worksite into a single verified footprint. No contemporaneous Hemphill payroll, lease or ledger was retrieved. [3, 5, 6, 8]
