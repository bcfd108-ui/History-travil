## 2. The story
**Original interpretive framing:** The emigrants called the rocks a city because familiar buildings gave them a way to describe an unfamiliar landscape. But naming a place is not discovering an empty place. This visit should begin with homeland and end with the physical traces of travelers whose own imagined destinations lay elsewhere.

NPS identifies the area as part of Shoshone homelands and describes Shoshone and Bannock use of pinyon nuts and other resources. Its archaeological account includes earlier occupation and Fremont-style ceramics. These are agency interpretations, not quotations from Tribal narrators, and do not turn every rock into a documented ceremonial site. [4]

**The Tribe's published voice:** the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes' own history explains seasonal hunting, fishing and plant gathering across broad ancestral lands. Its contemporary account emphasizes protecting resources “for future generations.” That three-word excerpt comes from the Tribe's climate statement, not a City of Rocks oral history. The same Tribal publication describes present sovereign governments and community institutions. Tribal history belongs in the present tense as well as the past. No site-specific Tribal story, ritual or traditional name has been invented for this entry. [5]

**Primary emigrant excerpt, agency transcription:** Margaret Frink wrote on July 17, 1850, “a sublime, strange, and wonderful scene”. Her passage describes the rocky landscape as buildings and notes the rejoining Salt Lake route. NPS publishes the transcription; the original manuscript/page has not been independently collated in this pass. Her impression is evidence of her perception, not an objective claim about Indigenous absence or a precise land survey. [6]

The site's historical network includes Twin Sisters, Pinnacle Pass, the Salt Lake Cutoff junction, and the later Kelton–Boise stage-station remnants at Emigrant Canyon Spring. Those are different components and periods, not one preserved wagon encampment. [7]

The NPS Cultural Landscape Inventory identifies trail remnants, inscriptions and views as surviving California Trail-era characteristics. Their value lies in the relationship between route, water, rock and movement, not only an isolated signature. [8]

## 3. Then / now
- **Then:** travelers described a city of forms and compared rocks to familiar architecture. **Now:** a visitor can compare those metaphors with the landscape without assuming a precise diary viewpoint. [6]
- **Then:** trail routes converged here. **Now:** public interpretation can explain the network, but a modern road is not automatically an original wagon trace. [7–8]
- **Then:** names were placed on rocks. **Now:** surviving inscriptions are fragile historical evidence, not an invitation to add a name. NPS's Junior Ranger material specifically directs educational visits to Camp Rock/Register Rock. Our activity is observation only. [9]
- **Then and now:** this is more than emigrant scenery. The official Tribal account and NPS homeland history prevent a “people arrived here in 1849” framing. [4–5]

## 4. What's still there
**Public, conditional on current closure/route checks:** Almo visitor center; designated reserve roads, pullouts and marked pedestrian approaches; interpreted Camp Rock/Register Rock viewing areas. These are intended visitor resources, not blanket access to all terrain. [1–2,9]

**Public landscape, not guaranteed winter-reachable:** Twin Sisters landscape and designated trail viewpoints. Historic-site descriptions establish significance, not today's road condition. [3,7]

**Restricted / permission-dependent:** private parcels inside the reserve. IDPR notes roughly one-quarter private ownership. Fences, gates, homes, livestock facilities and informal tracks are not shortcuts. [1]

**Protected/no-contact:** inscriptions, archaeological traces and artifacts. Do not touch, rub, chalk, wet, trace, clean, collect, climb for a closer inscription view or publish sensitive archaeological coordinates. No scavenger prompt requires approaching cultural material beyond the normal authorized viewing area.

**Not included:** rock-climbing routes, pinnacles, scrambling, unmarked spring/ruin exploration, Indigenous cultural sites or camping. An old nomination's “unrestricted” box cannot override today's closures or ownership. [10–11]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Planning sequence, not a verified continuous winter route; allow roughly 60–90 minutes plus driving only when the manager confirms the chosen short stops are accessible.

**First, Almo visitor center:** obtain the current public route/map and read the homeland and resource context. If conditions block the reserve, let this become the whole visit instead of improvising a winter expedition. Address: 3035 S Elba-Almo Road, Almo. [1–2]

**Second, a manager-confirmed public landscape pullout:** see the scale before reducing the place to names. Read Frink's brief excerpt and ask which forms explain the metaphor. Exact pullout and safe photo point remain unassigned; no supposed diary camera position.

**Third, Register Rock or Camp Rock, whichever public approach is cleared:** read current interpretation first, then observe from the designated visitor surface. No need to complete both. Compare the extensive landscape with a small surviving human mark. [9]

**End with the strongest story moment:** step back, without leaving the public path, and hold together the landscape, Tribal continuity and the emigrant's desire to be remembered. The present visitor's contribution is restraint: a record in the app, not another mark on the rock. This conclusion is our editorial recommendation, not a claimed Indigenous teaching.

Optional Twin Sisters/route-junction interpretation belongs only in a later manager-cleared circuit; it is not required for this winter-ready short version. Return the way confirmed open, never through Emery Pass merely to complete a loop. [3,7]
