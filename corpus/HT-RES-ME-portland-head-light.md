# HT-RES-ME-portland-head-light — Portland Head Light: the first lighthouse lighted by the United States government
Division ME seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

Portland Head Light was first shown on January 10, 1791 — the first lighthouse lighted under the United States government, one of only four authorized during George Washington's first term, built of local field and shore stone at his direction. The tower has been raised and shortened but never rebuilt. It stands in Fort Williams Park at Cape Elizabeth, still an active Coast Guard light, and the HABS frame in §7 shows it with its 1891 keeper's house on the headland.

## 1. Trip gate
The lighthouse stands in Fort Williams Park on Portland Head. The park is open to the public year-round; the former keeper's house operates as a museum with seasonal hours. The tower and light remain an active aid to navigation maintained by the U.S. Coast Guard. Confirm park hours, museum status, and any access restrictions with the Town of Cape Elizabeth or Fort Williams Park before visiting. Parking is available in the park.

## 2. The story
Portland (then Falmouth) was a major port by the late 18th century, yet Maine had no lighthouses. Merchants petitioned the Massachusetts government; construction of a rubble-stone tower began under Massachusetts in 1787. After the federal government assumed responsibility for aids to navigation, Congress appropriated additional funds in 1790. Local masons Jonathan Bryant and John Nichols completed the work. The light was first shown on 10 January 1791, making it the first lighthouse lighted under the U.S. government and one of only four authorized during George Washington's first term. Washington directed use of local field and shore stone.

The tower has been altered in height several times (shortened in 1813 and again in the 1880s, then raised) but never rebuilt from the ground up. It has carried successive Fresnel lenses and, later, modern aerobeacons. A substantial keeper's house (1891) and support buildings stand on the headland. The station was automated in 1989. It draws tens of thousands of visitors annually and is among the most photographed lighthouses on the Northeast coast. Longfellow is said to have walked out to the light while living in Portland. The LOC significance statement records more than 60,000 visitors a year and calls it perhaps the best known and most photographed lighthouse on the northeast coast.

## 3. Then / now
- 1787: Massachusetts begins construction.
- 1790: federal completion funds appropriated.
- 10 January 1791: first lighted under U.S. government.
- 19th–20th centuries: height alterations, lens changes, keeper's house of 1891.
- 1989: automated.
- Today: active Coast Guard light; park and museum open to visitors; tower and complex intact.

## 4. What's still there
The white conical rubble-stone and brick tower with black lantern stands on the rocky headland at the entrance to Portland Harbor. The 1891 keeper's house (museum), fog-signal building, and related structures remain. Fort Williams Park surrounds the site; the rocky shoreline and Casco Bay setting match the historic views.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Enter Fort Williams Park and follow signs or paths to Portland Head Light. View the tower and house from the public overlooks and shoreline trails. If the museum is open, enter the keeper's house for exhibits on the station's history and optics. Walk the headland for views of the harbor entrance and nearby fortifications. Return via park roads.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Precise original tower height and the exact sequence of 19th-century shortenings and raisings vary slightly across secondary accounts. Contemporary construction contracts and early keeper logs were not examined for this entry. Visitor numbers are approximate.
