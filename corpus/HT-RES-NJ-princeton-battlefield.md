# HT-RES-NJ-princeton-battlefield — January 3, 1777: the field, the farmhouse, and the common grave
Division NJ seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On the morning of January 3, 1777, at the end of the ten days that saved the American Revolution, George Washington's army fought British regulars across the Clarke family's farm fields south of Princeton and won — Washington's first field victory over British troops. Princeton Battlefield State Park preserves that ground: the open fields, the Thomas Clarke House where the wounded of both sides were carried and where General Hugh Mercer died, and the common grave of British and American soldiers who were buried where the battle left them. This entry is written plainly, as the ground asks.

## 1. Trip gate
The park is held by the New Jersey State Park Service, and its terms as the Service states them govern this entry. The park address is 500 Mercer Road, Princeton, NJ 08540 (the Service's brochure gives the ZIP as 08540-4810); the park phone is 609-921-0074. The Thomas Clarke House — the park's museum and the battle's surviving building — is open Wednesday through Saturday, 10 AM to 12 PM and 1 PM to 4 PM, and Sunday, 1 PM to 4 PM, per the State Park Service's park page (checked November 26, 2026). The Service's brochure adds the house rules: no smoking, food, or beverages inside the Clarke House; no handling of artifacts; and no interior photography, videotaping, or audiotaping, so that the collections survive. Restrooms are in the carriage barn. The park and the Clarke House are partially accessible to persons with disabilities; the Service asks visitors to call 609-921-0074 for access needs, and TTY users to call the NJ Relay & CapTel Service at 711.

The Service's standing rules for the ground print in its brochure and are repeated here because they are part of visiting a burial ground respectfully: alcohol, metal detecting, smoking, and vaping are not permitted in the park (smoking and vaping only inside a personal vehicle); pets must be on a leash no longer than six feet and under the owner's control, and owners clean up after them; trash cans are not provided — visitors carry their trash out. Group and school programs are available by reservation. Emergencies and suspicious activity are reported at 1-877-WARN-DEP (1-877-927-6337) or 911. No admission fee and no grounds hours are stated in the State Park Service sources checked for this entry, and none is printed here; a visitor who needs either confirmed should call the park at the number above.

## 2. The story
People were on this land long before the battle. The State Park Service's brochure records that stone tools and arrowheads found in the park and the surrounding area show Native Americans used the land for thousands of years before European settlement. In 1696 Benjamin Clarke II purchased 1,200 acres here, settling with the families of two of his brothers-in-law; the area became known as Stony Brook Village and was then part of West Windsor, Middlesex County.

The farm the battle crossed belonged to the next generations of that family. Thomas Clarke, a Quaker farmer, acquired 200 acres in 1772 from his older brother William and built his house on it — the two-story frame house that still stands. He lived there with his sisters Sarah and Hannah and with Susannah, a Black woman enslaved by the Clarke family. The Service's brochure records what is owed to her by name: Susannah was freed from enslavement on her thirtieth birthday, in February 1779, two years after the battle fought around her home.

The battle came out of the worst weeks of the war. After the defeats of the summer and fall of 1776, the British and their German troops drove Washington's army across New Jersey and over the Delaware into Pennsylvania, then quartered troops in the towns between the Raritan and the Delaware to hold the region and stage an attack on Philadelphia in the spring. Washington, his army shrinking by desertion and expiring enlistments, crossed the icy Delaware on the night of December 25, 1776 and struck Trenton the next morning. The British fell back on Princeton and fortified the town; their force there rose to some 8,000 men, and Lord Cornwallis arrived on January 1. On January 2 Cornwallis marched for Trenton, leaving 1,200 men at Princeton and another 1,000 at Maidenhead (now Lawrenceville) — and that night Washington slipped around him, leaving a small party at Trenton as a decoy, and marched on Princeton.

The armies met just after dawn on January 3, 1777, in the Clarke fields. The National Historic Landmark nomination tells the meeting in the ground's own terms: the American column came up the Quaker Road paralleling Stony Brook and divided before the Friends Meeting House, and General Hugh Mercer's vanguard met Colonel Mawhood's regiments near the orchard where Mercer Road now runs, in the park's eastern portion. Mercer was struck down there — mortally wounded by bayonet, the nomination's continuation records. Washington himself rallied the army on the rise east of the Clarke House, and the British were driven back across Mercer Road to the Stony Brook bridge, which was destroyed on Washington's order; the present bridge is its replacement. In the town, the British garrison in Nassau Hall surrendered after American artillery fired on it. The nomination's figures for the day: British losses estimated at 400 to 600 killed, wounded, and captured; the Americans lost 30, disproportionately officers.

Then the farmhouse did the battle's second work. The Clarke House was used as a hospital for the wounded of both armies. General Mercer was carried there and attended by Dr. Benjamin Rush — a signer of the Declaration of Independence — and died in the house. The victory at Princeton closed the campaign remembered as the Ten Crucial Days, and the British withdrew toward New Brunswick. Thomas Clarke lived in his house until his death in 1802; it passed to his brother Ezekiel and then to his sister Sarah, who died in 1840. In 1944, per the State Park Service's brochure, the State of New Jersey purchased the Clarke House and the surrounding land for a state park.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** January 3, 1777 — a Quaker family's farm fields, orchard, and farmhouse, crossed at dawn by two armies; by the day's end the house was a hospital and its fields held the dead of both sides. **Now:** the fields are held open as a state park; the house stands furnished in the period of the Revolution; and the common grave of the battle's British and American dead lies under a colonnade at the field's edge, marked by a poem rather than a tally.

**Then:** the Mercer Oak stood in the field near where General Mercer fell, a living witness tree. **Now:** the oak collapsed from old age in March 2000; an offspring grown from one of its acorns in 1981 grows beside the stump of the original tree (State Park Service brochure).

**Then:** the men who fell were buried together, British and American, in one grave on the field. **Now:** the grave remains, its entrance the Ionic colonnade re-erected here in 1959 — a Philadelphia house façade of 1836, designed by Thomas U. Walter — and a poem Alfred Noyes wrote for the grave in 1916 is inlaid in bronze over it (State Park Service brochure).

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its source attached:
- The open fields of the Clarke farms — the battlefield itself, preserved as park ground (State Park Service brochure)
- The Thomas Clarke House — the original two-story half-Georgian frame house with its enlarged wing; seven rooms, furnished in the period of the Revolution (1775–1783), with much of the original flooring, moldings, and windows remaining; the wing holds exhibits on the battle and the war (State Park Service brochure)
- The carriage barn (restrooms) and the smokehouse, completing the Clarke farm group (State Park Service brochure)
- The common grave of the battle's British and American dead, with the colonnade and stone patio at its entrance and the Noyes poem inlaid in bronze (State Park Service)
- The Mercer Oak's stump and its offspring, grown from an acorn of the original tree in 1981, near Mercer Road (State Park Service brochure)
- The Princeton Battle Monument — on state park property at Stockton Road (Route 206) and Nassau Street, built on the site of British defensive works, designed by Frederick MacMonnies, and dedicated in 1922 by President Warren G. Harding (State Park Service brochure)
- The Stony Brook Friends Meeting House, adjacent to the park and still active — built 1726, rebuilt 1760 after a fire; used as barracks during the British occupation and as shelter for the wounded after the battle; its graveyard holds unmarked graves and the grave of Richard Stockton, a signer of the Declaration of Independence (State Park Service brochure). The meeting house is a living congregation's, not the park's to offer.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route. This is a battlefield and a burial ground; the progression below is a way to stand on it in the order the day happened, quietly:

1. **The open fields from Mercer Road:** Begin with the ground itself — the Clarke farm fields the armies crossed at dawn on January 3, 1777, still open under the park's care. The battle is read from the land first, before any building.
2. **The Thomas Clarke House (open hours in §1):** The farmhouse that became a hospital for both armies, where General Mercer died attended by Dr. Benjamin Rush. Seven rooms in period furnishing; the Service's house rules in §1 apply, including no interior photography.
3. **The colonnade and the common grave:** The burial place of the battle's British and American dead, together. The Noyes poem inlaid over the grave is the stop's text. This is the ground's center, and it is visited as a grave is visited.
4. **The Mercer Oak stump and its offspring:** Near Mercer Road, close to where Mercer fell — the witness tree gone in 2000, its descendant growing beside it.
5. **The Princeton Battle Monument, Stockton Road and Nassau Street:** The park's separate town parcel, on the site of the British defensive works — the battle's other end, where the garrison in Nassau Hall surrendered.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The park's size does not reconcile across the sources, and this entry does not choose. The State Park Service's current brochure says "the 85-acre park preserves the open fields of the Clarke farms." The National Historic Landmark nomination, prepared in 1975, describes a 40-acre state park within a nominated property of roughly 200 acres (its acreage line is imperfectly legible in the digitized copy). Secondary accounts commonly give 681 acres for the park. Section 2 prints no acreage; the three figures stand here with their sources.

The common grave's count conflicts inside the State Park Service's own publications. The Service's park page states that the colonnade and stone patio mark the common grave of 21 British and 15 American soldiers. The Service's brochure states that the colonnade was re-erected "as the entrance to the common grave of 21 British and 16 American soldiers killed in the Battle of Princeton." Fifteen or sixteen American dead: both forms print here, unreconciled, and §4 carries the grave without a number.

The park's founding prints two ways. The State Park Service's brochure says the State purchased the Clarke House and surrounding land in 1944. Secondary accounts give 1946 as the park's establishment, on about 40 acres. The 1944 purchase is what §2 prints, on the Service's own account; the 1946 form stands here.

The Landmark nomination itself carries errors a reader should not inherit. Its description section dates the battle "January 3, 1776" — the battle was January 3, 1777, as the nomination's own history and every other source here agree — and it dates the Trenton losses to "Christmas Eve" rather than the December 26 attack the Service's brochure describes. Both are printed here as errors in the source, not as facts.

The nomination carries UTM boundary references for the property on a continuation sheet (Zone 18), but in the digitized copy those figures are degraded past reliable transcription. Under this entry's rule, a coordinate prints only when a primary source carries it legibly and exactly; the nomination's references therefore do not print, no conversion was made, and §10's latitude and longitude stand blank. The nomination also marks the property's street and number "NOT FOR PUBLICATION" and locates it as the "southern edge of Princeton on New Jersey Route 583"; the park's public address in §1 and §10 comes from the State Park Service, which publishes it for visitors.

One more gap, stated plainly: the State Park Service sources checked do not state the year the Clarke House was built. They establish that Thomas Clarke acquired the land in 1772 and built the house after that purchase, and that it stood before and during the battle — its file record on Wikimedia Commons likewise describes it as "standing since before the time of that battle in 1777." No more precise year is printed in this entry.
