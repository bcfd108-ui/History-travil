## 2. The story

**Named historical worker: Elizabeth Langdon Williams.** Lowell's institutional exhibit describes her employment from 1905 as a human “computer,” beginning in its Boston office. It places her move to Flagstaff in **April 1919**, where calculation and correspondence continued. It states that after she married fellow employee George Hall Hamilton in 1922, Constance Lowell asked the couple to leave because of the marriage. This is the archive's historical interpretation, not a personnel file independently inspected here. [2]

**Specific primary archival record:** a handwritten letter by Williams to Percival Lowell, dated **August 15, 1910**, cataloged as **Omeka item 2**. The item's record was read. Its manuscript image and complete text were not available in the rendered record, so no firsthand quotation is invented. Importantly, this is evidence from her **Boston period**, not a letter claimed to have been written in Flagstaff. [3]

**Original interpretive voice:** Before “computer” named a machine on a desk, it could name the person doing the work. An observatory's public story often points upward, toward an object discovered. This story turns the attention back toward employment: who performed the patient intellectual labor, how their contribution entered the record, and what conditions determined whether they could keep doing it. A signature and a job title can make that work visible without turning a worker into a footnote to someone else's fame.

No mathematical procedure, instrument operation or present research workflow is reproduced. This is a labor-and-recognition history, not an astronomy operations lesson.

## 3. Then / now

The historical institution depended on people whose work can be traced through correspondence and other records. Today the public visitor program and the archival catalog offer different kinds of access. A museum experience is not the same as a research appointment, and a digital record is not proof that its original is currently exhibited. [1,3]

Lowell's online *Women in Astronomy* exhibit presents Williams alongside Wrexie Louise Leonard and Elizabeth Roemer using multiple record types. Their careers are distinct; neither Leonard's observing records nor Roemer's later career is attributed to Williams. [4]

The appropriate comparison is not “a modern machine replaced this woman.” It is how the language of work can hide the person who performed it. Do not imply that any present exhibit, current employee or modern machine corresponds directly to the 1910 letter.
