# Jimmie Rodgers Museum and the making of a recording career

*On Old Ground · Regional March · Mississippi OP12 · Labeled research draft*

## 2. Story

The record that started Jimmie Rodgers's career arrived in stores as two sides of a single Victor disc. One side was a sentimental song about a soldier. Across from it was a song with yodeling, “Sleep, Baby, Sleep.” Virginia Humanities catalogs a surviving 1927 recording under that title, identifying Rodgers as the recorded voice and Victor as the original publisher. The title is quoted from the record description. The audio itself was unavailable for listening during this check, so the record's sound and lyrics are not quoted. The Bristol museum's session list dates both sides to August 4, 1927. [3–5]

What happened in Bristol took several people. Producer Ralph Peer brought a commercial label's equipment and talent search to the region. The Birthplace of Country Music Museum identifies Anita Peer and Victor recording engineers Edward Eckhardt and Fred Lynch as part of the operation that set up the temporary studio. The museum's teaching record says Rodgers came with a group but recorded alone, and treats the company's subsequent Camden session as a distinct decision and trip. LOC identifies those sessions as part of a change in recording practice, including electrical recording that let a microphone take over work once done through an acoustic horn. [3, 4, 7, 8]

The story is also Meridian's. The Library of Congress's historical summary calls Rodgers “The Singing Brakeman,” a name connecting his public image to earlier railroad employment. The Foundation traces the festival to a public commemorative event in 1953 and now maintains the museum collection. The move to 22nd Avenue in 2021 took people and local support. At the ribbon cutting director Leslie Lee told WTOK, “We’ve had such great support from all of Meridian.” That is a contemporary news quotation about the museum move, distinct from any claim about Rodgers's own working life. [2, 6, 9]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic locations.** The first Victor session took place in Bristol. Later Victor work occurred at Camden. Meridian is a place of work, memory and commemoration in the wider Rodgers story, but source material reviewed here does not connect the present gallery address to a recording session. [3, 4, 6, 7, 9]

**Present public anchor.** Staffed Jimmie Rodgers Museum at 1200 22nd Avenue. Drive to the museum block, use lawful marked parking and walk the public sidewalk to the signed public door. Confirm the physical entrance and any step-free arrangements with the operator if those affect the trip. [1, 2, 6]

## 5. Public progression

Begin at the signed front door during posted hours. Inside, see how the exhibit pairs the railroad identity with the recording career. Where an instrument is on view, use its case label to establish whose object it is and what the museum says about it. The story lands at **The guitar and the record label**, a moment to read one musician's physical tool beside the industrial process that carried a performance out of a temporary studio. The 1927 recording page offers the companion evidence to consult before or after the museum. [1, 3–5, 7, 8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The present downtown site is explicitly confirmed by operator and contemporaneous relocation report. Catalog or map records listing a previous location describe an earlier venue or associated outdoor display and should not be used to find the current public gallery. [1, 2, 6]
- LOC's guest essay on “Blue Yodel” says Peer discovered Rodgers in North Carolina several months before Camden. The Bristol museum and LOC's Bristol essay document the August 1927 Bristol recording and conventionally attach discovery to the Bristol sessions. The accession chronology is stronger than the flexible word “discovered.” Earlier contact remains open. [3, 4, 7, 8]
- The Smithsonian artifact URL returned HTTP 520, “Unknown Status Code.” The accession image and measurements were unavailable at that endpoint. The title and recording date are supported independently by the session list and the Virginia Humanities audio record. The audio record description loaded, but the audio itself was not played. [3, 5, 10]
- The gallery guitar's inventory record, maker, service history and individual display commitments were not retrieved. Current museum materials identify a guitar of Rodgers; they do not document use at a particular recording session. [1]
