# Fort Massachusetts and the men who guarded Ship Island

*On Old Ground · Mississippi · Harrison County · Research draft for Dash · Military installation and incarceration history · Reflection only · Hunts DISABLED*

## 2. Story

In January 1863 seven companies of the Second Louisiana Native Guard took up duty on Ship Island. The men had enlisted in New Orleans. Among them were coopers, printers, masons and other tradesmen. The regiment had Black company officers when it entered federal service. On the island its companies guarded prisoners, drilled and maintained the post. Three companies received additional artillery instruction. Keeping a Union garrison here required soldiers whose authority to serve as officers their own army would soon challenge. [4, 5]

On April 9 two companies went from the island to East Pascagoula. They raised the United States flag over a hotel and returned to Ship Island that day. During the summer, military review boards and demands for resignations pushed most or all of the regiment’s officers of color out of their positions. The surviving paperwork also changes the regiment’s name. In June 1863 it became the Second Regiment, Corps d’Afrique. In April 1864 it became the 74th United States Colored Infantry. Those names lead to records of the same men at different dates. [4, 5]

The island had a working garrison before it had a finished brick fort. Lieutenant Frederick E. Prime ordered brick from Jules Blanc’s yard in New Orleans. The state militia took the site in 1861. Federal occupation resumed that September. Under Lieutenant John C. Palfrey the construction force included paid workers from New York and prisoners. The military engineers left a particularly good check on later accounts. Their plans show the state of the work on June 30 in **each year from 1862 through 1865**. Prison custody, construction work and military service were different experiences on the same island. The records do not assign any specific surviving arch to an individual worker. [6–8]

The population in custody also changed. Civilian detainees were sent from New Orleans in 1862, and Union soldiers convicted of offenses were held here. The large transfer of Confederate prisoners came in October 1864. A later photograph of soldiers posed at a gun has often supplied the kind of face missing from written accounts, but its Library of Congress record identifies the **39th U.S. Infantry in 1868–69**. It depicts a later garrison. It cannot supply a portrait of the Second Louisiana Native Guard on duty in 1863. [5, 9]

## 3. Then and now

The deep-water approach gave the island its military importance. A ferry passenger today reaches an NPS visitor site with masonry walls, covered rooms and a parade ground. In 1863 soldiers were already standing guard while construction continued. In 2005 Hurricane Katrina drove debris into the same arched spaces. The inspected NPS photograph records the condition after the storm. The pile on its floor belongs to 2005. [1, 5–8, 10]

For a winter reading, start with the 1863 and 1865 engineers’ plans in illustrations 4 and 6 of the federal report. Both carry the same reporting date, June 30, two years apart. Read the regiment’s duty record beside them. In season, the same comparison can be made at the staffed fort. The accessible extent depends on which spaces the park opens. This is a quiet-witness stop. No shore, battle or prison-site route is proposed. [1–4, 6–8, 13]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Completion date.** Mississippi’s preservation inventory gives a construction field of 1856–62 and says in its narrative that the fort was complete in 1863. The NPS fort history gives 1866. Its ferry departure page gives 1868. The engineers’ later plans and correspondence show work after 1863. They do not make those three public dates interchangeable. No single completion year is assigned here. [3, 6–8]
- **Prison chronology.** James G. Hollandsworth Jr. reports that no Confederate, Union or civilian prisoners remained on June 8, 1865. The state inventory describes federal prison use until 1870. Those statements refer to different intervals. The later admissions or administrative records that could connect them were not established. [5, 8]
- **Firsthand voice.** Colonel Nathan W. Daniels’s diary survives at the Library of Congress. The volume I image viewer was inaccessible on this check. No unverified passage is quoted or attributed to the enlisted men. Their individual accounts remain a gap. [4, 14]
- **Tier 1 scheduling signal.** NPS reported 7,576,923 recreation visits to the entire two-state seashore in 2025. That number includes Florida. A standalone Ship Island or fort attendance figure was not established here. No independent coordinate pair was verified for the fort’s public entrance. [1, 12]
