# Walnut Ridge Army Air Field and Wings of Honor Museum
HT-RES-AR-walnut-ridge-rail-and-airfield-workers · Arkansas OP05 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

James T. Kenny had made a couple of night landings with his instructor before being sent out on his own. In his February 11, 1995, Rutgers oral history, he put the scene at Walnut Ridge. As he turned to line up, “right over top of me about ten feet was this airplane.” He remembered that the other aircraft had lost electrical power and could not contact the tower. After the near miss, a voice on the radio indicated someone was flying in a section he should not have been in. Kenny suspected it was him. His interview describes the work of an individual student and his own response to a demanding training system. It supplies no incident report or second account. [2, printed p. 16]

Kenny also recalled reaching the field in an Army truck and stepping down into mud. He thought the earlier part of that trip had been by train and could not remember where he was let off. Rail and road helped move him into a substantial new institution. The commercial district nomination documents the railroad’s role in shaping the town and separately describes the federal airfield as a second expansion. Its plans provided for barracks and services as well as runways. Its aerial illustration shows the field’s new layout within farmland. A traveler can follow that transformation in the documents while keeping the working transportation systems outside the visit. [2–4]

The museum describes Walnut Ridge as a basic flying school using the Vultee BT-13, with graduates proceeding to advanced schools. A student had to learn a sequence of procedures and take account of other aircraft, instructors, mechanics and controllers. The remembered clearance and the aircraft Kenny described without lights give a human scale to the organizational chart. No aircraft displayed today is identified as the one in his account. [1–3]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The wartime airfield was built outside the town. The modern museum provides a public approach on the landside of the present airport area. The commercial district is a distinct historic registration. Kenny’s arrival platform, exact runway and night training position are unresolved. [1–5]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Wings of Honor Museum, 70 Beacon Road, Walnut Ridge. Drive to the signed public museum approach and take the short walk to the entrance during public hours. Keep airport fencing, equipment yards, ramps and runways outside the visit. The downtown rail corridor and platforms remain outside this dossier’s progression. [1, 4, 5]

## 5. Public progression

Arrive at the landside museum entrance. Begin with the training exhibits and read the distinctions between basic and advanced flying. Use the surviving 1942 aerial reproduction as document context when available, then read Kenny’s transcribed interview. **Strongest story moment · A voice on the radio.** From the gallery, consider how a student who had lost track of his assigned section heard the tower trying to put the pattern back in order. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The nomination, repeated by the museum chronology, places activation in August 1942 and the first arrival of trainees in October 1942. The museum’s Cadet Training page puts the basic school start in October 1943. A signed class-start or station record is needed to reconcile the year. [3, 4, 6]
- The interview’s remembered clearance, approximate separation and darkness are retrospective. A corroborating incident report, class roll and named mechanics remain open. Kenny is not placed at a specific depot. [2]
- The registration is for the Walnut Ridge Commercial Historic District. It contextualizes airport construction without supplying a verified airfield or exhibit identifier. [4, 5]
