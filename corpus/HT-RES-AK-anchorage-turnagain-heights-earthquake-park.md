# HT-RES-AK-anchorage-turnagain-heights-earthquake-park — Anchorage: the Turnagain Heights slide and the park that keeps its ground
Division AK seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

At 5:36 p.m. on Good Friday, March 27, 1964, the magnitude 9.2 earthquake shook south-central Alaska for roughly four and a half minutes. At Turnagain Heights in Anchorage, a residential bluff resting on weak layers of the Bootlegger Cove Clay failed: the slide's western lobe is now Earthquake Park, where the broken ground itself is the exhibit. This entry is reflection-first disaster ground.

## 1. Trip gate
Checked November 25, 2026. The lawful public anchor is the Municipality of Anchorage's Earthquake Park, reached from West Northern Lights Boulevard. The municipal project record identifies a parking lot, a connecting trail, interpretive panels, a Cook Inlet overlook and a connection to the Tony Knowles Coastal Trail. The park preserves part of the western lobe of the Turnagain Heights landslide. This entry does not direct visitors into adjacent residential land or down unstable coastal bluffs. Posted closures, trail conditions, ice and erosion govern any visit.

## 2. The story
At 5:36 p.m. Alaska Standard Time on Good Friday, March 27, 1964, the earth began moving beneath south-central Alaska. The magnitude 9.2 earthquake continued for roughly four and a half minutes. Anchorage stood about eighty miles west-northwest of the epicenter, but local ground conditions determined much of the damage. At Turnagain Heights, a flat residential bluff rested above weak layers within the Bootlegger Cove Clay. The slide began after shaking had already continued for about two minutes and kept moving after the most noticeable shaking stopped.

The western and eastern slide lobes together ran roughly 2.6 kilometers along Knik Arm. The more developed eastern lobe destroyed about seventy-five houses and took four lives. The western lobe, now largely Earthquake Park, moved and rotated great blocks of ground into a broken field of ridges, depressions and fissures. Later erosion softened some of that geometry, but the land still records the failure.

Robert B. Atwood, editor and publisher of the *Anchorage Daily Times*, was in Turnagain Heights. His published account describes walls weaving, trees falling and his own house moving away. He escaped across ground that broke into chasms and tilted blocks, then helped a neighbor reach safety. His words matter because the slide is not only a geologic case study. It was a neighborhood coming apart under the people who lived there.

## 3. Then / now
Before the earthquake, this was a bluff-top residential district. During the slide, parts of the pre-earthquake surface disintegrated and dropped while other blocks rotated or traveled outward. The modern park keeps open land over the western lobe and interprets the disaster from a public trail and overlook. Houses still stand outside the park boundary; they are not exhibits and no visitor route extends onto their lots.

Earthquake Park is also evidence of a change in public understanding. Hansen's post-disaster investigation tied the Anchorage slides to the engineering behavior of the Bootlegger Cove Clay and associated sand layers. The Alaska Earthquake Center uses the same ground today to explain why similar shaking can produce very different results only short distances apart.

## 4. What's still there
- Western slide terrain — uneven ground and the broad disturbed landscape preserved within the park. The sharpest 1964 relief has been reduced by erosion and vegetation.
- Interpretive area — municipal records identify interpretive panels and an overlook reached by the connecting trail. Panel condition and visibility require a dated field check.
- Cook Inlet / Knik Arm view — the overlook gives the spatial relationship between the bluff, water and the direction of failure. Stay behind barriers.
- Trail connections — a public connection leads toward the Tony Knowles Coastal Trail. It does not establish permission to leave maintained public routes.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Begin at the signed Earthquake Park parking area. Read the posted interpretation before walking toward the overlook. From the public path, compare the remaining broken relief with the Hansen photograph: the archival frame shows a newly destroyed neighborhood, while the park shows what six decades of erosion, vegetation and public management have left. At the overlook, read the bluff and water together. The slide moved outward toward Knik Arm; it was not simply a set of houses shaken off their foundations. Return on the marked trail.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The USGS and Alaska Earthquake Center consistently describe about seventy-five houses destroyed at Turnagain Heights. This entry does not assign a house-by-house total to the western park lobe.

The Alaska Earthquake Center reports 131 earthquake-linked deaths across the event, while this entry prints the four deaths assigned by the state field guide to the developed Turnagain Heights lobe. These are different scopes, not competing totals.

The municipal capital-project page records requests for trail rehabilitation, erosion control, lighting and security cameras. It does not by itself establish that work is funded or completed. Current on-site notices control.
