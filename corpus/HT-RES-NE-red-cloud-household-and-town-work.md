## 2. The story

### A room small enough to work in

Writing to her brother Roscoe in 1945, Willa Cather remembered her Red Cloud attic as “my ‘rose bower’ in our old first house.” Read the phrase in the state conservation team's published transcription. She was writing from Maine and comparing a sloped ceiling there with the small room she remembered. The letter gives the stored memory of a person who knew the space. The conservation account gives the evidence that the room could be read materially. It reports surviving wallpaper and a north-facing window whose light faded it. Conservators filled losses with new toned paper to protect the edges of surviving original material. [3]

The house became the family's town home in 1884. The operator places Cather's own residence there through her departure for study in 1890 and describes a longer tenure for the household. Family testimony mattered to the building's survival. Mildred and Wilbur Bennett bought it in 1960. Mildred's notes drew on recollections from Cather's sisters and Carrie Miner Sherwood about how the family arranged and used the rooms. During later work a surviving cubbyhole panel helped test those recollections against the building. The attic door and a missing ceiling were reconstructed. The wallpaper tells a different story of conservation, including original paper alongside later fills. [2–4]

### A local story moves from kitchen to newspaper to scrapbook

In 1946 Sherwood wrote about an early Red Cloud Thanksgiving, based on an account from an aunt. The Center reproduces the piece from *The Commercial Advertiser*, with its account of neighbors from Guide Rock, young hunters, garden produce and a meal prepared for about thirty people. Those details reached print through family memory, decades after the claimed 1870 event. The Center also reproduces Cather's December 18, 1946 reply: “I have put your article in my scrap book.” The response records a writer preserving a neighbor's work. It does not make her an eyewitness to the earlier meal. [5,6]

The Center distinguishes late holidays Cather remembered at the family's second Red Cloud home from the town's earlier gathering and the Childhood Home now on tour. These locations and occasions belong to separate stories. The most useful way to visit is to read the household testimony beside the objects and then ask what later cataloging and restoration had to do to make the room understandable again. [2–6]

## 3. Then / now

The house once held an active family household. The Foundation interprets it as a historic home and maintains archives and exhibitions at its separate Center. The attic retains historical material with conservation fills, selected reconstruction and an interpretation informed by recollections. The second home is a separate building and is unnecessary for this progression. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Foundation tour and archives give 1879 as the construction date. The state historical marker page gives ca. 1876. A contemporaneous construction record was not retrieved. The operator's 1884–1890 span for Willa's residence and the family's longer interval have distinct subjects. [2,4,7]
- The Center and local tourism pages disagree on Saturday closing time and the start of winter hours. The Foundation's dated holiday and hours page controls this draft. Tours are separately booked. [1,2,8]
- The UNL letter URL initially displayed its cataloged letter heading, then returned an internal retrieval error during direct read/find. The Center's reproduction supplied the line quoted here. Sherwood's aunt's separate original account was not retrieved. [5,6]
- Public Center hours do not grant access to archival material. The Foundation posts separate conditions for collections research. [10]
