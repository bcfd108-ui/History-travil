## 2. The story

In a 2023 published interview, **Elizabeth Woody**, the museum's executive director, responds to a young visitor recognizing things from his own family experience in the exhibits. For her, that recognition demonstrated authenticity. Later she explains that Tribal members helped shape the building's design, rather than merely providing objects to fill someone else's museum. Her concise description of the collections is the strongest moment: **“These are living objects, they’re not things that are static.”** [4]

This is Woody's named first-person interview in OPB's published transcript, which OPB labels computer-generated and volunteer-edited. The transcript was read; the audio was not independently heard. The passage concerns community care and conservation. It does not authorize visitors to handle objects.

The story continues in work being made now. The museum lists the **33rd Annual Warm Springs Tribal Member and Youth Exhibit**, November 18, 2026–February 20, 2027. This is the current published exhibition window, not independent confirmation of every installed work or of yesterday's reception having occurred. [1]

A separate museum newsletter records a September 2026 poetry program. Woody describes inviting young Warm Springs people to reflect on heritage and becoming through **Joy Harjo's** work. Harjo is identified as **Muscogee Nation**, a visiting artist, not a member of one of the three Warm Springs Tribes. The event is past, not an invitation to enter the youth center now. [5]

## 3. Then / now

The Tribal government's history distinguishes the **Wasco**, associated with Chinookan language, Columbia River fishing and trade; **Warm Springs bands**, associated with Sahaptin language and seasonal movement; and **Paiute bands**, whose southeastern Oregon history and language differed. These are the source's broad historical descriptions, not universal statements about every person today. [6]

The same account identifies the Wasco and Warm Springs treaty of 1855 and Paiute settlement beginning in 1879 after forced removals. It dates organization of the three Tribes as the Confederated Tribes to 1937. Do not describe all three as identical parties to the 1855 treaty. [6]

The museum's fact sheet explains a community effort to retain material culture as objects left for collectors and outside institutions, followed by a Tribal construction appropriation approved in 1988. The resulting museum is a deliberate act of cultural stewardship, not simply a roadside collection of old things. [7]

**Now:** historical collections and contemporary community art share the interpretive setting. This draft does not identify a particular historical object as currently on view, or promise an operating audio station.

## 4. What's still there

- **Public cultural institution:** The Museum at Warm Springs, with permanent and changing galleries. Its fact sheet describes governance through a Board of Directors and Board of Regents; neither a staff list nor public admission grants research or publication permission. [7]
- **Closed outdoor component:** all outdoor trails are expressly closed. Historic descriptions of creekside walks are not clearance to use them. [1]
- **Restricted or unverified components:** archives, storage, staff rooms, classrooms and event spaces are not automatically included in ordinary admission.
- **Living community:** the museum is not the boundary of contemporary Tribal life. Conversely, learning about that life here does not turn residences or ceremonies into attractions.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Suggested interpretation sequence, not a surveyed walking route or timed gallery plan:

1. **Arrive with the clock in mind.** Use either side of the lunch break; do not plan a noon entry. Check actual signs for closures before leaving the public arrival area.
2. **Start with difference.** Use the museum's own interpretation and the Tribal history source to recognize three distinct histories within the confederation.
3. **Look for community authorship.** Read object labels for names, provenance and attribution. Do not assume a displayed object is anonymous, communal property or free to copy.
4. **Bring the story forward.** If installed and open as advertised, visit the Tribal Member and Youth Exhibit. Read artists as present-day makers, not examples of a vanished culture.
5. **End with Woody's thought in §2.** Reflect on what changes when collections remain connected to families and community care. This is the strongest narrative moment, not a hunt for restricted objects.

If weather or closure prevents entry, use the published sources remotely. Do not substitute the closed trails or another community building.
