# Jane Addams Hull-House Museum — the Hull-House settlement
HT-RES-IL-jane-addams-hull-house-museum · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The house came first. Charles J. Hull built his Italianate residence in 1856, when Halsted and Polk stood in a fashionable district. Hull died in 1889 and left the house to his cousin and assistant Helen Culver. By then the neighborhood around it had become one of the densest immigrant districts in the fastest-growing city in the world — Czech, German, Greek, Irish, Italian, Polish, and Russian families, and in later decades Mexican and African American communities as well. [2, 3]

On September 18, 1889, Jane Addams and Ellen Gates Starr moved in, with Mary Keyser, who had handled domestic work for the Addams family and became an important liaison with the neighborhood. Culver was the settlement's first supporter. Addams and Starr, college friends who had visited Toynbee Hall in London, set out in Starr's phrase to pursue "the scheme" — to live *with*, not *for*, their neighbors, in solidarity with immigrant communities the museum's history describes as neglected by the city and exploited by capitalism. [2, 4]

What they built outgrew the house. Hull-House became, in the museum's words, the country's most influential social settlement, expanding to thirteen buildings offering kindergarten and day care, an employment bureau, an art gallery, libraries, English and citizenship courses, and theater, music, and art classes. The residents around Addams — among them Florence Kelley, Alice Hamilton, and Julia Lathrop in the wider record — championed immigrant rights and public health, worked to end child labor, helped institute juvenile justice, supported unions, built playgrounds, and advocated for public housing. The Social Welfare History Project, drawing on Harvard's library record, credits the Hull-House circle with launching the Immigrants' Protective League and the Juvenile Protective Association work behind the first juvenile court in the United States, and with protective legislation for women and children, child-labor regulation, and compulsory-education laws. Addams, a founder in the national child-labor and peace movements, became in 1931 the first American woman to receive the Nobel Peace Prize. She died in 1935. [2, 4, 6, 8]

The ending of the complex is part of the record, not an epilogue. In 1963, despite resistance from neighborhood residents and activists across the nation, the City of Chicago demolished most of the Hull-House settlement and the surrounding neighborhood for Mayor Richard J. Daley's urban-renewal project, including the construction of the University of Illinois Chicago. Two buildings survived. The Hull-House Association itself continued elsewhere and ceased operations in January 2012; the surviving house and dining hall are the museum. [2, 3]

**Lore, labeled as lore.** Hull-House carries two famous tellings, and both are documented as tellings. Addams herself recorded the first: earlier tenants held the house to be haunted by Mrs. Hull, and left a pitcher of water at the stairs in the belief a spirit could not cross it — a custom Addams reported in *Twenty Years at Hull-House* (1910). The second is the Devil Baby: in 1913 crowds came to the door demanding to see a deformed child they believed Addams had locked in the attic. No such child existed; Addams and her staff said so at the time, and in her October 1916 essay "The Devil Baby at Hull-House" she recorded the versions visitors brought and read the legend as a vehicle through which older women spoke about bereavement, abuse, and children they could not protect. The documented record is the crowd, the denial, and Addams's analysis — the child is lore. [7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The Hull-House complex at its peak covered thirteen buildings on the Near West Side around Halsted and Polk. That neighborhood fabric was largely demolished in 1963 for the UIC campus. [2, 3]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Jane Addams Hull-House Museum, 800 S. Halsted St., Chicago — the surviving Hull Home (a National Historic Landmark) and the Residents' Dining Hall, joined on a small parcel at the eastern edge of the UIC campus. The nomination records that the dining hall, the only one of twelve additional settlement structures preserved, was moved from its original position and realigned to fit the parcel, and that the house exterior was restored toward its ca. 1856 appearance while the interior was restored toward its post-1890 settlement appearance. Read the survival itself — two buildings of thirteen, inside a university that replaced a neighborhood — as part of the exhibit ground. [1, 3]

## 5. Public progression

Enter through the Hull Home and take the rooms in order: the 1856 house first as a house, then as a workplace — kindergarten, employment bureau, classes, and campaigns all ran from domestic-scale rooms. Cross to the Dining Hall, the settlement's communal table, moved to survive. **Strongest story moment · Two buildings of thirteen.** The settlement that taught a city how to care for its newcomers could not save its own street; what stands is both the achievement and the loss, in the same small parcel. Keep the lore in its place at the end of the visit: the pitcher of water and the Devil Baby crowds are evidence about the neighborhood's fears and hopes, told and labeled as Addams herself told them. [1–3, 7]
