# HT-RES-RI-paw-slater-mill — Pawtucket: the mill where American industry started spinning
Division RI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Samuel Slater arrived in Pawtucket in December 1789, an English textile mechanic carrying in his memory the machine designs Britain forbade him to export. Within a year, working with Pawtucket blacksmiths and carpenters, he had water-powered machines carding and spinning cotton in America for the first time — worked, on the first run, by nine children aged 7 to 12. The wooden mill built for the work in 1793 still stands at Pawtucket Falls on the Blackstone River, restored to its 1830s appearance, and is now run by the National Park Service.

## 1. Trip gate
Checked November 26, 2026, against the National Park Service's own pages for Blackstone River Valley National Historical Park. Slater Mill stands at 67 Roosevelt Avenue, Pawtucket, RI 02860 (park phone 401-725-8638). The grounds around the three historic buildings are open daily from dawn to dusk, year round, with interpretive panels across the grounds. Inside Slater Mill is a visitor information station with exhibits and an 8-minute introductory video; ranger-led guided tours run seasonally — spring (April 24 – May 16) Fridays and Saturdays, summer (May 17 – October 31) Thursdays through Sundays, fall (November 1 – 28) Fridays and Saturdays, at 10:30 A.M., 12:30 P.M. and 2:30 P.M. From November 29, 2026 the station closes for winter and tours run by appointment only, while the grounds stay open dawn to dusk. The Wilkinson Mill and the Sylvanus Brown House are closed to the public at this time and are seen from the grounds only. No entrance pass or admission fee applies. Tours are modified or canceled when the heat index exceeds 87 °F; NPS directs visitors to call ahead.

## 2. The story
Pawtucket was an ironworking center before it was a textile town. Artisans had worked forges and mills at Pawtucket Falls for generations, and dams stood at the falls from 1718 and 1792 — the waterpower and the metalworking skill were both in place before cotton arrived.

In Providence, merchant Moses Brown backed an attempt at machine spinning and hired Slater, who had spent seven years in an English textile mill rising from apprentice to overseer. Slater judged Brown's existing machines unworkable. Working with local mechanics — Oziel and David Wilkinson on the metalwork, Sylvanus Brown (no relation to Moses) on wooden patterns and parts — he got new machines running in December 1790. For the first time, water-powered machines produced cotton thread in America. The first workforce was nine children between the ages of 7 and 12, among the first hourly wage earners in the country.

In 1793 the firm of Almy, Brown and Slater replaced the experimental workshop with a purpose-built mill — the first successful water-powered cotton spinning mill in America. Its machinery was driven by a waterwheel set to one side of the building. The mill was expanded six times during the 1800s, worked as a textile mill until 1895, and stayed in industrial use until 1920.

The Wilkinson works are the other half of the ground. Oziel Wilkinson moved his family's blacksmithing operations to the falls in 1783; his son David developed a screw-cutting lathe (patented 1798) and worked at the edge of early machine tools, mechanized looms and experimental steam power. By 1810 the Wilkinsons had built a three-and-a-half-story rubble-stone mill beside the cotton factory; its first-floor machine shop became a training ground for the next generation of American machinists, and David installed a steam engine there as auxiliary power, one of the earliest in the Americas. The Wilkinson Mill was named a National Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark in 1977.

Slater Mill's success set off a chain reaction along the Blackstone River — mill villages rose up and down the valley in Rhode Island and Massachusetts. That valley-wide story is why the site now sits inside Blackstone River Valley National Historical Park, established in 2014.

Preservation came early. The Old Slater Mill Association was founded in 1921 to restore the mill, restored the building in 1924 to its 1830s external appearance, and opened the museum in 1955. The property was designated a National Historic Landmark and listed on the National Register on November 13, 1966 — the first property added to the National Register. In March 2021 the association donated the mill, the Wilkinson Mill, the Sylvanus Brown House and the grounds to the National Park Service.

## 3. Then / now
In Slater's day the falls were crowded: a whole community of mills, shops and houses lined both sides of the river at Pawtucket. Today three buildings survive — the 1793 mill with its additions, the 1810–11 stone Wilkinson Mill, and the pattern-maker's house — set in 4.23 acres of parkland around the two early dams, with a partially reconstructed raceway and tailrace, stone and concrete river walls, remnant foundations and a footbridge. Modern development and urban renewal removed everything else. The valley's intact mill villages survive elsewhere in the park, at places such as Slatersville and Ashton; Pawtucket keeps the beginning.

## 4. What's still there
- Slater Mill (1793) — the original 1793 section stands in about the middle of the enlarged building, with additions of 1801, about 1817 and about 1830, restored in 1924 to the 1830s appearance. It houses the visitor contact station and exhibits.
- Wilkinson Mill (1810–1811) — three-and-a-half-story stone mill. Its first-floor machine shop, about 70 by 30 feet, remains full of machines driven by line shafts and belts; below, in a stone-walled room, sits the waterwheel that drove them. Closed to the public at this time; NPS publishes a virtual tour of the interior online. The park's administrative office is in this building.
- Sylvanus Brown House (1758) — the pattern-maker's house, built elsewhere and moved to the site in 1972. Closed to the public at this time.
- The river setting — the dams at Pawtucket Falls on the Blackstone River, the raceway and tailrace, and interpretive panels across the grounds.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Start on the grounds, which are open dawn to dusk every day of the year: read the falls, the dams and the raceway first, then the three buildings as a set — the wooden cotton mill, the stone machine-shop mill beside it, the pattern-maker's house. If the visitor station is open, take the 8-minute introductory video and a ranger-led tour of Slater Mill (tours at 10:30 A.M., 12:30 P.M. and 2:30 P.M. on scheduled days). The Wilkinson Mill's machine shop and waterwheel cannot be entered at this time; the NPS virtual tour carries that interior. In winter (from November 29, 2026) the station is closed and tours are by appointment only — a visitor who has not booked ahead gets the grounds, the exteriors and the panels. Call 401-725-8638 for the latest information before going.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Two of the three buildings are closed. The Wilkinson Mill's machine shop and waterwheel — arguably the most evocative machinery on the site — and the interior of the Sylvanus Brown House cannot be entered at this time, and no reopening date is established from the pages checked.

The 1793 mill is a restoration, not a time capsule: the building a visitor walks through is the 1793 core wrapped in additions of 1801, about 1817 and about 1830, restored in 1924 to present the mill's 1830s appearance.

The Brown House was moved here. It is Sylvanus Brown's real house, but it was built elsewhere and moved to the site in 1972; it did not stand beside the mill in Slater's day. Sources also differ on its date — the Department of the Interior's 2021 release gives "ca. 1753" where NPS park pages and the Cultural Landscape Inventory give 1758. Both dates are printed here rather than resolved.

Whether NPS acquisition of the Slater Mill dam, anticipated in a January 2021 Senate release, has been completed is not established from the NPS pages checked. Tour length, reservation requirements for individual visitors, parking arrangements and interior accessibility details are likewise not established from the operator's pages and are left blank rather than guessed.
