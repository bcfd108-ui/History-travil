## 2. The story
**Reflection only. No game, clue, find, timed challenge, disaster reenactment or photo recreation.** Visitors may stop reading at any time. The subject is survival, loss and the long aftermath, not spectacle.

**Named published primary evidence:** Yellowstone Public Radio reporter Orlinda Worthington interviewed survivor **Anita Thon** in a piece published August 19, 2024. The published text identifies Thon as twelve during the 1959 earthquake and includes her own recollections. Text was read directly; the six-minute audio was not played. [3]

Thon describes confusion during the event, concern for her family and the continuing emotional burden of surviving. Her brief words **“You’re grateful you're alive”** are a useful starting point, provided they are not isolated into a cheerful ending: her surrounding statement also expresses guilt. The report says she first returned on the fiftieth anniversary, when she first publicly shared her account. [3]

This is retrospective testimony mediated by a reporter, not a contemporaneous diary or a clinical diagnosis. Her family's interpretation of a premonition is a remembered belief, not scientific evidence of earthquake prediction. No graphic injury details are needed to convey the meaning of her account.

**Original interpretive voice:** A landscape can look settled long before a person's memory does. Listen without asking the survivor to give us either a thrilling escape story or a reassuring conclusion. The purpose of this stop is not to finish her experience for her. It is to leave room for the parts that continued after the shaking ended.

USGS dates the earthquake to August 17, 1959, at 11:37 p.m. local time and identifies it as magnitude 7.3. Its historical account explains that the associated landslide caused fatalities and created Earthquake Lake. [4] This provides event context, not a present hazard forecast or guidance for entering the affected terrain.

No claims about all survivors' feelings follow from one person's words. Family history, emergency response and geological history overlap here but are not interchangeable. The presence of one published account does not authorize contacting survivors, locating relatives or investigating private grief.

## 3. Then / now
- **Then:** the event disrupted ordinary family travel. The later interview preserves one survivor's account rather than a complete collective memory.
- **Now:** visitors can learn through published testimony without standing at the precise location of loss.
- **Different evidence:** a scientific chronology establishes an event framework; testimony conveys what the experience and its aftermath meant to an individual. Neither should be used to erase the other.

Original reflection prompt: *What does it mean to listen without requiring a story to become a lesson or a victory?* Silence is a complete response. Nothing must be discovered, collected, photographed or entered as an answer.
