# Spokane, August 4, 1889: the night downtown burned, and the brick city that answered

Research draft for Dash QC • 2026-11-18 • local only, no upload or outreach.

**Trip gate:** This is a downtown walking entry on public sidewalks, runnable in any season and any weather a visitor accepts — no gates, no hours, no tickets for the core route. The one interior, the Davenport Hotel, is an operating hotel whose lobby and public rooms receive visitors daily; the city's own walking tour directs visitors upstairs to the restored Hall of Doges. The Fire of 1889 plaque stands at North Lincoln Street and Railroad Alley, at the theorized origin point. Winter daylight is short and sidewalks can be icy; the route below is compact by design. Local time: Pacific. [K3]

## 1. The story

Spokane Falls — the city's name until 1891 — was a three-year-old boomtown's downtown stacked in wood when the fire came. HistoryLink's essay on the fire, built from the contemporary record, gives the shape of the evening: on Sunday, August 4, 1889, shortly after 6 p.m., fire broke out "in an area of flimsy wooden structures and quickly spread to engulf the substantial stone and brick buildings of the business district." The most credible and enduring account of the origin places it at Wolfe's lunchroom and lodgings, opposite the Northern Pacific Depot on Railroad Avenue. The flames raced through the makeshift buildings near the tracks, and then did the thing the town had believed impossible: "The nearby Pacific Hotel, a fine new structure of brick and granite, was soon engulfed in the wall of fire advancing on the business center." Brick was not yet a defense — the fire was hot enough and the wind right enough that material alone did not save buildings. [K1]

The firefight failed on water, not courage. The city's new water system did not deliver pressure to the hoses — a pump-station failure at the fire's critical hour — and blame settled, in the way of such nights, on a man: Rolla A. Jones, the water superintendent, said to have gone fishing. HistoryLink treats the assignment of blame as part of the event's mythology — its essay is explicit that "accounts of the fire's origin and assignment of blame for its catastrophic expansion illustrate how historical myths begin and are perpetuated." Dynamite was used to blow buildings for a firebreak; the flames jumped the gaps and made their own firestorm. In a few hours, 32 square blocks — virtually the entire downtown — were destroyed. One person died. The National Guard came in to guard the bank vaults and safes standing in the ruins, Mayor Fred Furth warned against price gouging, and business resumed in a tent city in which even the *Spokane Daily Chronicle* published from a tent. [K1]

The rebuild is the second half of the event, and it was a policy as much as a construction boom. The city prohibited wooden structures in and near the new downtown, installed an electric fire alarm system, and established a professional, paid fire department. Insured owners rebuilt quickly in brick and stone; within a year the population had risen to 20,000, and in March 1891 the voters adopted a charter that dropped "Falls" from the city's name. HistoryLink's thumbnail history calls it the "phoenix effect": post-fire Spokane "bore the stamp of Kirtland K. Cutter and other distinguished architects and was soon regarded as the finest city between Minneapolis and Seattle." A Dutch mortgage bank, the Northwestern and Pacific Hypotheekbank, financed many of the post-fire buildings — Spokane's brick downtown is partly Amsterdam's money, a fact the buildings themselves do not announce. [K1, K2]

The Davenport Hotel is where the rebuild generation proved itself, and the National Register nomination makes the fire the direct cause of both of its makers' careers. Louis Davenport arrived in Spokane Falls in March 1889 "with $1.25 in his pocket"; the nomination records that "the great fire of August, 1889 which destroyed the business section of Spokane enabled Davenport to earn $125, enough to purchase a tent, waffle iron and a sign" — Davenport's Waffle Foundry, the seed of a restaurant empire that by July 10, 1890 had moved into a brick building on Sprague Avenue. Of the architect: "Like Davenport, the fire of 1889 enabled young Cutter to start his career when there was a demand for his services." Twenty-five years after the fire, the two men's hotel opened on the fire ground itself: business opened September 1, 1914, with grand-opening celebrations September 17–19, 1914 — a $2 million, 400-room hotel by Kirtland Cutter that incorporated fabric of the block's 1889–90 rebuilding, including the retained Marie Antoinette Room from the Pfister Block. The nomination's verdict stands as the entry's: "The Davenport has been Spokane's leading hotel since its completion in 1914 and the city's leading restaurant since 1890." The hotel closed in 1985, stood vacant for years, and was bought and restored by Walt and Karen Worthy, reopening July 15, 2002 — the rebuild generation's monument, itself rebuilt. [K4, K6]

The pairing with Wallace belongs in the entry: thirteen months after Spokane, fire took Wallace, Idaho (1890), and Wallace answered the same way — a downtown rebuilt in brick that still stands as the town's historic core (see Wave 1's Wallace entry). Two towns, two fires, one regional lesson learned in masonry within a year. Spokane's is the bigger canvas; Wallace's is the more complete survival.

## 2. Then / now

Then: a wooden railroad town whose one great night erased 32 blocks and whose brick showpieces burned anyway. Now: the fire's footprint is Spokane's historic downtown — the post-1889 generation of brick and stone commercial buildings is precisely what survives and is listed, because the rebuild ordinance made the second downtown out of material that could last. The clearest then/now instrument on the ground is the city's own heritage walking tour, whose central-downtown loop threads the plaque at the fire's theorized start, the 1890 first-wave rebuild blocks, and the Davenport. What does not survive is equally instructive: the Pacific Hotel, the fire's most famous brick victim, is gone, and the Great Northern depot that the rebuild era produced was demolished for Expo '74, leaving its clock tower standing alone in Riverfront Park — a second erasure, by choice rather than fire. [K1, K3]

## 3. Surviving features and access classification

All stops are exterior, public-sidewalk viewing unless noted. Register tags are the City of Spokane walking tour's own: (NR) National Register, (SR) Spokane Register. [K3]

- **Fire of 1889 Plaque, N. Lincoln Street & Railroad Alley:** the tour's stop 14, marking where "the fire is theorized to have started when a kitchen fire grew out of control." Public sidewalk. Note the tour's own hedge — *theorized* — and keep it in the entry's wording. [K3]
- **Bennett Block, 206 N. Howard Street (1890):** erected the year after the fire; one of the first-wave rebuild buildings, later an early adaptive-reuse project in the 1970s. [K3]
- **Whitten Block, 1 N. Post Street (1890; NR & SR):** "one of the buildings constructed in 1890, after the fire," designed by L. M. Boardman. [K3]
- **Miller Building (Hotel Lusso), 806 & 810 W. Sprague Avenue (1890; NR & SR):** W. J. Carpenter's sandstone-detailed 1890 building for an ice-company co-owner; rehabilitated as a hotel in the 1990s — its lobby is a working hotel lobby, entered as a guest of the business. [K3]
- **Peyton Building, 722 W. Sprague Avenue (NR & SR):** the tour notes its Riverside front "was one of the largest buildings constructed after the 1889 fire" — and that the original building itself burned in 1898, killing eight people in the upper-floor apartments, after which Colonel Isaac N. Peyton rebuilt on the remaining walls and later added two stories and a second building. A rebuild-era building with its own fire inside the story: brick reduced risk; it did not abolish it. [K3]
- **The Crescent / Review Building block, W. Riverside Avenue:** the Crescent department store "opened its doors the morning after the 1889 fire in a crescent-shaped building on West Riverside Avenue, next to the Review Building" — the Review Building (1890) is among the first permanent buildings of the new downtown; the Crescent's surviving fabric is its 1898 Riverside building and the 1917–18 terra-cotta addition at 707 W. Main Avenue (SR). [K3]
- **The Davenport Hotel, 807 W. Sprague Avenue (1914; NR & SR):** National Register ref. 75001874 (listed September 5, 1975). Operating hotel; the tour directs visitors to the second-floor Hall of Doges in the grand ballroom, restored in the 2002 renovation. Public-room access is a hotel's courtesy, not a right — present it as such. [K3, K4, K5]
- **Clock Tower, Riverfront Park (1902 depot remnant; NR):** not a fire survivor but the rebuild era's railroad monument, retained when the Great Northern station was demolished for Expo '74. Optional extension stop; label it honestly as a 1902 building's remnant, not an 1889 relic. [K3]

## 4. Trip-shaped stop progression

**1. Start at the plaque, Lincoln Street & Railroad Alley.** Read the city's own wording — "theorized to have started" — and stand in the railroad corridor where the wooden lodging houses stood. The stop teaches the entry's method in its first minute: what is documented, what is tradition, and how the marker itself separates them. [K3, K1]

**2. Walk the first-wave blocks: Bennett (1890), Whitten (1890), Miller (1890).** Three buildings from the rebuild's first full year, within a few blocks. The interpretive task is material: brick, stone, sandstone trim — the ordinance made visible. [K3]

**3. The Peyton Building.** The rebuild's complication: built big after the fire, burned in 1898 with eight dead, rebuilt again on its own walls. This is the stop that keeps the entry honest — 1889 changed the odds, not the nature of fire. [K3]

**4. End at the Davenport.** Go in. The lobby and the Hall of Doges are the argument's conclusion: the waffle-tent man's hotel, designed by the fire-made architect, opened twenty-five years after the fire on the burned ground, closed and derelict within living memory, and restored to working life in 2002. If the traveler takes one interior in downtown Spokane, it is this one. [K3, K4]

**Strongest story moment:** the Davenport's own numbers, from its Register nomination, told in its lobby: a man who arrived with $1.25 in March 1889, a fire that August that let him earn $125 and buy a tent and a waffle iron, and the $2 million hotel that stands where the ashes were. No novelist would risk the symmetry; the nomination documents it. [K4]

## 5. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **The origin is "most credible," not proven.** HistoryLink: Wolfe's lunchroom and lodgings is "the most credible and enduring story" — other tellings place the start in a saloon or a hotel, and the city's plaque says only that a kitchen fire at that location is "theorized." Public copy must keep the hedge words. The cause was never officially determined. [K1, K3]
- **Blocks destroyed: 32** in HistoryLink and the plaque's account ("leveling 32 blocks"); some popular retellings say "over 30." Use 32 with attribution, or "more than 30" without. Do not print a larger figure; none was sourced in this pass. [K1]
- **The Jones blame story is flagged as myth-making by the best source.** Whether the water superintendent was fishing, and how much of the failure was one man's absence versus a new system's pump failure, is not resolvable from the sources read. Print the failure as documented (the new water system did not deliver pressure) and the blame as tradition. [K1]
- **One death** is the figure in HistoryLink and the standard civic histories. Later popular accounts occasionally inflate fire deaths by conflating the 1898 Peyton Building fire (eight dead) with 1889. Keep the two fires' tolls separate — the Peyton's eight belong to 1898. [K1, K3]
- **Davenport opening dates:** business opening September 1, 1914; grand-opening celebrations September 17–19, 1914. Both are correct for different events; state which one is meant. [K6, K4]
- **The Davenport was "neither Louis Davenport's idea nor built with his money"** (the hotel was a civic businessmen's project that hired him to run it) — a useful correction to the lone-genius version, sourced to the hotel's standard history; the nomination emphasizes Davenport and Cutter without addressing financing. Print the nomination's career facts and the financing nuance together. [K6, K4]
- **Survivor list is the city tour's, not a fresh survey.** Building conditions, current tenants, and the plaque's present legibility were not field-checked in this pass. The tour PDF is undated in the text retrieved; confirm the current edition with Spokane's Historic Preservation Office before publication.
- **No coordinates adopted.** No source read in this pass prints decimal coordinates for the plaque or the route stops; locator coordinates stay blank rather than inheriting map-service pins.

2. **Date the rebuild with your eyes:** at the Bennett, Whitten, and Miller buildings, find each building's date evidence — a datestone, a name/date panel in the parapet, or none visible. Record which of the three carries its 1890 date on its face. Answer/key: blank until field check. [K3]
3. **The walls that survived a second fire:** at the Peyton Building, identify from the street the seam the tour describes — the rebuilt 1898 work rising from the earlier building's remaining walls, and the 1908 two-story addition. Record which floors change material or rhythm. If no seam is legible from the sidewalk, the task is disabled rather than guessed at. Answer/key: blank. [K3]
4. **The retained room:** in the Davenport, find the Marie Antoinette Room — the interior the nomination says was retained from the Pfister Block and incorporated into the 1914 hotel. State, from hotel interpretation or staff, whether it is open to view today. This is an ask-a-person task, done politely or not at all; if the room is closed to the public, the correct field report is "not viewable," and the task stays a story point, not a find.
