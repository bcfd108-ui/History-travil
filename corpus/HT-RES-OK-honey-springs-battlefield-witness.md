## 2 · The story

### People caught between armies

Cherokee Nation’s public history records Cherokee and Creek regiments on both sides at Honey Springs, with the 1st Kansas Colored Volunteer Infantry also engaged. Mvskoke Media records that the battle took place at a settlement in the Muscogee Nation. It describes the Civil War’s divisions within communities and families and reports the Nation’s recognition of the battlefield as a Traditional Cultural Property. These sources provide the setting for the military reports. The reports record units and losses, and the Nation’s history explains why those names belong to communities for whom the history continues. [2–3]

Honey Springs also served as a Confederate supply point in the fight over Fort Gibson. The Oklahoma Historical Society reconstructs the long supply line from Fort Scott and the pressure on it. On July 17, 1863, troops fought around Elk Creek and the Honey Springs settlement. Read the visitors’ center account with the official reports in view. An officer recording events after a fight could see only part of what was happening. [4–5]

### What the officers could see

Union Lt. Col. John Bowles reported for the 1st Kansas Colored Infantry on July 20. He recorded that he was “partly shut out from view of the left by the thick brush” and initially could not see that Col. James M. Williams had fallen wounded. In the same report he credited his regiment’s men and officers for their conduct. The regiment has a nineteenth-century designation quoted only as printed by the records. Bowles’s report is an officer’s account. Individual Black enlisted men’s own testimonies remain to be found. [5]

Confederate Brig. Gen. Douglas H. Cooper wrote from near the Canadian in August. He recorded the retreat and credited the troops under Col. D. N. McIntosh with contributing to “the safe retreat of the train and brigade.” He also named Lt. A. G. Ballenger of the Second Cherokee Regiment among those killed. Cooper’s claim about what caused his defeat and his strengths and casualty estimates are his own. The reports do not resolve one another by averaging their numbers. [4–5]

### The deaths before the battle

Union Lt. Col. Fred. W. Schaurte’s report places three losses at the Arkansas River crossing before the engagement. He prints Huston Mayfield, Key Dougherty and To-cah-le-ges-kie. The transcription varies in the last man's spelling across the body and the return. His name and those of the other two belong in the reading because a battle headline does not tell readers where every family’s loss fell. [5]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- OHS gives Blunt’s early figures of **17 Union killed and 60 wounded** and his estimates of **150 Confederates buried, 400 wounded, and 77 prisoners**. Cooper’s own side is summarized by OHS as **134 killed or wounded and 47 prisoners**. The revised Union return prints **13 killed and 62 wounded**, aggregate **75**, and notes the three river deaths in the table. The NPS battle database instead gives **77 Union and 134 Confederate estimated casualties**. These are different sources and accounting categories. [4–6]
- Visit Cherokee Nation gives approximately **9,000** men involved. Mvskoke Media reports approximately **3,000 Union and 6,000 Confederate**. The NPS database's forces field lists **3,000 total and 0 Confederate**, inconsistent with the report and database narrative. The zero field cannot establish that strength. [2–3,5–6]
- The Mvskoke public account reports a Traditional Cultural Property designation and ongoing consultation. The underlying designation record was not retrieved. A Cherokee Phoenix search was blocked by its robots rules. This draft relies on Cherokee Nation’s published tourism history and the Muscogee Nation’s media account for the communities’ perspectives. [2–3]
- The official reports were read in the OHS compilation transcribing the published U.S. War Department series. No signed manuscript or privately held soldier letter was verified. [5]
