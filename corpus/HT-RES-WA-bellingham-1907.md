## 2. The story
On September 4, 1907, a white mob attacked South Asian millworkers in Bellingham and forced people from the city. The victims were predominantly Punjabi Sikhs, but the community also included other religious identities. Contemporary newspapers' blanket label “Hindu” is not a reliable statement of each person's faith. The University's collection explicitly warns that contemporary reporting varies in accuracy. [5–6]

**Named community voice, not an invented survivor:** Satpal Sidhu, chair of the Arch organization in the 2018 announcement, connected remembrance to education: **“We believe the best way to honor our ancestors is to educate our future generations.”** This is his published modern statement, not testimony from a person present in 1907. The same announcement locates the initiative's beginnings with members of the Lynden Sikh Temple at the 2007 centenary. Community members were authors of remembrance, not simply subjects of a city's apology. [7]

**Contemporary primary record:** A.W. Mangum Jr., a soil scientist working in the region, wrote to his mother on September 8, 1907. SAADA publishes a partial transcription and eight document images, held in the University of North Carolina's Mangum Family Papers. The letter reports the expulsion while repeating racist wage and racial-threat claims. It criticizes whom the mob attacked while endorsing exclusionary premises. That is evidence of a writer's prejudice and response, not reliable demographic or wage evidence, nor a South Asian survivor's voice. The partial transcription was read; the eight original pages were not fully collated. [8]

A September 16, 1906 *Puget Sound American* item cataloged by SAADA documents earlier pressure on workers to remove their turbans. This is a contemporary press source predating the expulsion, not a September 1907 eyewitness account. Its framing requires the same care. [9]

No named expelled worker's directly inspected first-person testimony was recovered in this pass. That gap stays open rather than being filled with an imagined monologue.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** Working people faced violence where they lived and worked. Modern references describe refuge or confinement in the old City Hall basement. None of that establishes that the present memorial's ground was a specific assault or detention location. [5–6]

**Now:** An arch installed in 2018 commemorates three distinct histories: Chinese expulsion in 1885, South Asian expulsion in 1907, and Japanese American removal in 1942. They share an interpretive setting but are not interchangeable events or legal processes. [1]

The old City Hall in historical photographs and today's City Hall associated with the 2018 ceremony must not be conflated. The monument is an intentional later act of memory, not a surviving mill structure or an original doorway through which workers were forced.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Religion and terminology:** The period press's terminology is quoted only when identifying sources; it is not applied indiscriminately to the people attacked. [5,9]
- **Counts and destinations:** Accounts vary on the number attacked, sheltered, detained or expelled. This dossier does not invent a single total or assert that everyone followed one escape route. [5–6]
- **Voice gap:** Sidhu is a modern community organizer, not a survivor or a proven descendant of a named 1907 victim. No such genealogy asserted. [7]
- **Primary limits:** Mangum is an outsider correspondent, and the read text is a partial archival transcription. Neither his racism nor the newspaper's terminology is endorsed. [8–9]
- **Memorial chronology:** The 2018 WWU item announces the forthcoming ceremony; the library's later report confirms dedication on April 21, 2018. Announcement and completion evidence remain distinct. [1,7]
- **Access:** General city park hours verified, but current site-specific exceptions and accessible path geometry are not field-verified. [3]
- **Tribal history:** The library describes recognition of Lummi and Nooksack peoples in the memorial. That is not a substitute for a researched Tribe-published account of this place; no invented Indigenous story is supplied. [1]
