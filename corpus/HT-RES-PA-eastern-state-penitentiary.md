# HT-RES-PA-eastern-state-penitentiary — Seven cellblocks radiating from a central hub inside 30-foot granite walls
Division prisons-asylums-tours-001 • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A prison built on a theory: that a person kept alone, in silence, with work in his cell and time to think, would become penitent. Eastern State Penitentiary opened in Philadelphia in 1829 to test that theory at full scale, in a granite-walled radial complex designed by John Haviland. The theory failed in practice and was formally abandoned in 1913, but the building outlasted it — operating as a congregate prison until 1970, standing empty for more than two decades, and reopening in 1994 as a historic site held in a stabilized, semi-ruined state. What a visitor walks through today is substantially the whole prison: walls, administration building, and the radiating cellblocks, preserved rather than restored.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a historic-site visit: Eastern State Penitentiary Historic Site, 2027 Fairmount Avenue, Philadelphia, PA 19130, operated by Eastern State Penitentiary Historic Site, Inc., a charitable nonprofit organization. The operator's own "Know Before You Go" page describes two forms of daytime admission — a self-guided audio tour (the main tour runs ten stops and about 40 minutes) or a guide-led tour and discussion (45 minutes, led by an educator) — and states that all standard programming, including tours, exhibits, and art installations, is included in one admission price. Admission as the operator's own page states it: when purchasing online, daytime admission is $21 for adults, $19 for seniors, and $17 for college students and youth ages 7–17; when purchasing at the door, $23, $21, and $19 respectively; members receive free daytime admission; the site is not recommended for children under the age of seven. The operator's own Hours of Operation page states: March–November, open daily, 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.; December 1–21 and January–February, open Wednesday–Monday, 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., closed Tuesdays; December 22–31, open daily, 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.; closed on New Year's Day, Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Eve, and Christmas Day; all hours subject to change. The operator also states plainly what kind of building this is: it was abandoned for more than 20 years, remains in a state of semi-ruin, has no climate control, and visitors should stay within clearly marked public areas. The visit is to a working prison's surviving fabric, not a reconstruction.

## 2. The story
Eastern State began as an argument. In 1787 a group of Philadelphians — Benjamin Franklin and Dr. Benjamin Rush among them — founded the Philadelphia Society for Alleviating the Miseries of Public Prisons, concerned, in the operator's summary, with the harsh conditions and punitive nature of America's prisons and guided by the belief that people could change. The Society's proposal was a prison that would not simply punish but would move the prisoner toward reflection and change: isolation from other prisoners, with labor, in silence. In 1821, at the Society's urging, the Pennsylvania legislature approved funding to build it. The National Register nomination records that the legislature appointed a building commission to oversee construction of a 250-cell prison based on the principle of solitary confinement, and that after a public competition won by John Haviland, the cornerstone was laid in 1823. The Historic American Buildings Survey (HABS) history dates the design competition to 1821, Haviland's appointment and the start of construction to 1822, and the legislature's order implementing "separate or solitary confinement at labour" to 1829.

Haviland's plan was a wheel: seven cellblocks radiating from a central hub, so that a guard standing at the center could see down every corridor. The operator's history quotes Haviland's own stated aims for the plan — "watching, convenience, economy and ventilation" — and notes the deliberate contrast of the building's faces: a medieval Gothic façade meant to intimidate, and a church-like interior meant to instill reverence. The nomination describes the fabric in survey terms: an exterior rectangle of 30-foot-high walls of hewn and squared granite, tapering in thickness from 12 feet at their bases to 2.75 feet at their tops, a projecting tower at each corner, and a main administration building 200 feet long pierced by a gateway formerly 27 feet tall and 15 feet wide, closed by a massive wrought-iron portcullis and double oaken doors. Inside, the central building was 40 feet in diameter, with the guard house in its basement and a watch platform and cupola above.

The cells were the system made concrete. The nomination records that the first three cellblocks Haviland built were single-storied, with cells 12 feet by 8 feet by 10 feet high, each divided from the corridor, each with one door opening onto an enclosed exercise yard 18 feet long; the only opening on the interior side was a food hatch that converted into a table, and a one-way peephole for the guards; each cell also had a convex reflector, a "deadeye," for light and ventilation. The HABS history adds the mechanical ambition of the place: each prisoner in his own cell, centrally heated, with running water and a flush toilet and a skylight, at a time when those were novelties in American building. Prisoners were hooded when moved outside their cells so that they would not see, or be seen by, another prisoner. The HABS history records that the penitentiary received its first prisoner on 22 October 1829 — received by the first warden, Samuel Wood, before the heating system was even installed, so that Wood bought six small coal stoves to warm the cells. (The one-day discrepancy in this date is carried in §6.)

The experiment drew the world's attention, and its critics, from the start. After an 1842 visit, Charles Dickens called the practices "rigid, strict and hopeless solitary confinement" and wrote, in the operator's quotation of him, "I believe it, in its effects, to be cruel and wrong." The system also could not hold its own numbers. The prison had been proposed for 250 occupants; the nomination records that after the first three blocks, the proposed occupancy was expanded to 400 and the next four blocks were built double-storied — the second story, of course, had no exercise yards, and its cells were doubled in size to compensate. The operator's history puts the population peak at approximately 1,700 in the mid-1920s, in a building designed for 250. New blocks kept filling the ground between the originals: the nomination counts three new cellblocks built between the originals in 1877–79, an eleventh in 1894, and a twelfth in 1908–11, with the exercise yards eliminated along the way and replaced by machine shops and boiler rooms. The HABS chronology continues the sequence the nomination stops short of: Cellblock 13 erected in the years after 1909, Cellblock 14 in 1923, and Cellblock 15 in 1956–59. In 1913, the nomination and the operator agree, the Pennsylvania System was officially abolished — though the operator notes its practice had begun eroding decades earlier, as prisoners started to share cells, dining spaces, and more under the pressure of numbers. Eastern State then ran for nearly six more decades as a congregate prison.

The later population included the two names the site is still asked about most. The archival finding aid for the penitentiary's own records, quoting the historic site's history, records that Al Capone, sentenced to one year in 1929, spent most of that sentence at Eastern State, where he was allowed to furnish his cell with antiques, rugs, and oil paintings; the operator's timeline gives his stay as eight months across 1929–1930, and his cell is a stated point of interest on the visit today. The same finding aid records that bank robber Willie Sutton joined eleven other men in a 1945 tunnel escape; the operator's timeline adds that the tunnel emerged at Fairmount Avenue and 22nd Street, that most of the men were caught within minutes, and that prison plaster worker Clarence Klinedinst designed and built most of the tunnel — a credit Sutton also claimed for its planning (see §6).

The end came in stages, and the stages matter to the dates printed for this place. The operator's history states that in 1970, faced with costly repairs and deteriorating conditions, Pennsylvania ceased the operation of Eastern State Penitentiary. The HABS history is more precise: the prison officially shut down in January 1970, and the last prisoners left in April. In 1971 the Commonwealth leased the site to the City of Philadelphia for $1, and after a riot at Holmesburg County Prison the city transferred a number of inmates to Eastern State as an emergency measure; the building then stood empty through most of the 1970s and 1980s, used by the city for storage and police-dog training, at risk of demolition. In 1988, at the urging of a task force of architects, preservationists, and historians, Mayor Wilson Goode halted the sale of the site. In 1994 the historic site welcomed its first visitors — 10,000 in the first year of hard-hat tours, per the operator — and the nonprofit operator has held the site since as a stabilized ruin and a museum of the American prison, then and now.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a radial machine for solitude — seven cellblocks fanning from a 40-foot central guard building inside 30-foot granite walls, each early cell a self-contained world of 12 by 8 by 10 feet with its own 18-foot exercise yard, its food hatch, its deadeye vent, and a hooded prisoner moved through corridors designed so he would never see another man's face. **Now:** the same wheel, grown to fifteen blocks in the operator's count, held as a semi-ruin — peeling paint, rusted bedframes, and empty corridors walked daily by visitors on an audio tour, with the operator interpreting both the 1829 experiment and the present-day prison system in the same visit.

**Then:** on 22 October 1829 (HABS) — or 23 October, as the operator's timeline prints it — one prisoner received by Warden Samuel Wood in an unfinished building heated by six coal stoves. **Now:** a National Register-listed National Historic Landmark (No. 66000680) whose nomination calls it "the prime exponent of the Pennsylvania system of imprisonment" — a system of limited influence at home that was studied and applied widely abroad.

## 4. What's still there
- The full perimeter: the 30-foot granite walls, tapering from 12 feet thick at the base, with their corner towers, substantially as the National Register nomination describes them
- The administration building on Fairmount Avenue — 200 feet long, with its two 50-foot towers, battlements, and the central gateway — now the visitor entrance
- The central hub: the 40-foot-diameter central building from which the cellblock corridors radiate, with its guard tower above
- The radiating cellblocks themselves, from Haviland's single-story first three blocks with their exercise yards to the later infill blocks — including the cells, food hatches, and skylights of the separate system, in stabilized-ruin condition
- Al Capone's cell, presented as a point of interest on the operator's tour, and the penitentiary's historic synagogue, likewise named by the operator
- The hospital and industrial fabric of the later congregate prison, and the operator's installed exhibits — *Prisons Today*, *The Big Graph*, and 14 art installations at the time of checking

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Fairmount Avenue front:** Start outside the walls, at the southeast corner of Corinthian and Fairmount Avenues — the viewpoint of this entry's first photograph — and read the nomination's exterior against the stone: the tapering granite wall, the corner towers, the administration building's Gothic front.
2. **At the central hub:** Stand where the system was watched from — the center of the wheel — and take the corridors in order, earliest blocks first, so the plan's logic (one guard, seven sightlines, total separation) is plain before the later infill blocks complicate it.
3. **In an early cellblock:** Read one original cell against the nomination's dimensions — 12 by 8 by 10 feet, exercise yard beyond the door, food hatch in the corridor wall — and then a later double-storied block, where the yards are gone and the system's compromises are built into the plan.
4. **At the later prison:** Capone's cell and the congregate-era spaces close the arc — the penitentiary after penitence, run as an ordinary crowded prison until January 1970.
## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The first prisoner's day does not agree across the primary records. The HABS history (HABS PA-1729 data pages) states that Eastern State "received its first prisoner on 22 October 1829." The operator's published timeline gives the opening as October 23, 1829. Both print here; this draft does not merge them into a single day.

The closing date is a two-stage record, and single-year summaries flatten it. The HABS history: the prison officially shut down in January 1970, the last prisoners left in April 1970, and the city used the building for county prisoners in 1971 under a $1 lease. The operator's history says Pennsylvania ceased operation in 1970 and the building was "used briefly as a city jail in 1971." Records that give the closure simply as "1971" are counting the city use; records that give "1970" are counting the state penitentiary. Both stages print here rather than one year.

The cellblock count conflicts. The National Register nomination, written while the prison's end was recent, states that when the prison was finally closed "the total number of blocks was fourteen, accommodating 900 prisoners." The HABS chronology and the operator's history both count fifteen: the operator states the footprint expanded "from seven to 15 cellblocks by 1956," and the HABS chronology dates Cellblock 15's construction to 1956–59. The nomination's fourteen and the survey's fifteen both print; the discrepancy is in the federal record itself and is not resolved here.

The address has three forms in the records. The nomination gives the street address as 21st Street and Fairmount Avenue; the HABS survey titles the property 2125 Fairmount Avenue; the operator's current visitor address is 2027 Fairmount Avenue. The locator record below prints the operator's current address and notes the historic forms here.

Credit for the 1945 tunnel is disputed in the operator's own materials. The timeline states that Clarence Klinedinst "designed and built most of the tunnel" and that Willie Sutton "takes credit for planning the tunnel." Both claims print; the record does not settle who planned it.

Capacity figures measure different things and are printed as such, not reconciled: designed occupancy 250 (nomination and operator), expanded plan of 400 (nomination), a closing capacity of 900 (nomination), and a peak population of approximately 1,700 in the mid-1920s (operator).

The nomination's acreage — "approximate acreage of nominated property: 14.5" — is larger than the walled city block the complex occupies; the form does not explain the figure, and no other acreage prints here.

## 8. Standing on the ground
What a visitor can check against the record, on site:

- From the corner of Corinthian and Fairmount Avenues, compare the wall in front of you with the nomination's description — 30 feet high, granite, visibly thicker at its base than at its top — and with the 1967 HABS frame in §7, taken from this corner while the prison still held prisoners.
- From the central hub, count the radiating corridors and check them against Haviland's seven-block plan; then note the blocks that do not belong to the wheel's first geometry — the infill construction of 1877 onward.
- In an early cellblock, measure a cell by eye against the nomination's figures — 12 feet by 8 feet, 10 feet high — and find the food hatch in the corridor wall and the door to the individual exercise yard at the cell's far end.
- At the administration building, check the gateway against the nomination: 200 feet of front building, two 50-foot towers, and the gate passage that once held a portcullis and oak doors.
- In Capone's cell, hold the furnished room against the record's limits: the finding aid says he was allowed antiques, rugs, and oil paintings; what the room proves is what the site presents, and the presentation is a restoration, not a survival.
