Idaho's worst mining disaster, told in the numbers nobody in the Silver Valley has to look up: 173 men underground on a Tuesday morning, a fire in the air intake, 91 dead of smoke and carbon monoxide — and two men found alive after 175 hours in the dark. The memorial stands by the interstate: a 13-foot miner, drill raised, 91 names on the rock beneath him.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is the memorial: the Sunshine Miners Memorial, off Interstate 90 near Kellogg, Idaho (Big Creek exit area), at the mouth of Big Creek Canyon. A roadside memorial — park clear of the highway. The mine itself is closed and private; it is not the visit. The record names no hours or fees for the memorial, and none are printed here.

## 2. The story
The Sunshine Mine, between Wallace and Kellogg in Big Creek Canyon, traced its lineage to the Yankee Lode, claimed by the Blake brothers in **1884**, and grew into one of the nation's premier silver mines. On the morning of **May 2, 1972**, **173 miners** were underground. Just before noon — the Bureau of Mines record puts the first smoke report at about **11:40 a.m.**, when an electrician smelled smoke — a fire broke out on the **air-intake side** of the mine, below the 3100 level, and the ventilation system that kept men alive carried smoke and carbon monoxide through the workings instead.

What saved the men who lived was a man at a hoist. Thirty-six-year-old hoistman **Robert Scanlon** stayed at his post bringing miners up — Idaho Magazine's account credits him with lifting **eighty-one miners to safety** — before he himself succumbed to the gases. In all, **80 men escaped** on their own or were hoisted out in the first hours. Ninety-three were unaccounted for. Over the next seven days rescuers worked the mine; on the seventh day, **175 hours** after the fire, they found two men alive in the dark — they had survived on water dripping from the workings and food from deceased co-workers around them. The last of the dead were recovered by May 13. The final count: **91 miners dead** of carbon monoxide poisoning and smoke inhalation, leaving — in the words of the U.S. Senate's 50th-anniversary record — **77 widows and 200 children**. It was the worst hard-rock mining disaster in the United States since Butte's Speculator fire of 1917, and the worst in Idaho history.

The disaster rewrote mine safety practice — escape systems, ventilation, self-rescuers, and training all changed in its aftermath — and the Silver Valley marks it the way mining country marks its dead: by name, every year. Idaho declared **May 2 as Miners Memorial Day** in March 2022, ahead of the 50th anniversary, when the Senate record noted that *every year on May 2, the people of the Silver Valley gather at this memorial to remember.* At the annual ceremony, **91 chairs** are set out, a hard hat with its lamp lit on each; at the end, each lamp is turned off.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the nation's premier silver mine, working three shifts a day in Big Creek Canyon. **Now:** the mine closed in **2001**, and the ground a visitor stands on is the memorial just off Interstate 90: a **13-foot miner** sculpted by former Sunshine miner **Ken Lonn**, drill raised to the sky, standing on a boulder that carries the plaque of the 91 names and a poem by former Governor **Phil Batt**. You read the names at the rock, with the canyon the mine worked rising behind it.

## 4. What's still there
- The Sunshine Miners Memorial off I-90 near Kellogg — Lonn's 13-foot miner, the names plaque, and the Batt poem, at the mouth of Big Creek Canyon
- The names themselves: 91 of them, including a father and son from the Kitchens family of Kellogg
- The annual May 2 gathering — if you can time a visit to Miners Memorial Day, this is one of the few entries in this catalog where the commemoration itself is the living site
- The University of Idaho Library's disaster archive: thousands of pages of deposition, hearing, and trial transcripts from miners, managers, and company officials

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The underground count is 173 in the Congressional Record, the Bureau of Mines tradition, and Idaho Magazine; one university news account says 178 worked that day — the 173 figure is the one the arithmetic supports (80 escaped + 2 rescued alive + 91 dead = 173), and it is the figure used here. The two survivors' names are given as Ron Flory and David Wilkinson in the University of Idaho's account; Idaho Magazine names Flory's partner as Tim Wilkerson — the discrepancy in the second survivor's name is printed, not resolved. The fire's cause was never proven: the Bureau of Mines believed spontaneous combustion of refuse near scrap timber; the NIOSH summary calls it a fire of unknown origin below the 3100 level.
