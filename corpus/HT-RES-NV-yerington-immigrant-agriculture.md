## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** an immigrant agricultural story need not be a straight line from arrival to a successful farm. Family support, paid work, credit and changing livelihoods can be part of the same history.

A public family-history contribution **submitted by Pat Fabri on August 15, 2003** describes **Louis Fabri** and the Yerington family. It attributes to him ranch work, remittances to his mother in Italy, a period combining schooling with milking, freighting, and an unsuccessful leased ranch. It also records **Gemma's** remembered disappointment at her new surroundings and household conditions. [4] This is named family/community-published evidence, **not a contemporaneous account book or a verified verbatim interview with either spouse**.

An independently published family voice offers a narrower connection. In his **February 6, 2012** interview with **Alicia Barber**, **Dave Aiazzi** recalls the different paths taken by three immigrant brothers, describing the Yerington branch as having **“set out to buy land.”** He grew up in Reno/Sparks, not on the Yerington farm. His family recollection does not establish a parcel, purchase date or agricultural work routine there. [5, printed p.1]

These sources give a genuine Yerington nexus without pretending the speaker witnessed every earlier event. The story is distinct from Fallon reclamation history: it concerns Yerington family migration and the overlap of household, agricultural and other work, not a transplant of another valley's project narrative.

## 3. Then / now

The family contribution links migration to the need to support relatives and to more than one kind of employment. Treat its hardship and livelihood claims as attributed family history, not a complete economic survey. [4]

The state tourism account describes today's museum building as a former church moved from Mason to Yerington, later purchased by the museum society in **1978**. Its displayed domestic interiors are museum interpretation, not proof that a particular immigrant family lived in those rooms. [1]

The sources do not establish an original agricultural household preserved for public entry. A functioning farm is neither a museum nor a visitor invitation. A family surname on land, a historical road reference or a public photograph does not establish access rights.

This bounded dossier also does not equate immigrant settlement with empty land. It makes no account of Paiute agricultural labor or land history without an appropriately sourced community voice. Those relationships require separate evidence rather than a decorative sentence added to an immigrant success story.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The Fabri narrative's chronology is internally inconsistent. It dates Louis's birth to August 1882 yet calls him fifteen in February 1897; it places an agricultural/mining episode in February 1908 before describing an intervening three-year lease and then a 1907 occupation. These cannot all be treated as a verified linear timeline. The broad family-work account is retained with attribution; precise ordering is **GAPPED**. [4]

The Aiazzi interview explicitly frames the family origin as limited recollection. It is not firsthand agricultural labor testimony from Yerington. [5] A full first-person account by a Yerington farm worker or household worker remains a depth gap.

A local Portuguese-history post names **Josephine Borsini Manha** and describes dairy cleaning, milking and garden work. It is a useful further community-history lead, but its narrative's underlying attribution was not resolved here, so its quantities and current ownership statements are not promoted as verified findings. [6]

No land title, payroll, migration manifest or contemporary farm record was inspected. Current operator access, holidays and image rights are unresolved. This is not an approved travel stop or a claim of museum closure.
