## 2. The story
**Begin with continuity, not an empty river.** The Chinook Indian Nation's own published statement *Beyond Native American Heritage Month* identifies its continuing community at the Columbia's mouth and recommends the museum's collaborative **This is Our Place** exhibit. This is Tribal-published evidence of living presence and collaboration, not permission to retell all Chinook history from a museum summary or to turn cultural sites into destinations. The initially retrieved indexed passage, rather than a full underlying historical archive, supports this limited framing. [4]

**Named worker's voice:** retired Columbia River pilot **Thomas Thomayer**, interviewed in Caitlin Dwyer's *Knowing the Water* for Oregon Humanities' **Labor, Spring 2026** issue, described the family strain of extended tugboat work: **“You're living two lives.”** Dwyer presents his account of repeated absences and returning home, then a change in career. This is an attributed published interview quotation, not a separately deposited oral-history recording or a verified personnel file. The article also interviews **Cedric Barrett**; river pilots and bar pilots are distinct jobs, so neither man is relabeled a bar pilot here. [5]

Their inclusion shifts the story from a dangerous-looking river to working lives. The central historical question is how skill and employment shape a household, not how a visitor might reproduce professional work. A contemporary interview can illuminate a continuing occupational culture but cannot supply the experiences of nineteenth-century crews or today's entire workforce.

**Original interpretive voice:** *From shore, a ship can look like a moving piece of scenery. For somebody aboard, it is another shift and another stretch away from home. The museum visit becomes more useful when the visitor remembers the person behind the job title, and the household beyond the vessel.* This is original interpretation, not testimony.

This entry is about maritime navigation labor, public interpretation and remembered working life. It does not duplicate a cannery's processing line, harvest system or industrial production history. It does not offer operational instruction, training requirements, schedules, navigational margins, current ship tracking or professional-risk imitation.

## 3. Then / now
- **A career remembered:** an interview connects work on the river with family time. **Now:** keep the speaker's experience particular rather than claiming a universal maritime household. [5]
- **Vessels become exhibits:** the museum lists the retired pilot boat **Peacock** and the preserved lightship. **Now:** a displayed or museum-operated vessel is not evidence that similar working craft are open to visitors. [2,6]
- **Public memory expands:** a Chinook collaboration makes living culture part of the museum account. **Now:** neither an industry-focused narrative nor a historical collection should imply that Indigenous relationships ended with commercial shipping. [4,6]
- **An institution is changing:** a new building has a specific announced opening. **Now:** use date-sensitive language and verify actual availability separately from publicity. [1]

A museum's dramatic display is a representation, not a present hazard report. Do not turn shipwreck loss into a scored hunt or a reason to approach dangerous waterfront locations.

## 4. What's still there
The museum's **current-exhibits page** lists **Crossing the Bar: Perilous Passage**, a historic pilot pulling boat, and the retired **Peacock** at the east end of its campus. It also lists **On the River**, beginning with steamboat travel, and the Chinook collaboration photographed by **Amiran White**. These are separately checked operator listings, not confirmation that every display is unobstructed or operating today. No boarding access to Peacock is inferred. [6]

**Lightship Columbia** is a separate museum vessel. Its visitor status is established by the operator's ship page, not by a historic register or an archive photograph. It is not a pilot boat; the distinct work represented by each should remain clear. [2]

**Historic documentation:** the Library of Congress catalogs the lightship as **HAER OR-159**, item **or0583**, including five photographs and a written history. This establishes a documentary record, not current physical access. No claim is made that those photographs are displayed at the museum. [7]

The named pilot interview is reading material for this dossier; it is not promised as an installed museum oral-history station. Staff, workers and ordinary visitors are not subjects to photograph or approach for verification.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Single museum visit, not a port circuit.** Suggested **90–120 minutes** is an editorial allowance, not an operator duration or timed booking. No inter-stop drive time is asserted.

1. **Before departure:** recheck the operator's opening/holiday notice and any campus changes. Use the existing museum as the anchor; verify the new hall separately after its announced opening.
2. **At the public entrance:** use the designated visitor route and admission desk. Check the ship's actual availability and whether any gallery or route is temporarily restricted. The indoor core remains sufficient.
3. **Start with living place:** if available, encounter the Chinook collaborative exhibit on its own terms. Do not treat it as a vanished-world prelude to shipping.
4. **Move to maritime work:** read the short Thomayer quotation, then examine the museum's labor-related interpretation. Ask how time away from home changes the meaning of a job usually presented through vessels and equipment. No navigational lesson or risk challenge is attached.
5. **Optional museum vessel:** only during the separately authorized public window and if staff confirm suitable access. Skip it if boarding is closed, conditions poor or access unsuitable. Do not substitute a commercial dock or working boat.
6. **End with the strongest story moment indoors:** return to the contrast between the job title and the person. What did the exhibit tell you about the vessel, and what did the interview add about being away from home? This ending works without outdoor views, boarding or a ship passing.

Current winter mode is the indoor museum with conditional optional boarding. A fair-weather visit can reassess public campus features but does not automatically extend into the working waterfront. No Route Group is activated.
