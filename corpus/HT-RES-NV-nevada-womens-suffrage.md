## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** Nevada suffrage organizers faced a practical problem before they could win a constitutional argument: how to reach a changing electorate scattered across an enormous state.

In **“The Winning of Nevada,” dated November 7, 1914**, **Anne Martin**, president of the Nevada Equal Franchise Society, described weekly newspaper bulletins, mailed literature and personal canvassing. Her phrase **“prospecting for votes”** turns the state's mining vocabulary toward political organizing. She expressly credited **Mabel Vernon**, sent by the Congressional Union, with organizing from March through the election. [6]

Read this as a leader's near-contemporary explanation of a successful campaign, not a neutral audit of every participant's contribution. The available three-page document is a modern transcription attributed to *The Suffragist*, not an inspected original magazine issue. No recording was heard; this is a printed primary voice.

**Original interpretation:** the interesting object is not just a ballot. A mailing list that goes stale, a bulletin that must be written again, and an audience that must be persuaded make enfranchisement visible as repeated work. This is Nevada's particular story, not another state's legislative sequence transplanted onto a Nevada landmark.

## 3. Then / now

The state-commissioned historical context records approval by Nevada's male electorate on **November 3, 1914**, with **10,936 in favor and 7,258 opposed**. It identifies **Sophia Zadow** arranging the Eureka campaign appearance and **Sara Bard Field** speaking at Winnemucca's final rally. These attributions extend the story beyond Martin without inventing firsthand quotations from those women. [7, printed p.60]

Historian **Joanne L. Goodwin**, writing through UNLV, also names **Sadie Hurst, Jeanne Weir and Bird Wilson** among the people who put the statewide strategy into practice. [8] This is institutional historical interpretation, not testimony recorded from those activists.

The 1914 change preceded the Nineteenth Amendment by six years, but neither date means every woman could actually vote. Citizenship and other qualifications remained consequential. The state context discusses immigrant women's exclusion and explicitly says the Nevada-specific experience of Indigenous voting after 1924 needs deeper research. [7, pp.67,69–70] Do not simplify this into a universal “all women won the vote” ending, or substitute another state's restriction dates for Nevada evidence.

The present archive offers a place to investigate how these claims were recorded. It is not the campaign headquarters and does not promise an original document will be displayed when a visitor arrives.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Its citation points to **1913 statutes, p.581**, for the constitutional language; the legislature's linked page failed with a decoding error in this check. The proposal's legislative date and the 1914 popular vote are different stages, not contradictory election dates. [7,10]

No named Nevada Tribal woman's firsthand account was recovered in this bounded check; the dossier does not supply an invented one. The SHPO report is a useful synthesis but should not replace community testimony or individual records.
