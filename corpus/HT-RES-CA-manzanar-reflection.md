## 2. The story
**Sue Kunitomi Embrey**, interviewed by **John Allen on November 6, 2002**, recalled arriving after dark, without immediate access to her luggage. Her brother had prepared the family's bedding. She remembered a crowded room without partitions and contrasted incarceration with what she had learned about constitutional rights in school. Segment 7 is a directly read transcript, not testimony inferred from a narrator biography. [5]

In segment 17, she traces a later turn toward civic participation and preserving Manzanar. Her phrase **“if we really wanted to get our voices heard”** leads into her explanation of democratic participation. She connects her involvement in the 1969 pilgrimage with increasingly sustained preservation work. This is her retrospective account, not proof that incarceration was beneficial or that one person alone created the national historic site. [6]

The distinct Manzanar story here is **a survivor helping make a public record where her rights had been denied**. It is not the Tule Lake questionnaire-centered story or an interchangeable account of another incarceration site. A museum can appear settled and inevitable once it exists. The testimony makes it possible to ask who insisted that this history remain publicly visible.

The strongest moment is not a reconstructed room. It is the change from a young person wondering why no one would speak for her to an adult participating in the work of remembrance. That is editorial framing of two attributed passages, not a universal arc of healing and not a demand that survivors forgive.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Conditional public-center sequence; use the same reading order remotely if the gate is closed or uncertain.**

1. Begin with a named person, not a camp statistic. Read the attributed arrival passage before interpreting the institution.
2. If the visitor center is confirmed open, remain within its authorized public interpretation. Distinguish original building fabric, later restoration and exhibition design. Otherwise stay with the online sources.
3. Read the later passage about civic voice and preservation. Do not make a visitor perform an interrogation, sign a mock form, choose a loyalty category or simulate confinement.
4. End with the strongest reflection: public memory was made through people's work; a building's existence should not obscure who demanded that history be heard.

The sequence finishes at reflection. It does not continue to a burial monument, cultural site or reconstructed dwelling for emotional effect. No physical route or travel-time promise is supplied.
