# Raymond courthouse square — the girl who followed a wounded soldier into town

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP11 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

The public stop is the exterior of the Hinds County Courthouse on the square at 121 West Main Street, Raymond. The Mississippi Secretary of State identifies it as the Second Judicial District courthouse. The city invites visitors to the square. Stay on the public sidewalk, clear of court business. Read the story here. No battlefield, cemetery, private home or courtroom visit is needed. [1–3]

## 2. Story

After the fighting near Raymond on May 12, 1863, a Confederate private named Frank Herron needed a wound dressed. In his later account, a Union surgeon first treated him at a field hospital at the McDonald family home. Federal officers occupied the house’s rooms, the family retained one, and other soldiers lay under an arbor. Herron remembered a girl bringing water, washing his face and combing his hair. He knew her as Myra McDonald. When he was moved into the courthouse hospital, she followed with food and flowers. Myra appears here as a named caregiver whose acts survive through her patient’s recollection. [4]

Outside town that day, Confederate brigadier general John Gregg had tried to stop Union major general James B. McPherson’s corps near Fourteen Mile Creek. The Confederates withdrew. Union commander Ulysses S. Grant decided to turn toward Jackson during the campaign for Vicksburg. At eleven that night, McPherson wrote to Grant that he had heard reinforcements might be coming and could not verify the rumor. Commanders still wondered whether another battle would begin in the morning. For people caring for patients, the fighting’s consequences had already settled in for days. [5–6]

The courthouse was only a few years old. The city dates its construction to 1857–1859. The American Battlefield Trust identifies the contractors as the Weldon brothers and records that enslaved craftspeople built it. Their work gave Hinds County a seat of government, then provided the walls within which other people worked to keep the wounded alive. The consulted accounts do not identify those builders individually. [1,3]

## 3. Then and now

The city records that wounded men from both armies were cared for in several places, including homes, St. Mark’s Episcopal Church and the courthouse. The Trust identifies Confederate patients in the courthouse specifically. Herron independently recalls his own transfer there. These statements support a network of improvised hospitals and one identifiable courthouse patient. They do not assign every patient to a building. Today the courthouse still serves a judicial district. [1–4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

The courthouse and square remain the physical link. Use the exterior to see the scale of a civic building pressed into hospital service. The city and local preservation organization also identify interpretation in town. No source used here identifies Herron’s room, his bed, the McDonald house as an open visitor site, or surviving wartime medical fittings. [1–4,7]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

**Read the two journeys at the square.** Grant’s correspondence follows an army out of Raymond. Herron remembered a girl making the much shorter journey into town to keep caring for him after his transfer. Years later he wrote the Raymond postmaster hoping to find the women who had helped him. He said the answer came from Myra herself, by then Myra Dennis. Her original letter has yet to be located for this entry. [4,6]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

Herron’s recollection appeared in a veterans’ anthology decades afterward. It gives his own memory and loyalties, including the people he noticed. The night dispatch supplies a contemporary account of military uncertainty. Neither document gives a complete nursing roster. One federal parkway wayside page prints May 8, while the NPS battle record and the dated commanders’ correspondence establish May 12 for the engagement. An early HABS catalog entry lists 1850 for construction; the city and Trust independently give 1857–59. [1,3–6,8–9]
