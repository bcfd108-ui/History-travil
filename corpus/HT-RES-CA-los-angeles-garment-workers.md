## 2. The story

**Firsthand worker voice:** Hope Mendoza Schechter, interviewed by Virginia Espino, session 1, November 19, 2009. In PDF pages 12–13 she recalls entering garment employment after leaving school, low piecework pay, a collective approach to the union, subsequent dismissal and then becoming a union organizer. Her remembered declaration is **“We don't have to put up with this.”** Treat the account of retaliation as her retrospective testimony, not an independently adjudicated finding about a named employer. [1]

The same session includes household sewing for her mother and sisters (PDF page 4). That joins paid industrial work to the skill and unpaid work supporting a household. Exact employment years are not established by these passages; the interviewer’s suggested age and Schechter’s distinction between initial work and union employment must not be collapsed into a single dated event. The PDF’s 2017 export stamp is not the interview date. [1]

**Original interpretive voice:** Clothing carries evidence of skill while often concealing the person who supplied it. This entry follows the shift from making something to naming what the maker deserves. A worker’s decision to speak is history too. The point is not to dress up as a seamstress, reproduce a work quota or turn hardship into a game. It is to hear how one woman understood the relationship between a household, a paycheck and collective power.

A separate institutional frame is available in the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County’s online *The 1933 Garment Workers’ Strike*, presented by interpreter Diana Sanchez. Its text explicitly places marginalized communities and labor justice at the center. This is contextual interpretation, not Schechter’s testimony or evidence that she participated in that 1933 strike. The embedded video was not watched. [6]

Hope Mendoza Schechter’s testimony concerns her own working life. A separate generation of workers left a record of what it took to challenge employers across the same regional industry. In an account published with attorney Julie Su, Sirilak “Jim” Charasri remembers boarding a plane to the United States: “I wanted to see this beautiful country.” On arrival, workers slept and sewed in the same El Monte apartment complex. The labels on the clothing connected those rooms to retailers elsewhere. [7]

Su describes Thai workers joining Latino garment workers in a lawsuit and a broader campaign. Meetings were part of the work. Buppha “Kaew” Chaemchoi remembered, “Our attorney wanted us to be in charge.” Read those words beside Schechter’s account. One woman recalled the moment she spoke up at work. Another described what happened when workers themselves had to make repeated decisions about a case. These women did not all work for the same employer or take part in the same action. The quotations are published recollections, not an independently examined recording or court transcript. Quiet documentary reading only. [1,7]

## 3. Then / now

Then: remembered working and organizing experience, recorded decades afterward. Now: a public university preserves a transcript whose meaning can be explored without entering the industry’s current workplaces. [1,4]

An archive is not the place where the original labor happened. Nor is the existence of a historical labor account permission to photograph present workers, solicit personal employment or immigration information, or investigate private shops. No contemporary worker’s identity or location is added here.

## 4. What's still there

The verified surviving object is the published transcript. Its selected pages are searchable and readable; original audio was not heard. ## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Archive-shaped progression, not a field route.** Suggested 30–45 minutes is an editorial allowance, not an operator duration.

1. **Orient:** identify narrator, interviewer and session date before interpreting the recollection. Notice the later export stamp without treating it as a historical date. [1]
2. **Read the worker passage:** selected PDF pages 12–13, distinguishing what Schechter remembers from what the interviewer proposes. Do not extract employer locations as destinations.
3. **Read the household passage:** PDF page 4. Keep skill and unpaid family work visible alongside paid employment. Avoid unrelated sensitive family details in the larger transcript.
4. **Widen carefully:** read the NHM introduction to the 1933 strike as a different historical context, not a prequel whose connection to this person has been proved. [6]
5. **Strongest closing moment:** return to the worker’s declaration. Ask what changes when the worker, rather than the garment, becomes the subject of the record. Leave with a distinction between testimony and corroboration, not a story of inevitable victory.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- Exact initial employment and organizing dates need corroboration; a first-person claim of being the first Latina hired by the union is not independently verified here and is not promoted as an established superlative.
- This is a transcribed oral history, not a contemporaneous wage record. No independent payroll, contract or employment-file comparison was completed.
- The transcript references another oral history at Bancroft; that separate record was not retrieved and must not be conflated with UCLA’s sessions.
- Session 1 is dated November 19, 2009; later sessions extend into 2010. The 2017 filename/export context does not revise those dates. [1]
- No appointment was made; UCLA’s current policy explicitly says it does not take reading-room appointments. Original-material availability remains separate from opening hours. [3–5]
- Winter holiday dates beyond those expressly checked remain open. No exact entrance coordinate or printed street address was recovered for this draft.
- Reproduction rights, donor conditions and original audio availability remain unresolved. Reading a public transcript is not a license to republish it.
