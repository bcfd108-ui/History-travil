# HT-RES-WI-pendarvis — Stone cottages a Cornish mining town built, and two men rebuilt
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a street called Shake Rag, under the hill where miners dug lead from shallow pits, Cornish tin miners built themselves cottages of local limestone in the 1830s and 1840s — fronts fitted so tight a knife blade cannot be forced between the stones. A century later the cottages were derelict and being torn down, and two men, Robert Neal and Edgar Hellum, bought the first one for ten dollars and spent thirty-five years restoring the row, running a Cornish restaurant out of it to pay for the work. The Wisconsin Historical Society runs the site now, and the houses stand on their original bedrock, on their original sites, as the best-preserved evidence of Wisconsin's territorial lead-mining era.

## 1. Trip gate
The operator is the Wisconsin Historical Society, and the visit runs entirely on the operator's terms. The Society's Register record describes Pendarvis as "open seasonally to the public, offering guided tours by costumed interpreters," and the operator's own site page, checked November 26, 2026, carries the standing notice: the site is closed for the 2026 season and "We reopen for general admission in May 2027." No daily hours and no admission fee are printed in this entry, because none was established from the operator pages consulted for this pass; the season, not a price, is what those pages state. The street address carried by the 1970 National Register nomination is 114 Shake Rag Street, Mineral Point. The house interiors are museum space entered on the operator's tours. Shake Rag Street itself is a public street in a lived-in historic neighborhood: the restored site houses are the exhibit, and the other stone houses on the street are private homes, not stops. Behind and above the site, the Merry Christmas Mine Hill trails and prairie are part of the Society's ground (see §4), carrying the mining features the cottages' builders dug.

## 2. The story
Lead was known on this ground long before Mineral Point existed. The Wisconsin Historical Society's site history records that members of the Ho-Chunk, Mesquaki (Fox), Sauk, and other tribes had mined lead for hundreds of years before French fur traders arrived in the 1600s. The ground that became Mineral Point was not open for white settlement in 1827, yet hundreds of miners and settlers poured in illegally, violating the treaty rights of the Native nations settled there; a series of treaties ceded all Indian lands south of the Wisconsin River to the United States by the early 1830s.

The miners who made the town were Cornish. The 1970 nomination records that unemployed tin miners came to Mineral Point from Cornwall in southwest England beginning in 1830, drawn by rich lead deposits lying not far beneath the surface of a hilly point of land dividing two branches of the Pecatonica River — the hill that gave Mineral Point its name. At the establishment of Wisconsin Territory in 1836, the nomination notes, the old lead region of southwest Wisconsin was the most populous part of the new territory. The Society's history adds that Mineral Point housed one of Michigan Territory's first land offices and served as a territorial county seat, and that lead and zinc mining remained the region's dominant economy into early statehood.

The Cornish built as they had at home. The nomination describes the site at the base of a bluff-like limestone outcrop, which let miners choose home sites where they could quarry the stone for their houses from their own back yards, on ground facing the hillside where the men dug pits into the surface for lead ore. The settlement along the street was known first as "Shake Rag Under the Hill," then simply Shake Rag: the nomination preserves the explanation — the men, working the eastern ridge with their windlasses and piles of ore, could look down about mealtime and see the housewives shaking a rag to tell them it was time to eat. The houses that survive at the site are the three the nomination documents: Pendarvis House, a one-story stone cottage with a chimney at each gable end; Trelawny, a two-story cottage with a saltbox profile and a rear lean-to built into the rock hillside; and Polperro, a three-and-a-half-story building, stone on the first story and log above, with a large stone chimney on the outside of its northeast gable end. All three stand on their original locations, set directly on bedrock that extends from the base of the quarry. Their fronts are carefully fitted native buff limestone laid in coursed ashlar with minimal mortar — some blocks fitted so tightly, in the nomination's words, that "one cannot force a knife blade between them" — while sides and backs are freer random ashlar. The walls are 18 to 20 inches thick.

The boom did not last. With declining demand for lead and the lure of California gold arriving almost simultaneously, the nomination records, many Cornish miners left for the west near the end of the 1840s, and by 1850 Mineral Point's role as a major center of lead-mining activity was past. A substantial number of the Shake Rag homes nonetheless stood intact a century after they were built — and by the 1920s and 1930s they were being torn down.

The rescue began in 1935. Robert Neal, a Mineral Point native of Cornish ancestry, had returned from London to find many of the cottages vanished (Society's site history); Edgar Hellum had come to Mineral Point seeking building material (nomination). The Society's history records that they bought the first cottage for just $10. Following the Cornish tradition of naming each house, they called their first restoration Pendarvis, after an estate in Cornwall, and gave the others Cornish names as well — Polperro and Trelawny. The restoration standard the nomination documents was exacting: replacement rock came from the original quarry; one gable wall, ruined by water, was rebuilt almost completely with the original builder's craftsmanship; and for Polperro's missing chimney the two men rebuilt the stone chimney themselves under the tutelage of Charles Curtis, "an eighty-five-year-old stonecutter and mason who learned his trade in Cornwall," working as his trained apprentices so they could later supervise or do their own rock work. In 1935 they also opened the Pendarvis House restaurant, serving tea, saffron cake, scones, preserves, and Cornish pasties from recipes passed down from Neal's own grandmother, a mid-1800s Cornish immigrant; the Society's history records that it earned a national reputation, drew diners from every state and many foreign countries, and ran for thirty-five years, until the two men retired in 1970. The Society's Register summary counts the proprietorship at thirty-five years, 1935 to 1970, and credits Neal and Hellum with reclaiming the songs, traditional foods, dwellings, mining tools, furniture, and historic photographs of the Cornish community along with the houses.

The Wisconsin Historical Society acquired the property at the end of that proprietorship — its site history says 1971, its Register summary says 1970; the conflict prints in §6 — and began operating it as a historic site interpreting Cornish settlement and Wisconsin's lead-mining heyday. The Register record dates the National Register listing to January 25, 1971. In 1988, the Society and the Wisconsin Conservation Corps began a 43-acre prairie restoration on Merry Christmas Mine Hill, continued in 1993 with a Department of Natural Resources cleanup of the remaining environmental impact of decades of mining; the Society's history records that the prairie now protects historic mining features amid indigenous grasses and flowering plants.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the 1830s and 1840s — Cornish cottages rising along Shake Rag under the hill, their fronts of knife-tight coursed limestone, the men digging lead pits on the ridge above and watching for a shaken rag at mealtime. **Now:** the same three houses on the same bedrock, restored, furnished, and toured seasonally as a Wisconsin Historical Society site, with gardens and courtyards behind them as Neal and Hellum made them.

**Then:** the early 1930s — the cottages derelict, boarded, and disappearing to demolition, as the Historic American Buildings Survey photograph in §7 shows the saltbox cottage before any restoration: shuttered, weathered, its picket fence sagging. **Now:** that cottage, Trelawny, stands restored beside the one-story Pendarvis House, and the street reads as the mining-era row it was.

**Then:** 1935 to 1970 — a working restaurant in a one-story stone cottage, its Cornish menu financing the restoration of a street, its proprietors living above the visitor center in Trelawny (nomination-era arrangement). **Now:** no restaurant; the site interprets both stories the Society's own pages name — the Cornish miners who built the houses, and Neal and Hellum, the couple who saved them.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The three restored Cornish houses on Shake Rag Street — Pendarvis House (one story, stone, gable-end chimneys), Trelawny (two stories, saltbox profile, rear lean-to into the hillside), and Polperro (three and a half stories, stone first story, log upper stories, exterior stone chimney) — all on their original locations and bedrock foundations (National Register nomination, 1970)
- The rowhouse up the hill, acquired and restored by Neal and Hellum after the first houses (Society's site history; the history gives it no separate dates in the pages checked)
- Gardens, walks, and courtyards connecting the houses at the rear, begun by Neal and Hellum in the 1930s to recall the gardens Cornish settlers planted (nomination; Society's site history)
- Interiors furnished with antique period items obtained largely from the Mineral Point locality (nomination, describing the restored houses as of 1970)
- Merry Christmas Mine Hill — 43 acres of restored prairie protecting historic mining features, a joint Society and Conservation Corps project begun 1988 with a Department of Natural Resources cleanup in 1993 (Society's site history)
- The Register record's full count for the property: 11 contributing buildings, 1 contributing site, and 1 contributing object (Wisconsin Historical Society Register record NR1806) — the nominated complex is larger than the three named houses, and this entry does not enumerate the remainder from the sources checked

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the conditions are the operator's: a seasonal site, house interiors on guided tours, and a public street shared with private homes:

1. **From Shake Rag Street, on public ground:** Read the row first — stone cottages at the base of the outcrop, facing the hillside where their builders dug lead pits, the ground plan the nomination describes. The miners quarried house stone from their own back yards here.
2. **At Pendarvis House:** The knife-blade front — coursed ashlar fitted with minimal mortar, walls 18 to 20 inches thick, a chimney at each gable end. This is the cottage Neal and Hellum restored first and ran as their restaurant for thirty-five years.
3. **At Trelawny:** The saltbox cottage — two-story front, roof sloping to the rear lean-to built into the rock hillside. Compare it against the pre-restoration HABS photograph in §7: same house, boarded and sagging, before 1935.
4. **At Polperro:** Stone below, log above — and the chimney Neal and Hellum rebuilt stone by stone as apprentices to an eighty-five-year-old Cornish mason, because no one else was left who could teach them.
5. **On Merry Christmas Mine Hill:** The prairie over the diggings — the pits the windlass men worked, now under restored grassland that the Society manages to protect the mining features themselves.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The construction dates do not reconcile across the primary sources, and all forms print. The Historic American Buildings Survey records for the Shake Rag houses carry "ca. 1828 Initial Construction." The 1970 nomination dates Pendarvis House "c. 1835" and says the log-and-stone house was "also built c. 1835." The Wisconsin Historical Society's Register record dates the complex "ca.1842–ca.1852," Pendarvis House specifically to 1845, Trelawny to "ca. 1842," and Polperro to the mid-1840s. This entry prints the spread rather than choosing: §2 uses no single build year for any house.

The Society's acquisition year conflicts inside the Society's own publications: its site history says "In 1971, the Wisconsin Historical Society acquired the property," while its Register summary says "In 1970, the Wisconsin Historical Society acquired the property." The nomination, prepared September 28, 1970, still lists Edgar Hellum and Robert M. Neal as the owners, and the Register listing itself is dated January 25, 1971 — so the transfer belongs to that turn of the year, but the two Society forms are printed here as they stand, unreconciled.

The $10 purchase price for the first cottage is carried by the Society's site history alone among the sources checked; no second primary source in this pass states it, and it prints attributed to that history. The Shake Rag name explanation — the rag shaken at mealtime, seen from the ridge — is the nomination's recorded tradition and prints as the nomination carries it, not as independently verified fact. The nomination-era uses of the houses (Pendarvis House as restaurant, Trelawny's first floor as visitor center and waiting room, its upstairs as the owners' living quarters and the restaurant kitchen) are the arrangement of 1970 as the nomination records it; this entry does not assert the present interior arrangement of any house. Finally, the HABS frame in §7 is titled only "Cornish Miner's House No. 2" in its own record; its identification as Trelawny is this entry's, made from the nomination's description — saltbox profile, rear lean-to into the rock hillside, cut-limestone front — matching the frame point for point, with the one-story Pendarvis House visible at the frame's left edge where it stands today. The identification is stated here so a reader can weigh it.
