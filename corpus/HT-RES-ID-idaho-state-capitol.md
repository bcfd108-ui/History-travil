## 1. Trip gate
Idaho State Historical Society currently publishes **Monday–Sunday, 7 a.m.–7 p.m.**, at **700 W. Jefferson Street, Boise**. Use Mountain Time. These are building visitor hours, not guaranteed access to every room or a scheduled docent tour. The Commission directs tour scheduling and detailed operational information to the Legislature; its current pages could not be retrieved because of robots restrictions. No specific guided-tour departure is promised. [1,2]

This is a working government building. The official 2021 usage manual distinguishes public space from offices/reception areas with limited access and from legislative space subject to separate rules. Respect doors, barriers, staff direction and posted restrictions. No dome, roof, service tunnel, committee-room or office access is assumed. The manual is a dated source, not a claim that all rules are unchanged in 2026. Current entry screening details and day-specific closures remain unverified. [3]

## 2. The story
The Capitol is not one construction moment. ISHS Reference Series 133 reports the March 1905 authorization and a commission that chose to act as its own contractor. It describes acquiring equipment, contracting portions of the work and purchasing the Jellison Brothers' Table Rock quarry on October 26, 1906. Construction strategy and material procurement shaped the monument as much as architectural style did. This is an official historical synthesis; the original commission minutes and purchase deed were not inspected. [4]

The Commission credits architects John E. Tourtellotte and Charles Hummel. It also records the use of penitentiary labor to transport large sandstone blocks. Do not describe the completed stonework as simply the achievement of architects while omitting coerced labor. No named quarry worker's account was located, and this draft invents neither conditions nor testimony. [5]

The state’s later restoration master plan supplies an institutional primary voice about the pressure of everyday government: **“modifications have included the reconfiguration of spaces in order to accommodate new agencies, programs and additional staffing.”** This is the plan's executive-summary language, not a quotation by Tourtellotte. Only its indexed executive-summary passage was retrieved; the full PDF exceeded the browsing fetch limit. [6]

The point is a productive tension: a symbol meant to endure still has to accommodate people, records and changing technology. The Commission records rededication on **January 9, 2010** after restoration and underground expansion. Its project-management account attributes the project’s funding and direction to 2006 legislative action. Those are agency project records, not an independent audit or a substitute for the original appropriation. [7,8]

One object makes continuity tangible. Charles L. Ostner's **1869** Washington equestrian sculpture was carved in yellow pine and originally displayed outside the Territorial Capitol. The Commission documents later gilding, relocation and conservation; its present supporting base incorporates old black marble and wood from trees removed during the restoration. Survival here includes movement and repair, not unaltered fabric. [9]

## 3. Then / now
The central building and dome came before the full wings. The official historical account places central completion in late 1912, wing work in 1919–1920, and distinguishes construction cost from furnishings. [4] The 2022 Commission handout instead labels the central phase 1905–1913 and links 1913 to the inaugural ball. These are different completion/use milestones, not a reason to assign every part a single date. [10]

Today, exhibitions and legislative work occupy the same building. Look for changes in floor levels, interpretation and the relationship between older aboveground form and later underground accommodation, without assuming every finish is original. That is a proposed reading of the documented phases, not a field-verified fabric survey.

## 4. What's still there
- **Garden Level rotunda:** ISHS identifies *Governing Idaho: How People and Policy Shape Our State* as the permanent introductory exhibit. Public visitor access only when that area is open. [1]
- **Fourth-floor Statuary Hall entrance:** the ISHS exhibit list places Washington and the Winged Victory replica here. The latter was a gift from France associated with the postwar Merci Train, not an ancient original. [1]
- **Exterior sandstone and central dome:** Commission-documented features; inspect from permitted public space, not rooftops or the roadway. [5]
- **Active rooms:** chamber galleries, offices and the Lincoln Auditorium are not automatically available merely because the building is open. Apply the access distinction in section 1. [3]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Suggested interpretive order, not a measured route or accessibility certification:
1. Start at an authorized public entrance and orient with the Garden Level exhibit when open.
2. Examine the central interior from permitted visitor circulation. Read it as both architecture and an adaptation to working government.
3. If the relevant floor is open, end at the Washington sculpture and its conservation interpretation. **Strongest story moment: a nineteenth-century wooden figure survives inside a stone statehouse because successive people moved, repaired and reinterpreted it.** [9]

If interior areas are closed, use the exterior and official photo catalog instead. Do not force access to finish a sequence. No ride, lift operation, step-free route or precise duration was verified.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Central phase: late-1912 completion versus 1913 public-use milestone; retain both with scope. Wings: construction finished 1920; the official handout dates dedication January 3, 1921. Do not conflate completion and dedication. [4,10]
- Reference Series 133 contains an apparent erroneous “Idaho Admission Act of 1898” statement. Do not repeat it as fact or silently use that passage to calculate land-sale financing. Original legislation and commission minutes remain research targets. [4]
- Some tourism descriptions imply an individually listed Capitol. The verified register record here is **Boise Capitol Area District**, with the Capitol among its components. No separate individual listing was verified. [11]
- No original labor testimony, 1905 construction contract, current security protocol, live chamber access or field-tested accessible itinerary was inspected. No present political-office holder or legislative activity is inferred from old photographs.
