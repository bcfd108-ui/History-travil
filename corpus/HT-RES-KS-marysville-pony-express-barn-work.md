## 2. The story
The 1973 National Register nomination identifies **T. Lee Holloway** as stock tender here in 1860–61 and says he later spoke about the barn to local historians and newspapers. The nomination also identifies **Ernest Cottrell** as later stable operator, 1903–14, who recounted what his father had told him. Holloway is a named worker, but his original testimony has not been examined; Ernest's inherited recollection is not firsthand evidence of the Pony Express period. [3]

NPS names **Joseph H. Cottrell and Hank Williams** as builders and describes a lease to Russell, Majors & Waddell for livery use. The north end was the blacksmith shop, with horse stalls on the other side. The actual lease and payroll were not inspected. Read this as the infrastructure and labor of the relay, not just a story about riders. [3–4]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a stone barn built in 1859–60 housed shop and stable functions. The nomination describes later changes after apparent fire damage in 1876 and later commercial uses. **Now:** a museum incorporating the historic barn; don't claim an unchanged 1860 room. Current NPS distinguishes the stable from the historic Barrett Hotel station location, while the operator uses “home station” language for the barn and the nomination notes the home/relay distinction was debated. [1,3–4] No verified matched photo pair.

## 4. What's still there
The historic stone barn and museum interpretation within the admitted public footprint. Display and stall/forge access are staff-controlled; no working stable, historic hotel entry, railroad passage, trail following or private-property access inferred. [1,4]

When closed, use the NPS and nomination material from home; no quiz or relay reenactment.
