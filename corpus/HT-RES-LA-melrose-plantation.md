# Melrose Plantation — Yucca Plantation of the Metoyers, Isle Brevelle
HT-RES-LA-melrose-plantation · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The ground begins with Marie Thérèse, called Coincoin, born enslaved in 1742 in the household of Louis Juchereau de St. Denis, founder of Natchitoches. Leased as a housekeeper to the French merchant Claude Thomas Pierre Metoyer, she lived with him for nineteen years and bore ten children. Metoyer purchased and freed her and several of the children, and at the end of their union she held a small tract — about sixty-eight acres in the later record — with a yearly allowance. From tobacco, cattle and bear grease, and from land grants made to her and her sons, the family she headed became the leading free Creole family of color on Cane River, in a community of *gens de couleur libre* who owned land, businesses and, in their turn, enslaved people. That full record — freedom built inside a slaveholding economy, by a woman born enslaved who also bought enslaved labor — is the record this ground carries, and it is not simplified here. [2, 3, 6, 7]

In 1796 her son Louis Metoyer received a grant of some 911 acres on Cane River. The operator's history places first work on the east bank soon after, and development of the west bank — the present museum ground — between about 1810 and 1815, when the Yucca House, the African House and a large barn rose. The Yucca House is a *colombage* frame filled with *bousillage*, mud mixed with moss and hair; the African House is a near-square, heavy-timber building whose great hipped roof oversails its walls by roughly ten feet on log struts, giving it an umbrella profile found nowhere else in the parish. In 1832 Louis began the Big House and died on March 11 of that year; his son Jean Baptiste Louis Metoyer finished it in 1833, a raised Louisiana French Colonial house with a brick basement story, galleries front and rear, and French doors opening from every room. [2, 3, 4, 6]

Debt closed the founding era. By 1847 the young heir Theophile Louis Metoyer had lost nearly everything to creditors, and Melrose was sold to brothers Henry and Hypolite Hertzog for $8,340. Under the Hertzogs the place stayed a working cotton plantation; the widowed Fanny Hertzog enlarged the two-room raised cottage into an eight-room house, and the Red River Campaign of 1863–64 passed armies across this river country without destroying the house — a survival the operator's history itself calls unexplained. The operator also credits Fanny Hertzog with a Freedmen's school at Melrose in Reconstruction, the first formal schooling for formerly enslaved people on the plantation. Joseph Henry bought the property in 1884 and gave it the name Melrose, after Sir Walter Scott's Melrose Abbey. [3]

The third era made the ground famous a second way. Joseph Henry's son John Hampton Henry and his wife Carmelite "Cammie" Garrett Henry took the house in 1899. Cammie Henry expanded the Big House — the hexagonal garçonnière pavilions at the gallery ends and the rear wing are hers — rescued old buildings, and moved threatened parish cabins onto the grounds: the Writers Cabin, the Weaving House and the Bindery standing here today are her rescues, not Metoyer buildings. After her husband's death in 1918 she kept the farm running and opened Melrose to working writers and artists, among them Lyle Saxon, who wrote *Children of Strangers* here, and, in the Society of Architectural Historians' list, Gwen Bristow, Erskine Caldwell, Caroline Dorman, William Faulkner, Alberta Kinsey, William Spratling and John Steinbeck. One long guest, Francois Mignon, arrived in the early 1940s for six weeks and stayed some thirty-two years; he was born Frank Mineah of Cortland, New York, and the tales he polished into Cane River lore still have to be separated from the documented record — the 2014 St. Augustine nomination, citing Elizabeth Shown Mills, traces at least one durable Melrose myth to his writing. [3, 4, 8, 9]

The plantation's own cook outlasted the colony. Clementine Hunter, born at Hidden Hill Plantation in late 1886 or early 1887, came to Melrose as a young field worker, later cooked for the Henry household, and began painting around age fifty with paints left behind by a visiting artist. The state encyclopedia counts more than five thousand works across her life — cotton work, washing, fishing, worship, funerals — painted on whatever surface came to hand. In 1955 she completed her largest work: mural panels in oil on plywood, installed on the upper floor of the African House, picturing Cane River life as she had lived it. Northwestern State University, which in the segregation years had kept her from entering an exhibition of her own work, gave her an honorary Doctor of Fine Arts in 1986. She died on January 1, 1988. [3, 5]

When Cammie Henry's son died in 1970 the Henry family sold Melrose at public auction. Furnishings went to the highest bidders, and Hunter murals were torn from other buildings and sold; the African House murals alone stayed. The land buyer, Southdown Land Company, in 1971 donated the 6.55 acres carrying the historic buildings to the Association of Natchitoches Women for the Preservation of Historic Natchitoches — today the Association for the Preservation of Historic Natchitoches (APHN) — which restored the complex and still owns and operates it as a museum. The plantation was entered in the National Register on June 13, 1972, and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1974. [2, 3, 4, 6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The Metoyer plantation on the west bank of Cane River at Melrose, Natchitoches Parish — Louis Metoyer's 1796 grant ground, developed as Yucca Plantation and renamed Melrose under Joseph Henry after 1884. Nine historic structures stand on the 6.55-acre museum parcel the operator received in 1971; the wider plantation acreage is not part of the visit. [2, 3]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Melrose on the Cane, 3533 Hwy 119, Melrose, LA 71452, operated by APHN. Check in at the Bindery gift shop. Guided tours (about 45 minutes) are the only access to the Big House interior and the upper floor of the African House with the Hunter murals; a self-guided grounds ticket covers the grounds and ground-level exteriors. The guided route involves more than one staircase; site terrain is brick paths, gravel and grass. No pets or smoking on the grounds. Groups of ten or more book ahead at 318-379-0055; Monday–Wednesday is reserved for pre-booked groups of ten or more. [1]

## 5. Public progression

Check in at the Bindery and take the guided tour first — it is the only way upstairs. Read the buildings in the order the ground gives them: Yucca House for the earliest surviving Creole construction on the site, African House for the building the whole country argues about, then the Big House for what a free family of color built at its peak in 1833, and the Henry additions for the preservation era that saved all of it. Finish on the grounds with the moved cabins, reading them for what they are — Cammie Henry's salvage, honestly labeled. **Strongest story moment · The upper room of the African House.** A cook who was never taught to read painted the whole working life of this river — baptisms, funerals, cotton, Saturday night — on plywood panels in the oldest-feeling room on the place, and the panels are still on its walls. Keep the Metoyer record whole while you stand there: the family that built this house held enslaved people, and the woman who painted it descended from the people plantations held. Both facts are the site. [1–9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Hours conflict.** The operator's own Visit page (checked this run) closes the site Monday–Wednesday and opens Thursday–Sunday. The National Trust Distinctive Destinations page lists Wednesday–Sunday, 10:00 a.m.–4:15 p.m., and older printed brochures list daily hours. The operator's page governs; the conflict is printed so a traveler is not caught by the stale listings. Admission on the operator's page: guided tour adults $20, seniors and veterans $18, ages 12–17 $15, ages 6–11 $10, APHN members free; self-guided grounds $15 / $10 / $8. A 2026 travel guide quoting $10 admission conflicts with the operator and is not followed. [1, 7]
- **The 1972 nomination is dated scholarship.** The 2014 St. Augustine nomination warns in plain terms that the National Register and National Historic Landmark nominations for Melrose "were completed forty years ago and contain many inaccuracies and misinterpretations." Where this dossier repeats 1972 description detail, it is building fabric description, not settled history. [4, 9]
- **African House origins are disputed.** The 1972 nomination calls the African House's umbrella roof "believed to be of African derivation" and repeats the claim that the house and Ghana House may be direct African designs. The 2014 nomination, following Mills (2013), answers that the buildings are better understood as Creole architecture with strong French antecedents alongside Native American and African influences, and calls the architect-of-African-forms story erroneous in many regards. This dossier prints both and adopts neither. The related tradition naming Louis Metoyer as designer-builder of African forms has, per Mills, no primary source, and may originate with Francois Mignon's writings. [4, 9]
- **Building dates spread across sources.** The operator dates west-bank development (Yucca House, African House, barn) to about 1810–1815; the state encyclopedia places the first buildings soon after the 1796 grant; SAH dates Yucca House 1796–1810 and the African House 1800–1830; the HABS record gives the African House as about 1820. No dendrochronology or building archaeology resolving the spread was retrieved in this pass. [2, 3, 6, 8, 10]
- **Grant acreage.** The operator's history gives Louis Metoyer's 1796 grant as 911 acres; the 2014 St. Augustine nomination gives 912 acres. The one-acre difference is unresolved and immaterial to the visit, but it is printed rather than silently harmonized. [3, 9]
- **Hunter's arrival age.** The operator's history says Hunter began as a field hand at Melrose at twelve; other accounts place the family's move to Melrose when she was about fifteen. Her birth itself is dated only to late 1886 or early 1887. This dossier says "young field worker" and leaves the age open. [3, 5]
- **Big House authorship shorthand.** The 1972 nomination's statement that the Big House "is known to have been built in 1833 by Louis Metoyer, Jr." compresses the documented sequence — begun by Louis Metoyer in 1832, finished after his March 1832 death by his son Jean Baptiste Louis Metoyer — given by both the operator and SAH. The fuller sequence is used here. [3, 4, 8]
- **National Historic Landmark date.** Landmark status in 1974 is stated by the operator and consistent across sources; the exact designation day was not verified against the NPS Landmark database in this pass and is not printed. The National Register entry date, June 13, 1972, is verified from the NP Gallery record. [2, 4]
- **Clementine Hunter House and Ghana House current condition** were not independently verified beyond the 1972 nomination and HABS-era records; the operator's current building-by-building condition notes were not published on the pages inspected. The NPS Cane River Creole Historic Resource Study (2019) is catalogued at the NPS DataStore but its full text was not read in this pass. [10, 11]

## 7. Historical photo records

**Inspected federal survey frames held.** The Historic American Buildings Survey documented the African House murals in 2004; the records sit in the Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division under survey HABS LA,35-MELRO,1B (building survey LA-2-69-B), and the file records were inspected through their Wikimedia Commons record pages, which carry the Library of Congress source links, in this run. Frames inspected as records: LA,35-MELRO,1B-12, elevation of Hunter's mural "Baptism," southwest corner, south wall, with scale (LOC item la0212.photos.204859p); 1B-17, mural "Photo and Airplane," northeast corner, north wall (la0212.photos.204864p); 1B-18, mural "Africa House–Melrose," northeast corner, east wall (la0212.photos.204865p); 1B-21, interior general view from the southwest corner (la0212.photos.204868p); and 1B-22, interior general view from the northeast corner (la0212.photos.204869p). Credit for a cleared use of a frame would be *Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, Historic American Buildings Survey, HABS LA,35-MELRO,1B-[number]*. The Commons records carry the standard HABS note that the survey photographs are National Park Service program records; that status covers the federal photograph, not Clementine Hunter's artwork pictured in it — the murals remain the artist's work and no right to reproduce the artwork is adopted here. The 1972 nomination also cites an earlier HABS survey, LA-69, with records at the Library of Congress. A second NP Gallery asset for this property (asset 7eb96fe1, a 7.6 MB PDF of nomination photographs) exists but was not opened in this pass; nothing from it is adopted. The state encyclopedia's Melrose photograph is credited there to Eddie Cazayoux; it was seen as a credited page image only and is not adopted. No Photo Point. [4, 5, 10]
