# HT-RES-MA-longfellow-house — The House That Sugar Built, and the Family Who Took Their Freedom Back

Division MA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On Brattle Street in Cambridge stands a yellow Georgian mansion built in 1759 on money made by enslaved people cutting sugar cane in Jamaica — and kept, in its first years, by enslaved people working in its own rooms and stables. Three full American stories meet at this one address, and the National Park Service now tells them together: Cuba and Anthony Vassall, separated by sale and reunited in freedom on this ground, and their son Darby, born enslaved here in 1769 and still fighting for Black freedom into the Civil War; George Washington, who made the house his first long-term headquarters in 1775–76; and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, who lived and wrote here from 1843 to 1882. The house is open today by guided tour, free of charge.

## 1. Trip gate

The operator is the National Park Service; its guided tours page states the terms. Entrance to the house is by guided tour only, and all tours are free of charge. For the May 22–November 2, 2026 season, tours are offered Friday, Saturday, Sunday, and Monday, including holidays. Same-day reservations can be made by phone (617-876-4491) starting at 9:30 am; walk-in visitors are welcome; advance reservations are not accepted for public tours. All tours are subject to staff availability, and the park advises calling to confirm tour times.

Three formats run in season. The Hourly Tour runs hourly from 10:00 am to 4:00 pm, lasts 55 minutes, covers about six rooms, linking slavery and freedom, Washington's headquarters, and Longfellow's legacy. The Express Tour (10:30 am, 3:30 pm) lasts 30 minutes and covers the first floor only, about four rooms. The 250th Deep Dive: Headquarters of a Revolution runs at 11:30 am and 2:30 pm for 55 minutes. All tours are limited to a maximum of twelve visitors. Standard and Deep Dive tours visit the first and second floors. Before entering, all bags and food and beverages, including water, must be stored in a complimentary Visitor Center locker. The Visitor Center and first floor are ADA accessible. Photography is permitted with flash and video off; visitors stay on a marked pathway.

Outside the house, the grounds are open year-round from dawn to dusk (the Superintendent's Compendium: daily, sunrise to sunset); the exterior porches are always closed to the public, and in winter the house is closed except by appointment. The brochure gives the approach: 105 Brattle Street, within walking distance of Harvard Square and its parking garages; no on-site parking other than designated accessible spaces; by MBTA Red Line to Harvard Square, exit on Church Street to Brattle Street, turn right past Mason Street.

## 2. The story

The mansion was constructed in 1759 for John Vassall Jr., a British subject and sugar plantation owner whose wealth derived from Jamaican sugar plantations, where the family enslaved hundreds of people. It stood among the estates of fellow loyalist elites along the street later known as Tory Row. The wealth was not only imported: John and Elizabeth Vassall also enslaved people at their Cambridge estate. The park's early history page names at least ten people enslaved here between 1759 and 1774 — Cuba, Dinah, Malcolm, William, James (a teenager), infants Darby and Cyrus, a "baby boy," and likely two others whose names are unconfirmed — who worked as domestic servants in the house and laborers in its orchards, garden, small farm, and stables.

Cuba was born on Antigua, where she, her mother Abba, and her five siblings were enslaved by Isaac Royall Sr. Royall brought at least 27 enslaved people with him to his Medford estate in 1737, Abba and her children among them, and in 1739 bequeathed Abba and her six children to his daughter Penelope. In 1742 Penelope married Henry Vassall, John Vassall's uncle, and took Cuba with her as property to her new Cambridge home at what is now 94 Brattle Street. There Cuba met and married Anthony — Tony — a coachman enslaved by Henry Vassall; oral accounts in the park's research trace Anthony from the Spanish Empire by way of kidnapping to Jamaica, where Henry Vassall purchased him.

Anthony and Cuba raised at least five children — James, Flora (born 1767), Darby (born 1769), Cyrus, and Nancy — but their enslavers separated the family repeatedly. Around Henry Vassall's death in early 1769, Cuba was sold to his nephew John, owner of 105 Brattle Street; two-year-old Flora may have been sold at the same time or earlier. In May 1769 Cuba gave birth to Darby at 105 Brattle Street, and John Vassall eventually "gave" the young Darby to George Reed of Woburn. Then, in December 1772, Anthony traveled to Billerica, where Flora was enslaved, and delivered £20 — provided, according to the deed, by his enslaver Penelope Vassall — to "manumit release and make free" his daughter; the deed released Flora to her still-enslaved mother.

In September 1774, after the Powder Alarm, Brattle Street's loyalist elite fled to Boston, the Vassalls among them. The people they enslaved remained in Cambridge — and seized their freedom. Anthony and Cuba reunited their family on a portion of the former estate, living in another dwelling on the grounds and farming three-quarters of an acre. Darby, now six, had returned home by July 1775 after his enslaver George Reed died. That same July, General George Washington arrived to take command of the Continental Army besieging Boston and chose the house as his headquarters for nine months, through the siege, while the Vassall family lived free on the estate around him. A story first recorded after Darby's death in 1861 holds that the boy refused Washington's order to work unpaid — "no gentleman, he wanted [a] boy to work without wages" — and the park prints it with its own caution. Martha Washington arrived that December, and in March 1776 Washington celebrated the British evacuation of Boston from this house.

Freedom on the estate was precarious. Anthony earned paid work, including on the confiscated Royall estate in Medford, and secured £222 from the confiscated Vassall estate. In 1780, facing eviction as the state prepared to sell the estate, they petitioned the General Court to remain on their small portion of land; the petition was denied. In early 1781 they petitioned again — "though dwelling in a land of freedom, both himself and his wife have spent almost sixty years of their lives in slavery" — asking not to be "reduced to the painful necessity of begging for bread." That petition succeeded: Anthony was granted £12 annually. The family was evicted that same year anyway. In 1787 they bought a home at what is now the corner of Shepard Street and Massachusetts Avenue in Cambridge, and later five additional acres. Anthony died on September 2, 1811, at the reported age of 98. Cuba received the pension after him and died on September 16, 1812. Their burial locations are unknown.

Darby Vassall's life runs from this house's slavery to the Civil War. By 1796 Darby and his brother Cyrus owned property on Boston's May Street (now Revere Street) and were founding members of the African Society. In 1825, toasting at an event celebrating Haitian independence, Darby said: "Freedom — May the freedom of Hayti be a glorious harbinger of the time when the color of a man shall no longer be a pretext for depriving him of his liberty." He supported the New England Anti-Slavery Society, and in 1861, at 92, signed a petition against the Fugitive Slave Act. On March 22, 1855, Darby came back to 105 Brattle Street to visit the poet now living in the house where he had been born enslaved; Longfellow recorded the visit in his journal — "old Mr. Vassall (born a slave in this house in 1769)" — and two days later recorded a $10 donation to him. Darby died on October 12, 1861, and was buried, by permission granted in 1843, in the Henry Vassall family tomb under Christ Church in Cambridge; William Cooper Nell's Liberator obituary praised his memory and lifelong anti-slavery work.

The house's next era began with Andrew Craigie, the Continental Army's first Apothecary General, who bought the house — the park's records give the year variously, as §6 prints — and added the east and west one-story piazzas and the service quarters. After Andrew's death in 1819, his widow Elizabeth, deep in debt from lavish living and failed investments, took in boarders. In 1837 a young Harvard professor, Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, rented two rooms — "They were once George Washington's chambers," he marveled — and in July 1843 married Fanny Appleton, whose father Nathan presented the house to the couple as a wedding gift. Longfellow lived here from 1843 until his death in 1882; Fanny died in a household fire in 1861. Longfellow had the carriage house built in 1844 and added the forecourt balustrade in 1872.

Longfellow's daughter Alice lived in the house until her death in 1928 and preserved it and its furnishings. In 1913 the Longfellow House Trust was established by Henry and Fanny's children to preserve the home; it opened to the public in the 1920s. The site was donated to the National Park Service in 1972; the park's 50th anniversary page marks October 9, 2022 as fifty years since establishment, placing it on October 9, 1972. The park was later renamed Longfellow House–Washington's Headquarters National Historic Site; the park brochure's timeline carries that name change at 2011. Descendants of Anthony and Cuba Vassall remain, in the park's words, stewards of their ancestors' legacies, and the park's research continues with its 2025 study Black History at the Vassall Estate.

## 3. Then / now

In 1759 this was a working estate, not a house on a lot. John Vassall built the mansion among farms becoming the lavish estates of Tory Row; the enslaved people held here worked a small farm, orchards, gardens, and stables as well as the house. After 1774 the Vassall family farmed part of it in freedom while Washington's headquarters occupied the mansion. The two-acre grounds today are, in the park brochure's words, the core of the much larger 1759 estate, with Colonial-era features still evident.

The street has changed around the house more than the house has changed on the street. Brattle Street is now a residential street in Cambridge, a short walk from Harvard Square, and the house still overlooks the Charles River — the park brochure calls it the best of the remaining Tory Row mansions. The Craigies' and Longfellows' landscape work is still legible: the wooden fencing and western carriage loop, the lilac beds, the preserved elms, and the 1872 balustrade. In 1775 this was a headquarters ringed by an army of nearly 20,000; today it is a 1.98-acre park unit visited by more than 50,000 people a year, entering the house in groups of twelve.

## 4. What's still there

The house itself is the artifact: the National Historic Landmark file describes a two-and-a-half-story, almost-square frame house, little altered, its interior woodwork intact: a five-bay facade with a central pavilion and pediment, a modillioned cornice, four giant Ionic pilasters, a double-hipped roof, and a central-hall plan with two interior chimneys. The room to the right of the front central hall was Washington's council chamber and later Longfellow's study; the rooms above served as Washington's quarters. The large library behind the study was redecorated in 1793. The east and west piazzas and rear service quarters are Craigie-era additions.

Inside, the tour passes through approximately six rooms furnished with pieces original to the Longfellow family's residence in the 1800s and early 1900s. Behind them stand large collections: the park brochure counts 35,000 furnishing and decorative arts items, a 14,000-volume library, and 775,000 archival items. Each tour concludes with a self-paced visit to the temporary exhibit on Washington's headquarters and its memory. Outside stand the forecourt, the carriage loop, the formal garden, and the 1844 carriage house, now a program and lecture space. The Historic American Buildings Survey documented the house in 13 sheets in 1935. What is not there matters too: no dwelling of the Vassall family's freedom years survives as a marked structure in the park material.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Start on the grounds, which are open year-round from dawn to dusk and free: walk the forecourt first, reading the facade the way the circa 1980 photograph in §7 shows it, then take the carriage loop and the formal garden. This order is free, needs no reservation, and is the whole visit in winter, when the house is closed except by appointment.

In season, go to the Visitor Center, where all bags, food, and drink, including water, go into a complimentary locker before the tour. Same-day reservations open by phone at 9:30 am and walk-ins are welcome, so arrive early for the first Hourly Tour — 55 minutes, about six rooms across two floors, capped at twelve people. If time is short, the Express Tour (10:30 am, 3:30 pm) covers the first floor in 30 minutes; the Deep Dive (11:30 am, 2:30 pm) is the version built around the headquarters story. The tour ends with a self-paced temporary exhibit; budget time after the guided portion.

Finish back outside. The grounds repay a second pass after the rooms — they read differently once you know who worked this ground and who farmed it in freedom. Groups of eight or larger should arrange a Thursday group tour in advance. There is no on-site parking beyond designated accessible spaces; Harvard Square's garages and the Red Line are the park's own suggested approach.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The year Andrew Craigie bought the house is printed three ways in the park's own records; this entry does not pick one. The park brochure's text and timeline say Craigie bought the house in 1791. The park's Forecourt place page says, "When the Craigies bought this property in 1792." The National Historic Landmark nomination file says, "Dr. Andrew Craigie, who acquired the house in 1793."

The count of people enslaved at 105 Brattle Street is also printed at two scopes. The park's early history page states that John Vassall enslaved at least ten people on his Cambridge estate across 1759–1774. The park's article "Though Dwelling in a Land of Freedom" names seven people as those known to have been enslaved at the address as of 1774. The figures describe different windows; no reconciled count is printed here.

The park carries the story of young Darby refusing Washington's order to work unpaid with its own caution — first recorded after Darby's death in 1861, "the veracity of the historical encounter is unclear" — and §2 prints it as a recorded story, not a verified event.

The register file itself contains errors this entry does not silently correct. The 1961 National Survey form gives Longfellow's dates as "1807-1862," against 1882, his death year elsewhere in the file and the park's material. The landmark file's supplementary sheet prints the address as "103 Brattle Street," against 105 Brattle Street everywhere else in the file. The file also gives the house 18 rooms in one form and four rooms on each floor in another; both stand.

Two dates could not be pinned down in this pass. The park brochure's timeline carries the name change to include Washington at 2011; no firmer date was verified from a primary record here, so the year stands as the brochure prints it. A 1979 date for the site appears in none of the park's own published records consulted — the park's dates are the 1913 Trust, the house opening to the public in the 1920s, and establishment and donation in 1972 — so no 1979 milestone is printed elsewhere in this entry. The park states that research into Cuba Vassall's life is ongoing, and the names of two children enslaved here in 1774 remain unknown.

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** A winter frame, taken head-on from the street side of the forecourt. Snow covers the ground, the steps, and the white balustrade in the foreground. The house fills the frame: ochre-yellow clapboards, five bays wide, four giant white pilasters rising the full height of the facade, a central pediment with a semicircular fan window above the white front door, and a small flag in the second-floor center window. Two dormers break the dark roof; two tall brick chimneys stand at either end. The one-story piazzas at both sides sit in deep shadow. A bare tree rises behind the right side, and the image carries the pale cyan-blue cast of an aged slide scan. It is a plain, square-on record of the facade a visitor reads from Brattle Street today.
