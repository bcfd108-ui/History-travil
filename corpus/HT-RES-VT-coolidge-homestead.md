# HT-RES-VT-coolidge-homestead — The Village That Swore In a President

Division VT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Town: Plymouth (Plymouth Notch), Vermont

In a bowl-shaped valley in the Green Mountains, where two local roads branch west and north off Vermont Route 100A, stands the village of Plymouth Notch — essentially the village center of Plymouth, Vermont, kept as it was in the lifetime of the thirtieth President of the United States. Calvin Coolidge was born here on July 4, 1872, in the small house attached to the rear of his father's general store, spent his boyhood in the farmhouse across the street, and in that farmhouse, at 2:47 a.m. on August 3, 1923, took the presidential oath of office from his own father, a notary public, by the light of a kerosene lamp, hours after word reached the village that President Warren G. Harding had died. The district holds the whole village that made those events possible: the store and birthplace, the homestead, the church, the schoolhouse, the cheese factory, the farmhouses of relatives and neighbors, and, up the steep hill, the cemetery where Coolidge rests among seven generations of his family.

## 1. Trip gate

The President Calvin Coolidge State Historic Site is operated by the State of Vermont through its Division for Historic Preservation, at 3780 Route 100A, Plymouth, VT 05056; its published telephone number is (802) 672-1195. It is a seasonal, ticketed site: the visitor walks the village itself — its green, its two roads, and the interiors of its historic buildings — rather than a fenced campus set apart from the place.

The State's own pages post the season plainly. For 2026 the site runs May 22 to September 20, Tuesday through Sunday, 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., and September 21 to October 25, seven days a week, 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. Admission is charged: $12 for adults 17 and up, $4 for children 6 to 16, children under 6 free, with a group rate for parties of ten or more. One standing exception belongs in the gate: during the 2026 season the Coolidge Homestead — the boyhood home and oath house — is closed for essential preservation work, the only building closed; the birthplace, the Cilley Store, the Aldrich House, the Summer White House, the school, the barns, the cheese factory, and the museum with its gift shop all remain open, and significant furnishings from the Homestead have been moved into the museum exhibit for the duration. Current hours, fees, and building status are as the State publishes them at historicsites.vermont.gov.

The visitor boundary is the village, and it is a lived-in one. The roads through Plymouth Notch are public roads; some district buildings are private residences, and the Plymouth Notch Cemetery is owned by the town. Parking is at the Museum & Education Center and across from the cheese factory. Pets are permitted on the grounds on a leash, but not in the buildings, service dogs excepted.

## 2. The story

The Coolidges were among the earliest settlers in the Plymouth area, and the village the visitor walks is substantially the village the family built its life in. The oldest building in the district is the Old Coolidge Farmhouse, built about 1800 by the President's grandfather and the birthplace of his father, Colonel John Coolidge; it retains its barn and outbuildings. The general store at the village center was built before 1835, a two-story frame and clapboard block with a storage ell, and behind it, about 1840, the one-and-a-half-story cottage that would become the birthplace. John Coolidge was already operating the store at the time of his marriage in 1868 — to Victoria Josephine Moor, whose childhood home, the Wilder House across the village, had been built about 1830 as a tavern — though he did not purchase the store building until later. The hall above the store served the village for public meetings and dances.

John Calvin Coolidge was born on July 4, 1872, in the downstairs bedroom of the small house at the rear of the store. Four years later, in 1876, the family moved across the street to the house now known as the Coolidge Homestead, which Colonel Coolidge had purchased that year: a one-and-a-half-story frame and clapboard farmhouse with attached woodshed and barn, to which he added the front porch and a two-story bay window. Calvin lived there continuously from the age of four until 1887, when he left for Black River Academy at nearby Ludlow. The village around him was complete and working: the Union Christian Church, erected in 1840 as a town meeting house and formally dedicated as a Congregational church in 1842, where he attended services; the stone schoolhouse from which he graduated from the eighth grade in 1885, replaced in 1890 by the present wooden one-room school built partly on the first building's stones; the stone farmhouse of Azro Johnson, about 1840, its walls laid in the snecked stonework typical of this part of Vermont; the Brown farm, whose mistress had attended Calvin's birth; and, from 1890, the Coolidge Cheese Factory, built by Colonel Coolidge, James S. Brown, and two other local farmers so their farms would have a convenient market for a growing milk supply.

Coolidge's own road out of the Notch is the one the National Register nomination traces: local grammar school, Black River Academy (1887–90), Amherst College, from which he graduated cum laude in 1895, then law study and a practice opened at Northampton, Massachusetts, in 1897. Following his father's example in local office, he rose steadily — Northampton city council in 1898, mayor in 1910–11, state senator, lieutenant governor in 1915, governor of Massachusetts in 1918 — and came to national attention in the Boston police strike of 1919. The Republican convention of 1920 put him on the ticket with Warren G. Harding, and the ticket won.

The village's second great moment came in the summer of 1923, when Vice President Coolidge was vacationing with his family at the Homestead. Word of President Harding's death reached Plymouth Notch in the very late hours of August 2. A few hours later, at 2:47 a.m. on August 3, 1923, Colonel John Coolidge — a notary public — administered the presidential oath of office to his son in the family home, by the light of a kerosene lamp. Years later, asked how he knew he could administer the oath, the elder Coolidge replied: "I didn't know that I couldn't." Coolidge was elected president in his own right in 1924, and that summer the hall above his father's store served as the President's office — the village's Summer White House. Tourists followed: cabins were constructed in 1927 for the first of the many visitors making the pilgrimage to the Notch. Coolidge left the White House in March 1929, returned to Northampton, wrote his autobiography, and died at his Northampton home on January 5, 1933. He was brought home to Plymouth Notch and buried in the hillside cemetery, where he rests among seven generations of his family.

Preservation began, in the State's own account, as a vision a month after the former president's death in 1933. The State of Vermont made its first purchase in 1947 — the Wilder House and Barn — and restored the Wilder Barn for an agricultural exhibit, the Farmer's Museum. In 1956, urged by the public and following the wishes of Grace Coolidge, John and Florence Coolidge donated the boyhood home and its contents to the State; the Homestead stands furnished as it was on the night of the oath. Over the following forty years the State obtained the major parcels and buildings of the village. The Calvin Coolidge Presidential Foundation, formed in 1960 by John Coolidge and fellow Coolidge enthusiasts and headquartered at Plymouth Notch, owns and maintains the Union Christian Church, gifted to it by the congregation in 1970. A Museum & Education Center was added in 1972 and enlarged in 2010. The district was designated a National Historic Landmark on June 23, 1965, and entered in the National Register on October 15, 1966.

## 3. Then / now

Then, Plymouth Notch was a working farm village, not a shrine: a general store with a post office and a dance hall upstairs, a cheese factory taking in the neighborhood's milk, a one-room school, a meeting-house church, and farmhouses whose families were bound together by work, marriage, and debt — the world that raised a president because it raised everybody that way.

Now, the same village is a state historic site, and the honest thing to say is how little of its fabric the change required. The National Register nomination, written in 1975, already judged that with the exception of the birthplace — modernized, and restored to its original appearance in 1971, with much of its original furniture returned by the family — none of the major buildings had been substantially altered, and the village preserved the atmosphere of nineteenth-century rural Vermont. The one deliberate modern intrusion, the Museum & Education Center of the early 1970s, was built of stone from a local surface quarry, roofed in Vermont slate, and set below the village core, screened by mature shrubs. The village still works in places: the cheese factory, closed in the 1930s, was renovated and reopened in the early 1970s producing cheese according to the original formula, and the church still holds services in season. What changed is the population: the farmers are gone, the houses are exhibits and a few private homes, and the traffic on the village roads is visitors'.

## 4. What's still there

The nomination counts some fifteen buildings along the two roads, all but three of early-to-late nineteenth-century date; the three modern ones are the Museum & Education Center and two farm residences. The district covers approximately 130 acres of the bowl valley, East Mountain on the east, Soltudus Mountain and Mount Tom on the south and southwest, Wood Peak on the north.

At the center stands the Coolidge Store and Birthplace: the pre-1835 store, two stories of frame and clapboard with a one-story storage ell, its upper hall — Coolidge Hall — the room that hosted the village's meetings and dances and served as the President's office in the summer of 1924; and at its rear the birthplace cottage of about 1840, five rooms, restored in 1971 and furnished with many pieces the Coolidge family used during their eight years in it. Across the street, the Coolidge Homestead keeps its 1876 form — the porch and two-story bay Colonel Coolidge added, the shed dormers, the woodshed and barn attached at the eastern end — and its interior is arranged essentially as it stood on August 3, 1923.

The supporting village is intact around them. The cheese factory of 1890 is a two-and-a-half-story frame building, two bays by seven; its original equipment is displayed in the Farmer's Museum, the great Wilder barn — hand-hewn beams assembled in a one-day raising — which also holds an exhibition of nineteenth-century farm tools collected in the Plymouth area. The schoolhouse of 1890 stands on a foundation of stones from the 1885 stone schoolhouse it replaced. The Union Christian Church of 1840 keeps its Greek Revival exterior and two-stage tower; its carpenter-gothic interior dates from an 1890 renovation. The Aldrich House, a Cape-form cottage, is the nomination's example of rural "continuous architecture," five barns and sheds attached in series at its rear; it was the home of Carrie A. Brown, Plymouth's postmistress for many years, who became Colonel Coolidge's second wife in 1891, and afterward of the man who came to operate the cheese factory. The Old Coolidge Farmhouse of about 1800, the Wilder House of about 1830, the stone Azro Johnson House of about 1840, and the Brown House of 1868 complete the family's village. Above it all, the Plymouth Notch Cemetery climbs its steep hillside: Coolidge's grave among seven generations of Coolidges, at the end, as the State's page puts it, of every journey to the Notch.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Come up Route 100A and start where the State starts you: the Museum & Education Center, set below the village core, for the exhibits that carry Coolidge from the Notch to the White House — and, in the 2026 season, for the Homestead's principal furnishings, moved here while the house is under preservation work. Then walk up into the village and take the green as its center, because it was.

Take the store building first: the Florence Cilley General Store at street level, still the village's store and post office under one roof, and Coolidge Hall above it — dances and town meetings in the father's day, the President's own office in the summer of 1924. Step behind it to the birthplace cottage and read it at its true scale: five small rooms behind a country store, where a storekeeper's son was born on the Fourth of July, 1872. Cross the street to the Coolidge Homestead and stand in the room of the oath — in a normal season the house is open, furnished to the night of August 3, 1923; in 2026, read the house from its exterior and its transplanted furnishings in the museum, and let the porch and bay window carry it.

Then work the village that surrounded the family: the cheese factory for the farm economy, the Farmer's Museum in the Wilder barn for its tools, the schoolhouse for the one-room education that ended, for Coolidge, with the eighth grade in 1885, and the Union Christian Church for the meeting-house the family attended. Finish the way the State's page finishes every account of the place: climb the steep hillside cemetery and find the President's grave among the seven generations of Coolidges.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The room of the oath is named two ways by the primary authorities. The National Register nomination states twice — in its building description and its significance narrative — that Colonel Coolidge administered the oath in the family dining room of the Homestead, at 2:47 a.m. on August 3, 1923. The State's site page and the Calvin Coolidge Presidential Foundation both place the oath in the parlor of the family home. The time, the date, the officiant, and the kerosene lamp agree everywhere; the room's name does not, and the discrepancy is printed unresolved. Section 2 follows the Foundation's wording ("in the family home") and gives the nomination's "dining room" here.

The nomination's own building description carries one evident slip: it dates the oath in the Homestead entry to "August 3, 1924." Its significance section, the State, and the Foundation all give August 3, 1923, and 1923 governs throughout this dossier; the 1924 line is recorded here so the slip is not silently absorbed.

The size of the ground is stated at two scopes. The nomination fixes the landmark district at approximately 130 acres. The Foundation describes the present historic site as comprising over 200 acres. These are different measures — the 1966 district against the site as since assembled — and both are printed with their scopes in §4 and here; no single acreage is asserted for both.

Building counts likewise vary by source and date. The nomination counts fifteen buildings, three of modern date. The Foundation describes the site as 12 or 13 buildings. No single present-day count is asserted here; the buildings are named individually, where the record names them.

The nomination's geographical data gives the district by UTM references in Zone 18, but the digits are not legible in the digitized copy consulted, and no other primary record fetched carries a point scoped to this property. No coordinates are therefore printed in §10; the points circulating on aggregator and map sites were not used.

One operating detail is dated by its source: the nomination records that the cheese factory, renovated in the early 1970s, "now produces cheese according to the original formula," and the State's current pages list the factory among the site's open buildings. This dossier asserts the reopening and the formula, not a current production calendar.

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** Dsdugan's 2015 photograph of the Florence Cilley General Store and Coolidge Hall at the village green, Plymouth Notch. The frame takes the village's heart rather than any single monument: the white clapboard store building fronting the street at the green, its post office sign at the front and the porch running along the face, the hall in the upper story above the store — the room of the village's dances and, in 1924, the President's office — with a barn standing in the frame at the left. It is the store Colonel John Coolidge ran, with the birthplace cottage behind it out of view. It is not the Coolidge Homestead: the boyhood home and oath house stands across the street and does not appear in this frame, and nothing in the picture should be read as it.
