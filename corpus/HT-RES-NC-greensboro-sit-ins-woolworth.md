## 2. Story

On February 1, 1960, North Carolina A&T freshmen Ezell Blair Jr., later Jibreel Khazan, Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil and David Richmond asked to be served at the downtown Woolworth lunch counter. The store sold merchandise to Black customers while refusing them seated service at the counter. The students stayed after being refused. The next day, the Greensboro Record reported that the four had come back and that the demonstration was drawing more students. The paper’s coverage records how quickly a local action became news. It also gives two names inaccurately. A newspaper records what a reporter could establish that day. The later interview records what two participants remembered about organizing. [2–5]

In an October 20, 1979 interview with Eugene Pfaff, Khazan recalled, “we just wanted to eat.” He also remembered hoping that the action would spread. McCain described how the group extended the protest to the nearby Kress when the Woolworth counter filled. In his account that move responded to growing participation and to a broader question about how other downtown businesses served Black customers. Khazan’s estimates of the third-day crowd are recollections, not turnstile counts. The practical detail is the part worth holding onto: as others joined, the organizers had to decide where they could sit next and which businesses should hear the demand. [4]

The larger action involved many students, churches and local organizations. The Smithsonian chronology records that Black patrons received service at Greensboro’s Woolworth on July 25, 1960. Read the months between the first request and the first service as the work of a community, including people the first newspaper account did not name. [3–6]

## 3. Then and now

The museum occupies the former Woolworth building. Its operator identifies the restored lunch counter in its historic location. The Smithsonian also holds a documented four-seat section of the original counter in Washington, D.C. These describe two portions of the same original fixture after the store closed. Treat the Greensboro museum as the public interpretation for the historic room. The surviving material does not establish which present stool or section each person occupied on February 1. [1–2, 6–7]

## 5. Strongest visit moment

Before the counter comes into view, read a short passage from the 1979 interview. In the gallery, **look at the distance between the sales floor and a seat at the counter**. The physical separation was small. The status assigned to customers on either side of it was the point of the action. Let the museum staff lead the visit. [2, 4]

## 6. Evidence limits and disagreements

The February 2 Greensboro Record transcription prints McLain and McNeill where the museum, the civil-rights archive and the oral history establish Franklin McCain and Joseph McNeil. Keep the newspaper’s date and contemporaneous perspective, and use the independently supported names. The transcript was inspected; the original newspaper page image was not independently recovered here. The 1979 interview is edited testimony recorded nineteen years later. Its crowd estimates remain attributed. Guilford County minutes report that museum tour coordinator Dillon Tyler said more than 85,000 people visit annually in March 2022. That is an attributed estimate, with no measurement period supplied in the cited statement and no comparable later door count verified. [3–5, 8]
