# Witch of Yazoo Grave, Glenwood Cemetery, Yazoo City — 1904 Yazoo City fire and Willie Morris's chained-grave legend

**Research draft · Labeled THEME SWEEP (Family 4 — Weird spots, with separate lore layer) · Yazoo City, Yazoo County, Mississippi · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate

The lawful public anchor is **Glenwood Cemetery**, an active historic municipal cemetery on the eastern bluff above Yazoo City, MS (see §6/§7 regarding cemetery hilltop location vs. secondary `1143 E. Broadway St.` directory listings). [1, 2]

**Verified operator & access rules:**
- **Daylight public hours:** Active historic cemetery open **daylight hours only** (posted signage enforces **"no entry dark 'til dawn"**; municipal/visitor directories cite `6:00 AM–7:00 PM` or `7:00 AM–7:00 PM` daily). Nighttime entry is prohibited and treated as trespassing. [1, 2]
- **Guided tours:** Group or individual guided cemetery tours (with costumed interpretation covering Confederate burials, Willie Morris's grave, and the chained plot) are available **by advance reservation** through the **Yazoo County Convention & Visitors Bureau / Visit Yazoo** (`(662) 746-1815` / `(800) 381-0662`). [1]

**Anchor ruling: ANCHORED (Glenwood Cemetery public grounds during daylight hours + advance-booked Visit Yazoo guided tours; strictly closed after dark).**

## 2. The story

**Glenwood Cemetery** dates back at least to **1856**, when **Captain John Willis** and his wife Annie donated bluff-top acreage overlooking Yazoo City; a mass-plot marker near the creek also commemorates Confederate soldiers who died in the Civil War hospital on South Main Street. Later, author **Willie Morris** (*North Toward Home*, *Good Old Boy*) was buried in Glenwood—thirteen steps south of the cemetery's most famous folkloric plot. [1]

The documented historical catastrophe behind the legend is the **Great Fire of Yazoo City on May 25, 1904**. Contemporary press coverage in the New Orleans *Times-Democrat* and *Jackson Daily-News* (May 27, 1904) documented a sweeping midday conflagration that destroyed **324 buildings**—more than 200 residences and virtually the entire downtown commercial district—causing over **$1.5 million to $2 million** in losses. Contemporary newspaper accounts reported that the fire originated at the residence of the **Wise family** on the wedding morning of Pauline Wise, spreading swiftly across dry frame structures and shingled roofs (regional weather summaries record no extraordinary storm winds that morning, contrary to later retellings). [3]

### Haunted history / lore

The **"Witch of Yazoo"** tradition became nationally known through native son **Willie Morris's** 1971 memoir/novel ***Good Old Boy: A Delta Boyhood*** (and was already part of mid-century Yazoo City oral lore). In the popular legend, an unnamed elderly eccentric—or "witch"—living in the hills/swamp outside town lured fishermen, was pursued into quicksand by a sheriff's posse around **1884**, and swore with her dying breath to break out of her grave and **burn Yazoo City to the ground in twenty years** (`1904`). [1, 4]

According to the tale, townspeople buried her in Glenwood Cemetery near the fountain inside a heavy **iron-link chain fence** (today a broken/split upright stone marked **`T. W.`** inside cast-iron posts and chains), only to find the iron chains snapped on the morning of **May 26, 1904**, the day after fire consumed the town. No nineteenth-century sheriff's docket, coroner's inquest, or 1884 newspaper record corroborates the existence, death, or burial of the "witch," and contemporary 1904 press accounts attributed the fire strictly to residential accidental origin. The story is carried here strictly as documented regional and literary folklore attached to a real 1904 urban fire. [1, 3, 4]

## 4. What's still there

- **Public (daylight hours):** Full **Glenwood Cemetery** hillside grounds—historic headstones, Victorian funerary monuments, the central fountain area, **Willie Morris's grave**, the Civil War plot near the creek, and the **chain-linked plot** (`T. W.` / split monument) associated with the Witch of Yazoo legend. [1, 5]
- **Restricted / prohibited:** **All nighttime access** ("no entry dark 'til dawn"). [1, 2]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Plan a daytime visit only** (well before dusk; or call Visit Yazoo `(662) 746-1815` ahead to arrange a guided cemetery tour). [1]
2. **Enter Glenwood Cemetery quietly** as an active burial ground, respecting all grave plots and posted signage. [1]
3. **Orient first at the historic markers and fountain area:** note the 1856 Willis donation marker and Civil War plot, separating documented cemetery history from the 1971 *Good Old Boy* narrative. [1]
4. **Read the chained plot (`T. W.`) and Willie Morris's nearby monument** side by side with the documented May 25, 1904 Yazoo City fire record before exiting prior to sunset. [1, 3]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Address discrepancy (`E. 15th St. / Grand Ave.` bluff vs. `1143 E. Broadway St.`):** Many online travel directories copy **`1143 E. Broadway St.`** as Glenwood Cemetery's street address, which geocodes roughly **1 km south** along East Broadway (`32.841505, -90.395917`). Both **OpenStreetMap** and **ArcGIS POI** place **Glenwood Cemetery** on the northern hilltop (`32.849311, -90.398719` / `32.850053, -90.397777`, Δ ~121 m), matching the cleared bluff cemetery footprint on the 1988 USGS `Yazoo City` quadrangle. Visitors should navigate to **Glenwood Cemetery** itself rather than street-number interpolation on E. Broadway. [6, 7]
- **Origin of the `T. W.` stone & chains:** Neither Visit Yazoo nor primary cemetery plat records inspected this run identify the real nineteenth-century interment behind the `T. W.` initials or when the decorative chain links were installed or broken. [1]
- **Opening/closing hour variations:** Sources cite `6:00 AM–7:00 PM` or `7:00 AM–7:00 PM` alongside strict "dark 'til dawn" dusk rules; daylight-only travel is the safe standard. [1, 2]
