# LaPointe-Krebs House and the date carried by its timber

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP23 · Labeled research draft · Quiet reflection.*

## 2. Story
The surviving house is the product of work in stages. The foundation describes a wall system made by pouring tabby, a lime-based material with shell, into forms. Short timbers embedded in the material gave carpenters points to fix openings and protected vulnerable corners. The house also contains materials from later building and restoration. Read the oldest fabric as evidence of people building with available material and changing what worked. The cited sources do not identify the individuals who made the early walls. [2, 3]

Records associate the larger plantation with Joseph Simon de la Pointe, then with Hugo Krebs and Marie de la Pointe. The archaeological report discusses a circa-1726 drawing by Dumont de Montigny that depicts the plantation and its work buildings, including quarters for enslaved people. The drawing locates that earlier plantation only approximately. It does not identify the surviving 1750s house among its buildings. Names and firsthand voices of enslaved people belonging to this particular household were not recovered in this pass. [3]

In 2018, Grant Harley, Justin Maxwell and colleagues published an analysis of timber from the building. Their free research abstract records the result: “three timbers from the Center Room have cut dates of 1757”. Two east-room samples dated to 1762, and three bark-bearing roof samples to 1772. The researchers suggested the last group could represent repairs after the 1772 hurricane. Cutting dates identify the trees’ outer rings; they offer stronger evidence for a construction sequence than a claim that every standing feature was finished in a single year. [4]

## 3. Then and now
HABS documented the house before the modern restorations. Its 1936 exterior shows a continuous low roof and added structures at the ends, and its interior records a working fireplace wall. The state archaeological report documents restoration work in the 1990s, Hurricane Katrina flood damage in 2005 and additional investigations in 2010. The operator now receives visitors at the museum and offers interpretation of the house. [1–3, 5]

The survey calls the property “Old French Fort” and the foundation records “Old Spanish Fort” as a former name. The house narrative rests on domestic and plantation evidence. These historical labels alone establish neither a military use for the building nor an invitation to tour fortification or shoreline ground. The HABS archive’s 4206 Fort Street is a historical address. The foundation uses 4602 Fort Street for the visit. [1–3, 5]

## 5. Public progression
Enter through the staffed museum. Learn the distinction between the earlier concession and the timber sequence of the standing house. If staff have opened the house, use the direct permitted approach and look at the visible change in wall structure and openings. End at **The Three Center-Room Timbers**. Let the 1757 evidence date that first phase, then consider what the 1762 and 1772 tree rings say about the people who enlarged or repaired it. Read the same sequence in the museum if the house is closed. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The older field report cites archaeological evidence favoring construction in the 1770s. The newer timber paper identifies three 1757 cutting dates in the center and separates the 1762 east addition and possible 1772 repairs. The original field disagreement remains important and the later absolute dates resolve a narrower question. The west room lacks a cutting date in the published sample. [3, 4]
- The operator and state identify the oldest surviving house in Mississippi. The operator’s broader Mississippi Valley superlative is not adopted here because the comparison set has not been independently audited. The freely readable scholarly abstract carries the reported sample dates. The full paper was not read. [1, 4, 6]
- Primary HABS presentation and item lookups returned HTTP 403 or 429. The 2020 state archaeological report URL exceeded the web reader’s size limit (HTTP 400, 19,402,232 bytes). Its alternate state PDF was downloaded, read and inspected. Individual early builders’ and residents’ records remain open. [3, 5, 7]
