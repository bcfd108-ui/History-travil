## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A forest story is also a story about authority: who was displaced, whose knowledge guides restoration, and who speaks to visitors now?

**Named Tribe-published testimony:** On the Yurok Tribe’s own ‘O Rew page, Joseph L. James, identified there as Chairman, says: “We created a new model of conservation that centers Indigenous land stewardship.” This is a public leadership statement reproduced by the Tribe, not an independently measured restoration outcome. The same page describes ecological harm alongside violence and displacement, and says the Tribe shaped restoration and visitor interpretation around Yurok knowledge and cultural priorities. [3]

The key distinction is between a landscape being protected by an outside institution and a Tribe exercising leadership over how its homeland is cared for and represented. That distinction is this dossier’s interpretive framing. It does not establish a legal conclusion about every redwood parcel, nor does it make every place mentioned by a public source a visitor destination.

**Another named voice with an explicit media boundary:** The Yurok Tribe’s own 14 April 2022 video description identifies Dr. Kishan Lara-Cooper discussing the significance of the Tribe’s agreement to operate the Chah-pekw O’ Ket’-toh Visitor Center. The description attributes this statement to James: “a significant step toward the healing of our people.” Playback was not reviewed, so no spoken wording or timestamp is assigned to Lara-Cooper. The quote above is from the published description, not a transcription made for this dossier. [4]

**Stewardship is not merely scenery.** The Tribal Cultural Resources Department describes responsibilities for archives, collections, repatriation, cultural-resource protection and interpretation. [5] The visit should foreground the community’s chosen public account, without asking a museum display to disclose restricted knowledge or treating material culture as a souvenir hunt.

## 3. Then / now
Then: the Tribe’s ‘O Rew account connects disruption of Yurok life with changes to the redwood landscape. Now: it describes Tribal-led restoration and public interpretation. These are related histories, not an argument that ecological restoration alone repairs every historical harm. [3]

Keep three institutions distinct:
- **Yurok Tribe:** a sovereign government and publisher of its own history and stewardship position.
- **Yurok Country Visitor Center:** the specifically advertised public institution chosen for this draft.
- **California State Parks and Redwood National and State Parks:** distinct public land-management institutions. Their names do not confer access to Tribal cultural places.

The Tribe’s Stone Lagoon page describes a government-to-government joint powers partnership beginning 1 June 2020; the 2022 video concerns a later public account of operating a visitor center. Those dates should not be collapsed into one event. Neither source makes the Klamath center the same institution as Stone Lagoon. [4,6]
