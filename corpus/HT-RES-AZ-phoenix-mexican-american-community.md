## 2. The story

**Named firsthand family voice: Diana Hinojosa DeLugan**, quoted in Emma Greguska's ASU-published *A tale of two families*, September 29, 2017. She recalls childhood in Phoenix's Las Milpas community with her father, musician **Fidencio Garcia Hinojosa**, visiting musical families and household citizenship study. Her own recollections are direct family testimony within a journalist's article, not a complete oral-history transcript. The embedded video was not watched. [5]

Her concise statement, **“His life was straddling both borders”**, frames the account without requiring a visitor to choose between cultural identity and civic belonging. [5]

**Original interpretive voice:** A city's history is not only the story of officials, buildings and streets. It is also what a family decides not to throw away. A songbook can hold learning as well as music. An ordinary paper can record an extraordinary effort to belong. When those materials enter an archive, the household becomes visible in public history without surrendering its right to privacy.

This is one family's account, not a universal Mexican American experience. It should open questions about memory, work, community and citizenship rather than supply a single model of identity. No migration route, private correspondence text or present personal information is reproduced.

## 3. Then / now

The 2017 article also includes **Laura Franco French**, discussing preservation of her family's Phoenix community records. She explicitly did not meet her grandparents; stories about them are inherited family memory, not her eyewitness evidence. [5]

ASU's archival catalog independently describes **Franco and French Families Papers**, connecting family history with the Spanish-language newspaper *El Sol* and Fiestas Patrias. The collection description is evidence of holdings, not proof that every item has been digitized or is available for immediate viewing. [6]

Then: households and community institutions created records for their own purposes. Now: selected records can support public research. That transition is the interpretive focus. It does not make a family's old home a museum or convert past celebrations into a currently scheduled event.
