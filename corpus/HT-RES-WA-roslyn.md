## 2. The story

Coal connected Roslyn to the Northern Pacific Railroad, but the community was more than its output. The city's own account describes coalfields supplying railroad construction and operation, workers arriving from several countries, and the company's recruitment of Black miners and families during the **1888 strike**. [3] A useful visit asks who controlled employment, who carried the risks, and how families made a life amid those pressures. It should not reduce Black residents to the circumstances of recruitment or describe ethnic coexistence as proof that racism disappeared.

**Named primary community voice:** In *Portland Observer*, **October 11, 1979, page 6**, **Norma Jean Darden's** *Pioneering Washington State*, part of a Kraft-sponsored cooking/history series, quotes **Ethel Craven** remembering community gatherings: **“People would get together and tell stories of their youth.”** The article also records household labor around miners' meals and bathing, and names **Beulah Craven Hart** discussing lunch pails. These are retrospective recollections mediated by a reporter, not contemporaneous company rules or a complete labor history. [4]

The strongest story is not a technical mining demonstration. It is the work that continued after a miner came home: feeding, cleaning, caring and sustaining relationships. The newspaper's nostalgic tone should not erase the burdens of that work or the tensions it also describes.

**Independent oral-history lead:** CWU holds **Ethel Craven Interview**, dated **October 28, 1981**, interviewer **Dorothy Hamilton**, item **23**. Its catalog describes mining, strikes, domestic labor, religion and family life, and identifies her as a coal miner's wife and a community caregiver. The index download returned 403; the audio player loaded but was not played. No words from that recording are quoted or represented as listened to. [5]

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a company-linked coal town brought workers and families into close proximity, with employment, strikes and household survival intertwined. [3–4]

**Now:** the museum interprets a living town rather than a deserted industrial exhibit. Its own introduction foregrounds the many nationalities represented in residents' family histories. [6] Present-day people, shops and homes are not props or automatic public exhibits. The continuity to notice is community memory, not an assumption that every facade or artifact remains in its original position.

A Washington State Historical Society event listing documents a museum coal-miner display for **June 14–September 30, 2026**, featuring a lunch bucket and hard hat with light. That is useful evidence of interpretive emphasis, **not proof that the display remains installed in November**. [7]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Proposed sequence, conditional on the gate above; not an activated itinerary.**

1. Begin with the museum if confirmed open. Otherwise keep the stop to lawful public-view orientation and the linked archive, without searching for another way inside.
2. Read an installed source about work and company life. Separate an object with provenance from a reconstruction or later reminiscence. Do not operate or climb machinery.
3. Shift deliberately from the underground workforce to household labor and community life. Ask whose contribution an exhibit can show and whose work is harder to collect in a museum.
4. End with Craven's remembered storytelling gatherings. The strongest moment is the change from seeing “a coal town” to hearing people remembering their own young lives. This is an interpretive proposal, not a claim that her interview is playing in the museum. [4]

Do not append a cemetery or mine excursion just to make the stop longer. If the interior is closed or outdoor conditions unsuitable, the researched archive remains a valid introduction and the physical visit can wait.
