## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** The same school name can conceal a fundamental change: education imposed to erase identity versus education governed by the communities whose children it serves.

**A named former student's directly published account:** **Pablita Velarde of Santa Clara Pueblo** discussed her art education with **Sylvia Glidden Loomis on September 29, 1965** in an Archives of American Art interview. The transcript was retrieved and relevant passages read directly after the web reader failed. She remembered Dorothy Dunn's support at Santa Fe Indian School and the significance of being paid for murals when students had little money. Her phrase **“just to get a few dollars for a painting was really something”** anchors agency and work, not an institutional success claim. [2]

**A different student experience:** Heard Museum's public boarding-school exhibition attributes a recollection of monotonous food to **Geronima Cruz Montoya, Ohkay Owingeh**, identifying her attendance at Santa Fe Indian School as **1927–1935**. This is a curator-published first-person excerpt, not the full recording examined here. No second quotation is needed to convey the contrast. [3]

**Institutional voice under Tribal governance:** SFIS's own history describes forced removal, suppression of Native languages and exploitative student labor in the federal boarding-school era. It also describes Pueblo leadership's work to reclaim education and identifies the contemporary school as owned and operated by **New Mexico's 19 Pueblos**. [4]

**Original synthesis:** A meaningful story must hold deprivation, artistic agency and self-determination together. A former student's gratitude toward a teacher does not excuse coercive policy. Historical abuse does not make today's tribally governed institution the same project under a new coat of paint.

## 3. Then / now

The school's account distinguishes **1976 Tribal control of Albuquerque Indian School** from the subsequent return to Santa Fe and later changes in governance and land status. Do not collapse those stages into a single date on which everything changed. [4]

The current institution's public history is a source of its own account, not an invitation to observe its pupils. Likewise, the selected museum is a separate state cultural institution, not a spokesperson for all Pueblos or a proxy school campus.

The encounter compares purposes and authority, not building footprints. No then-and-now photo alignment is proposed.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Before any travel:** read the school's own historical distinction between federal assimilation and Tribal control. [4]
2. **Read the named adult account:** encounter Velarde's recollection as a person's account of work and encouragement, not a verdict on the entire system. [2]
3. **Hold another experience alongside it:** Montoya's museum-published recollection prevents artistic achievement from becoming the only story. [3]
4. **Optional independent museum:** use only the currently public exhibits actually encountered; no promise of the selected works or testimony. If the gate is unavailable, remain digital.
5. **Strongest landing:** return to who has authority to define education. End on contemporary self-determination without requiring a present-day student to perform or explain it for visitors.

This is a reflection sequence, not a route through former school grounds, a school-day simulation or an emotional endurance test.
