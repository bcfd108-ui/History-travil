The dedicated museum page is stronger topic-specific evidence, but that inconsistency remains unresolved. [1,2]

The Tiburon ferry operator directly publishes November–December 2026 service: no Monday–Tuesday ferries; no regular Wednesday–Friday service, though added trips may appear; weekend public outbound departures 10:00, 11:00, 13:00. The 15:00 outbound is campers-only, not a late museum departure. Published weekend returns are 10:20, 11:20, 13:20, 15:20, first-come, first-served. ## 2. The story
**Law Shee Low**, interviewed **October 20, 1988**, recalled signing after questioning without understanding that admission had been granted. Back in the barracks she cried, fearing a mistake; only later did someone tell her she could leave. AIISF labels her detention ten days in 1922. Her short published words capture the uncertainty: **“I was still worried that I would have to go back and wait longer.”** [7]

This is a directly read, institutionally published firsthand excerpt, not a complete interview or original recording reviewed here. The foundation credits the Judy Yung Oral History Collection and the work of Him Mark Lai, Genny Lim and Judy Yung, and cautions that selected recollections do not represent every detainee. [7]

The story lands on the difference between an official decision and a person's knowledge of it. That is this draft's interpretive framing, not a claim about every immigration case. A visitor does not need to reenact interrogation to understand that an institution can control both movement and information. Do not test visitors on hypothetical eligibility or ask them to perform a detained person's fear.

Keep the person ahead of the building. Architecture can make detention visible, but it cannot explain a life without testimony. The reflection ends with the uncertainty in a remembered moment, not with a triumphal claim that every arrival ended in welcome.
