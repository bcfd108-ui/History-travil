# HT-RES-NH-fort-at-no-4 — The Fortified Village at the Top of the River

Division NH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a meadow above the Connecticut River at Charlestown, New Hampshire, stands a square stockade of pointed logs enclosing a ring of joined houses, a great chamber, and a watchtower: the Fort at No. 4, the fortified village that in the 1740s sheltered the northernmost British settlement on the river. What stands there today is a reconstruction, built in 1960 near the original site — no timber of the 1740s fort survives above ground. What does survive is the record: a soldier's plan of the fort drawn in 1746, the commander's own account of the April 1747 siege, journals, and muster rolls. The reconstructed fort is now The Fort at No. 4, an independent nonprofit open-air museum, added to the New Hampshire State Register of Historic Places in July 2020.

## 1. Trip gate

The visitor address is 267 Springfield Road (Route 11), Charlestown, NH 03603; phone 603-826-5700. The standing fort a visitor enters is the 1960 reconstruction, built to recreate the structures that stood here in 1746 — not an original 18th-century building.

## 2. The story

Number 4 began as paper. In 1735 the Massachusetts General Court established a row of land grants — the museum counts 32 plantations — along the upper Connecticut River, and grant No. 4 lay where the Black River comes into the Connecticut, about sixty miles north of Deerfield, Massachusetts. In 1740 the first families arrived from Rutland, Lunenburg, and Groton, Massachusetts: Stephen Farnsworth and his brothers Samuel and David were the first settlers, joined by the Stevens, Hastings, Willard, Parker, and Johnson families. By 1743 there were ten families at No. 4; by the mid-1740s the Spafford family had built a gristmill and a sawmill. They were the northernmost settlement of the English colonies at the time; the closest settlement of any kind was Fort Dummer, some forty miles south by the museum's measure.

The ground was Western Abenaki homeland, and the settlement sat between two empires. Phineas Stevens, one of the early settlers, kept a trading post at No. 4, his account book recording his peacetime trade with the Indians. War ended the peace. When King George's War spread to America in 1744, the inhabitants — who had already voted in 1743 to build a fort — drew their houses together into a fortified village. The state's highway marker dates the work plainly: in 1744 the settlers at No. 4 built a great log fort enclosing many of the town's dwellings. Its form is documented by a period record, a plan drawn by a soldier, John Maynard, showing the fort as it stood in the summer of 1746: a square of pointed twelve-foot posts set five inches apart — close enough to shoot through, too close to pass — the houses inside connected by lean-tos, a "Great Chamber" with an attached wooden tower serving as a lookout for miles up and down the river, and in time a cannon in the Parker House to sound the alarm.

Jurisdiction worked against the garrison. A royal boundary settlement in 1741 had run the Massachusetts line fifty miles south of the fort, leaving No. 4 in New Hampshire, whose government the settlers petitioned in 1743 and 1744 — John Spafford and Josiah Willard writing to Governor Benning Wentworth, in papers still at the State Archives. New Hampshire was slow; Massachusetts withdrew its soldiers, and in the autumn of 1746 the inhabitants abandoned the fort. It stood empty through the winter.

Massachusetts relented for one more season. Phineas Stevens — lieutenant under Josiah Willard, then captain for the defense of No. 4 — petitioned Governor Shirley in January 1747 to return with soldiers by April, arguing the fort's position as the first line of protection for the settlements below. Shirley granted it; Stevens reoccupied the fort in late March 1747 with some thirty provincial militia — and, by the accounts, several dogs. Eleven days later, on the morning of April 7, the dogs gave the alarm. Stevens' own account of the siege survives, retold by Parkman from it. A man who went out with the dogs — setting them on with a cry of "Choboy!" — was fired on twenty rods from the gate and escaped back, slightly wounded. Volleys beat against the wooden wall. The attackers fired a log house and fences on the windward side to burn the fort down on the wind; Stevens answered by digging eleven trenches under the wall on the fire side, six or seven feet deep, where men stood sheltered from bullets and dashed buckets of water, passed from within, against the scorching timbers. The wall was kept wet through the day. The attackers loaded a wagon with dry faggots behind a shield of planks, meaning to push the blazing machine against the wall — but called instead for a cessation of arms until sunrise. On April 8 the French commander came with a flag of truce, planted at a musket-shot from the fort, and demanded surrender; Stevens refused, backed by a council of his men. Firing and fire arrows ran on and were quenched. On April 9 two Native emissaries offered to end the siege in exchange for provisions; Stevens refused the food but counter-offered hostages, to be exchanged later for British prisoners. That was refused; a desultory fire followed, and the force withdrew north. The fort was never besieged again — the state marker's summary is exact: it was never again attacked.

The defense made Stevens' name in New England. Commodore Sir Charles Knowles, in Boston when the news arrived, sent him a silver-hilted sword in recognition. When the town was incorporated under its New Hampshire charter in 1753, the settlers remembered the gift and named it Charlestown, in Knowles' honor. Stevens served repeatedly as an emissary to Canada to redeem captives — his journals of 1749 and 1752 survive — and in 1752 he brought back, among others, a 24-year-old captive named John Stark. By 1754 the town had 180 inhabitants. Stevens died in 1756, on a campaign in Nova Scotia.

The French and Indian War made No. 4 a thoroughfare. In August 1754 Abenaki raiders took the Johnson family from Charlestown on the forced march to Canada that Susanna Johnson later published as *A Narrative of the Captivity of Mrs. Johnson*. Through the late 1750s the fort was garrisoned and enlarged as ranger and militia companies staged through it toward the Lake Champlain theatre; soldiers carved powder horns at No. 4 that survive as documents of the place — Samuel Lounsbury's, dated June 20, 1757, carries its double name, "Charlestown/alas No. 4." In October 1759 Major Robert Rogers' Rangers, starving after the raid on the Abenaki village of St. Francis, made their way back to the safety of No. 4. In 1760 the Crown Point Road — seventy-seven miles, cut by John Stark and other rangers — was completed from Charlestown to Chimney Point on Lake Champlain, fixing the fort at the head of the military road west. After the peace of 1763 the frontier moved on, but in August 1777 General John Stark mustered some 1,500 New Hampshire militiamen at Charlestown and the fort before marching to the victory at Bennington. After the Revolution the fort fell into disrepair and disappeared.

The survival is documentary, and in 1947 the documents produced a building. That year the museum organization was founded and John Maynard's 1746 plan was discovered; after years of planning and fundraising, the fort was reconstructed — the reconstruction opened in 1960, a short distance from the original site — as a living history museum. In July 2020 the property was added to the New Hampshire State Register of Historic Places.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the fort was not a fort in the garrison sense but a village that had pulled itself into a square: farmhouses dragged together, lean-tos filling the gaps, a stockade of pointed posts around them, a great chamber for worship and muster, a tower watching the river road. Its strategic fact was simple geography — the last English place up the river, and the door through which expeditions, captives, and armies passed for thirty years.

Now, the square stands again in a meadow on Springfield Road — but it is a 1960 reconstruction, and this entry treats it as one throughout. The genuine survivals are the records: Maynard's 1746 plan, Stevens' account of the siege and his journals of 1749 and 1752, Susanna Johnson's narrative, muster rolls, and carved powder horns. The original fort's ground lies in the village of Charlestown, where Highway Marker No. 2, on Route 12, marks the story — and where Marker No. 117, seventy-five feet north, records Stark's 1777 muster. The museum the visitor walks is the documentary fort rebuilt; the town to the south is where it actually stood.

## 4. What's still there

The reconstruction, as the museum presents it, recreates the fort of 1746: a square stockade of pointed twelve-foot posts; the settlers' houses brought inside the square and connected by lean-tos, with furnished period interiors; the Great Chamber — the two-story great hall — with its attached wooden watchtower, the lookout over the Connecticut River valley; the Parker House, where the fort's alarm cannon was placed; and the well and outbuildings shown on Maynard's plan. Costumed interpreters staff the buildings in season, and the museum's program runs demonstrations, reenactments, workshops, and lectures across the season's event weekends.

What is not there must be said in the same breath: no part of the standing fort is original. The original fortification was gone by the early republic, and its site in the village is marked only by the state highway markers. The documents are the survival; the building is their illustration.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Come on a Friday, Saturday, or Sunday in season — an event weekend if the calendar offers one — and arrive with at least an hour in hand; the last tickets are sold thirty minutes before closing.

Begin with the museum, where the story is laid out — the 1735 grant, the 1740 settlement, and Maynard's 1746 plan, the drawing the reconstruction was built from. Then walk the square as the plan reads it: the pointed stockade, posts five inches apart as the period description gives them; the ring of houses joined by lean-tos; the Parker House and its alarm cannon; and the Great Chamber, the fort's church, meeting room, and barracks in one hall. Take the watchtower's view if the day's staffing opens it: the lookout watched miles of the Connecticut River up and down.

Read the siege standing in the yard: here Stevens' men dug their eleven trenches under the wall and passed the buckets; here the parley flag was planted a musket-shot out; here, on the third morning, the garrison refused to trade provisions and the siege ended. The buildings around you are 1960 work — it is Stevens' account, not the logs, that is original here.

Finish in the village, at Highway Marker No. 2 on Route 12, which stands near the original fort and compresses the story into four lines — 1744, the 1747 siege, the 31-man garrison, never attacked again — and Marker No. 117 beside it, for Stark's muster of 1777. The reconstruction shows you the fort's shape; the markers stand on its ground.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The siege's numbers do not agree, and the disagreement is the record. English accounts of April 1747 claimed an attacking force of several hundred — "said to be 700 strong" is the figure that circulates, hedged even by its repeaters as "probably much less." The French-side record, as carried in the Dictionary of Canadian Biography's account of the expedition, puts the party at ten French troops of the marine and sixty Abenaki warriors — seventy men. Stevens' garrison is likewise counted three ways: the state's highway marker says the fort was beaten off "by the 31-man garrison"; the museum and most modern accounts give Stevens and thirty militia; one modern tabulation prints thirty-two. All three counts stand here unresolved; §2 prints the museum's form.

The French commander is named two ways. The English period accounts, and the retellings that follow them, name a "General Debeline." The Dictionary of Canadian Biography identifies the commander as Ensign Joseph Boucher de Niverville of the troupes de la marine. Both names are printed rather than reconciled.

Smaller dates move too. Stevens' arrival is fixed by the modern siege account at eleven days before April 7 — March 27, 1747 — while the Granite Monthly's retelling has him reach the fort "on the last day of March." The fort's building is dated 1744 by the state marker and most accounts, the museum agreeing the decision came in 1743; but the form the museum recreates is the fort of Maynard's plan, drawn in the summer of 1746, and one strand of the secondary record describes the houses as already joined in a stockaded square by 1743 — whether the 1744 work was a new fort or the completion of an arrangement already begun is not resolved. The distance to Fort Dummer is given as about forty miles by the museum and thirty-four by the modern siege account; both stand. Charlestown's naming is dated by the museum to the incorporation in 1753; secondary sources also print 1783, which is not asserted here.

The reconstruction's date is firmer in the secondary record than in the museum's own. The museum's history page says only that "in the 1960s the Fort was reconstructed," the restoration plan dating to the organization's founding and the Maynard map's discovery in 1947; the year 1960 is carried by the State Register coverage and the contemporary newspaper record (the Brattleboro Reformer, May 7, 1960), and §2 follows it on that basis. Where exactly the reconstruction stands against the original footprint is stated only generally in the sources fetched — "a short distance from the original site" — and no archaeological report on either site was located in this pass. Finally, the register status is stated carefully: the fort is listed on the New Hampshire State Register of Historic Places (July 2020); it is not in the National Register of Historic Places, and no National Register number exists for this entry.
