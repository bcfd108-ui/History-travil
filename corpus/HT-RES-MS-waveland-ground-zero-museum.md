# The Waveland school that became a place to remember Katrina

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP23 · Labeled research draft · Quiet reflection.*

## 2. Story
The Coleman Avenue building began as a school. State preservation researchers describe an H-shaped brick building with classrooms and an auditorium. Quoting a 1937 WPA survey, they report that the school was “equipped with modern seating, lighting and heating”. The amenities marked an investment in the new school. The same nomination identifies a limit on whom it served: the school was for white children, while Black children attended a separate one-room school on Waveland Avenue. The quote reaches us through the nomination’s transcription. The original WPA page was not independently retrieved. [2, 3]

Hurricane Camille damaged the school in 1969. The nomination traces a replacement school completed in 1972 and the old building’s reuse as a civic center. Classrooms gave way to meeting rooms and the auditorium to local performances. This was already a place of community recovery and reuse before Katrina flooded and badly damaged it in 2005. The city restored the building and it was rededicated in May 2009. [2, 3]

A hurricane report can describe the spread of the water. The National Hurricane Center identifies Waveland among the communities affected by the exceptionally high surge near St. Louis Bay. The report places the maximum observed 27.8-foot high-water mark at Pass Christian, a separate location. A preserved building can tell the story at a smaller scale: the nomination records damage to the Waveland school roof-level area and partial collapse of its southeast pavilion. [2, 4]

Lili Stahler-Murphy and Basil Kennedy opened the museum in 2013. The museum holds the Katrina Recovery Quilt series of Solveig Wells. A text from Wells’s website preserved in the exhibition account says, “Solveig collected and washed these Katrina-affected fabrics”. It describes cloth recovered from different parts of affected homes and shops and dates the making of the 55 quilts to January 2006 through April 2007. Their material brings people’s acts after the flood into a building they restored after the same storm. [1, 5]

## 3. Then and now
The former school’s low brick massing, recessed entrance and cast-stone school panel document the building’s former use. The present civic and museum use follows both storms and the later restoration. The nomination describes new windows made to match historical divisions. Some features are repaired building fabric rather than things that came through the storm intact. [1–3]

## 5. Public progression
On the way into the staffed museum, read the school name over the door. Consider the building’s earlier classroom use and the separate school that served Black pupils. In the museum, read the restoration and storm evidence as part of the same building’s history. End at **The Cloth That Came Back**, at the quilt display or the museum’s text when pieces are off view. Give time to what Wells made from surviving fabric without treating the disaster itself as an attraction. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The operator assigns a 1927 date. The state inventory estimates 1927–30. The nomination identifies the Coleman Avenue school as completed by 1931. Building, opening and the date later residents remember may differ. [1–3]
- The state nomination has an architectural section referring to the transition around 1972 and a historical section giving 1969 as the end of ordinary school operation after Camille. The separate 1972 completion of the replacement and subsequent civic use are recorded in that historical section. [2]
- The original 1937 WPA page and a freely accessible period school photograph were not recovered. The operator’s exhibit gives a firsthand project account as a reproduction, using variants “Sloveig” and “Solveig”. The draft uses “Solveig” as printed in the quoted exhibit text. An original contemporary photograph of the actual school with verified public-domain rights remains open. [2, 5]
