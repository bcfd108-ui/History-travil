## 2. Story
**Andalusia Farm, home of Flannery O’Connor · Research draft · Fred**

“The place is a dairy farm,” Flannery O'Connor wrote to poet Elizabeth Bishop on January 13, 1957. The Library of America has published the letter text. O'Connor was describing the place where she and her mother lived, and also describing the workers around them from a particular owner's household perspective. The letter notices a Polish displaced family at the farm, while withholding names from other households it classifies by race. That imbalance is a reason to remember the people the record does not identify. The letter is a voice of one resident, with its own limits. [3]

The farm had an older agricultural history. Georgia College records the purchase by O'Connor's uncle, physician Bernard Cline, during the twentieth century and identifies Regina Cline O'Connor in the successor household. After her lupus diagnosis, Flannery lived there from 1951 until her death in 1964. The register describes a house with later additions. The modern NPS account places the main house alongside tenant houses and agricultural structures. It identifies the surviving Hill House as former enslaved housing later used by tenants. This is a literary address and a labor landscape. The households and work spaces connected to the main house deserve the same historical attention as the books finished there. [2, 4–6]

## 3. Then and now
The 1979 nomination documents the place as a privately occupied residence. In 2003 the family transferred it to a private foundation for museum use. The university received it in 2017. That succession matters: current visitors enter a staffed interpretation program, and the nomination's historic description of rooms and use carries no current access authority. [1–2, 4]

## 4. Surviving features and access
- **Public interior while open** — Interpretive center and staff-authorized house tour.
- **Public exterior** — Signed approach and farm views within staff boundaries.
- **Sensitive history in context** — Former enslaved and tenant housing discussed from interpretation. No separate entry, scene recreation, photo prompt or routing onto sensitive ground. Keep away from water and staff or closed areas. [1–2, 5]

## 5. Public trip progression
Start at the interpretive center and ask which part of the public house tour is available. Follow the ordinary museum path to the house or read the farm account indoors if outside surfaces are unsuitable. End at **A sentence from a working farm**, with the letter's brief description and the public interpretation of work and households. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
- The earlier federal nomination gives 547 acres. The modern NPS site page gives 544. These are different property descriptions and no boundary survey was retrieved to resolve their difference. [4–5]
- The operator's 1980 listing and 2022 National Historic Landmark designation concern separate events. The deeper NHL nomination repository attempt returned a timeout. The 1980 nomination and public designation notice were retrieved. [2, 4, 7–8]
- The regular closure language and posted December special hours apply to different dates. Use the operator's dated announcement and avoid assuming an ordinary winter opening. The January 1957 letter was read in published transcription, and the manuscript and wage or tenant records were not retrieved. [1, 3]
