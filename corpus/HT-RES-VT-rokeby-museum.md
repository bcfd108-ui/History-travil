# HT-RES-VT-rokeby-museum — The Farm That Wrote It Down

Division VT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On the east side of US Route 7 in Ferrisburgh, set back on a gentle rise above the Champlain Valley, stands Rokeby: the farmstead of four generations of the Robinson family, Quakers from Rhode Island who bought the place in 1793 and kept it until 1961. Under Rowland Thomas Robinson and his wife Rachel Gilpin Robinson, Rokeby became a documented stop on the Underground Railroad — documented in the strictest sense, because the Robinsons saved their letters. Freedom seekers are named in that correspondence, their journeys described, their wages and work recorded, in one of the fullest abolitionist family archives in the country. The farm around the story was a working one — Merino sheep, then dairy — and it survives nearly whole: the furnished farmhouse, its farmyard, the sheep dip, fields and orchards going back to woods. Rokeby is a National Historic Landmark, designated in 1997 for this history.

## 1. Trip gate

Rokeby Museum is operated by Rokeby Museum at 4334 Route 7, Ferrisburgh, VT 05456, on the east side of US Route 7 about three miles north of Vergennes; its published telephone number is 802-877-3406. It is a ticketed historic site: a modern Education Center with the Museum's exhibitions, the historic Robinson home, nine historic buildings on the grounds, and hiking trails across the old farm.

The Museum's own visit page frames the season: in 2026 the Museum published hours of May 16 to October 11, open 10 a.m. to 5 p.m. daily, last admission at 4 p.m. The Historic Robinson Home is open for guided tours only — public tours Wednesday through Monday (none on Tuesday) at 11 a.m. and 1:30 p.m., lasting about an hour, at an added charge over admission; the grounds and outbuildings are self-guided with a tour sheet from the reception desk. Off-season, the Museum opens for programs, school visits, and by appointment. The hiking trails are open daylight hours year-round, with portions closed in mud season. Current hours, prices, and tour times are as the Museum publishes them at rokeby.org.

The visitor boundary is the Museum's own ground: the landmark boundary takes in the farmhouse, farmyard, fields, orchards, and forest of the Robinson farm. Route 7 frontage and the neighboring farms on either side are working countryside, not part of the site.

## 2. The story

The Robinsons came from Newport, Rhode Island. Thomas Robinson (1761–1851) left Newport in 1792 and in 1793 purchased the Ferrisburgh farm, including the small Cape the Dakin family had built in the late 1780s, now the back of the present farmhouse. He established saw, grist, and fulling mills on Lewis Creek a few miles away, and in 1810 bought some of the first Merino sheep imported from Spain — the purchase that set Rokeby toward becoming one of the region's largest sheep farms. His son Rowland Thomas Robinson (1796–1879), born at Rokeby, built the farm's large Federal-style front block in 1814, and in 1820 married Rachel Gilpin (1799–1862), whom he had met at Nine Partners, the Quaker boarding school in Dutchess County, New York. Rowland tended the sheep farm and the mills. His cause was abolition.

Rowland and Rachel Robinson were early, radical converts to Garrisonian abolitionism — devout Quakers who held slavery a sin to be opposed by every acceptable means, including aiding those who freed themselves. Rowland helped found the Vermont Anti-Slavery Society in 1834, the first state auxiliary of the American Anti-Slavery Society, and worked in antislavery societies from the local to the national level; Rachel kept the household free of slave-made goods — Garrison wrote in 1835 that not a particle of the productions of slave labor was used in her family. Rowland's convictions eventually cost him his standing among Friends: he resigned from the meeting in the 1840s after the Yearly Meeting disciplined and disowned other abolitionists for agitation. In 1843 he hosted one of the New England Anti-Slavery Society's hundred conventions at Ferrisburgh; Frederick Douglass, who spoke on that tour, recorded that there the way had been prepared by stalwart workers, Rowland T. Robinson among them.

What sets Rokeby apart is that the sheltering of freedom seekers here is not tradition but correspondence. The family kept its letters — more than ten thousand of them — and some three hundred of Rowland's letters from 1830 to 1865 survive, abolition their most common theme through 1850. His correspondents were the movement — Oliver Johnson of the American Anti-Slavery Society, Joseph H. Beale, Charles Marriott, Lucretia Mott, Garrison, Isaac T. Hopper. In those letters, the people sent to Rokeby have names.

In January 1837 Oliver Johnson wrote from Jenner Township, Pennsylvania, thirty miles from the Maryland line, about Simon, twenty-eight, who had escaped his Maryland enslaver with a $200 reward posted for him, and who could, Johnson attested, beat any man in his old neighborhood at mowing. Simon intended to go on to Canada but would rather stay in the United States if he could be safe; Johnson had no doubt he would be perfectly safe at Rokeby, and proposed sending him north with the names of abolitionists to call on along the way. That spring Rowland conducted, in his own name and openly, a negotiation no secrecy story would have allowed: Jesse, a man who had escaped his enslaver in Perquimans County, North Carolina, was working at Rokeby and had saved $150 from his wages toward buying his freedom — "the most anxious wish of his heart." Rowland wrote to the enslaver, Ephraim Elliott, on Jesse's behalf. Elliott replied on April 19, 1837, conceding that Jesse was for the present out of his reach but refusing to make title for less than $300; Rowland answered with Jesse's whole $150, refusing to add money of his own because paying for a man would recognize a principle God forbids; Elliott held his price in June. The letters do not record that Jesse ever got his paper; they record plainly that nobody, on either side, behaved as if Jesse could be seized in Vermont.

The pattern repeats across the correspondence. In 1842 Charles Marriott wrote of John and Martha Williams, a couple who had escaped and been sheltered since the fall, being sent further north — "either to you or to Canada," Marriott wrote, as if the two were near enough the same thing — because the Supreme Court's Prigg decision had unsettled the personal-liberty laws further south. In July 1844 Joseph Beale sent word of Jeremiah Snowden, "a poor hunted fugitive" who would be safer in Massachusetts or Vermont if work could be had, and shipped the man's clothes by tow boat to Vergennes. In 1851 James Temple, who had gone on to Canada, wrote back from Montreal at work at his trade, looking at the world through the glasses they had given him — spectacles that had belonged to Thomas Robinson's late wife — and hoping soon to send for his family. Rowland's youngest son, Rowland Evans Robinson, remembering his childhood in an 1896 letter to the historian Wilbur Siebert, recalled seeing four fugitives at a time in his father's house, and quite often one or two.

The work did not stop at shelter. In 1839 Rowland built a two-story brick school, the Brick Academy, north of the farmstead, and ran it until 1846; among its pupils were Black children, including the Turpin children, wards of a New York Friend — an algebra book signed by Henry Turpin is still in the collection. After the Civil War Rowland wrote to Washington offering shelter and jobs to freedmen, and in 1878 was still directing money to Black education, Garrison advising him on the colleges. Activity at Rokeby itself fell off after 1850, the year of the second Fugitive Slave Act; the Temple letter of 1851 is the last of the fugitive correspondence, and the reason is not documented — the nomination prints the fact and the open question together.

The farm went on. The sheep economy faded with Vermont's wool market, and Rowland's sons George Gilpin Robinson and Rowland Evans Robinson converted the farm from wool to dairy; the creamery turned out the family's noted "Rokeby butter." Rowland Evans Robinson (1833–1900) made the family's second reputation: a writer and illustrator who drew the farm he had hoped to escape, publishing the Danvis tales that made him one of Vermont's best-known authors, alongside, late in his career, several Underground Railroad stories of his own. His children kept the farm into the twentieth century — his daughter Rachel a commercial artist, his son Rowland Thomas, called Rowlie, farming it as a dairy — adding a tourist cabin in the 1930s for summer boarders. When Rowlie's wife, Elizabeth Donoway Robinson, died in 1961, the last of the family, she left the site by her direction to become a museum. It was entered in the National Register in 1974, and designated a National Historic Landmark on December 9, 1997.

## 3. Then / now

Then, Rokeby was a prosperous working farm with a cause: at its height one of Addison County's largest Merino sheep operations, its farmyard busy with shearing, dipping, and butter-making, its house a place where abolitionist mail arrived from across the North and where men and women who had escaped slavery came to work for wages and start free lives. The landscape was open — pasture and orchard, the big barns standing, a brick schoolhouse on the hill.

Now, the farmstead is a museum landscape, and the honest change is quiet. The house and farmyard stand as the family left them in 1961. But the barns are foundations — the sheep barn burned early in the twentieth century, the dairy barn was demolished in 1995 — and the farmland is closing in: fields and orchards returning to forest, crossed by trails, two hayfields still cut for a neighbor's cows. The work the place interprets is no longer done here; what survives is the evidence — buildings, tools, and paper.

## 4. What's still there

The farmhouse is three buildings in one skin, all clapboarded on stone foundations: the Dakins' story-and-a-half Cape of the late 1780s at the back, with its massive central chimney and original cooking hearth; the two-story Federal front block of 1814, five bays wide, its deep, gable-roofed porch on square posts — original, and the face of the house — sheltering a Federal "cross and Bible" door; and a one-bay, two-story extension of 1893 at the south end, the town clerk's office. Inside, the house is furnished with two hundred years of Robinson belongings — furniture, clothing, dishes, books, and art from the 1790s to 1961, including Rowland Thomas Robinson's desk and papers. The chambers where hired hands — and, in their season, freedom seekers — slept, and the kitchen where they ate, are unchanged.

Behind the house, sheltered by it, the farmyard keeps its nineteenth-century cluster: the smokehouse (ca. 1850), near-commercial in size, ash still in its chamber; the creamery and ice house (ca. 1850, with a 1940s refrigerated room added), where Rokeby butter was made; the privy (ca. 1850), a three-seater papered inside with wallpaper scraps; the toolshed and slaughterhouse (ca. 1850), with its hoist and drained concrete floor; the granary (ca. 1850), granary and corn crib in one, raised on stone piers against rodents; the hen house (ca. 1900); the surviving ell of the "other house" (ca. 1800), which lodged hired help and guests; and the tourist cabin (ca. 1930). South of the farmyard a path leads past the sheep barn foundation to the sheep dip (ca. 1810), a dam on a natural rock outcropping where sheep were washed before shearing — a rare survivor of Vermont's sheep-farming golden age. North stand the dairy barn foundation and, up the green trail in an abandoned orchard, the Brick Academy foundation (1839). The Museum's trails run over more than fifty acres of the old farm.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Begin at the Education Center, where admission and the galleries are. Take the exhibition first: the Museum's ongoing exhibition, *Seeking Freedom: The Underground Railroad and the Legacy of an Abolitionist Family*, which opened in these galleries in 2024 as the successor to *Free & Safe: The Underground Railroad in Vermont*, the 2013 exhibition built around Simon and Jesse. The seasonal exhibition in the Jane Williamson Gallery rotates; check what is up. The Museum's programming also carries the farm-labor story into the present: since its trustees enlarged its mission in 2019 to include social advocacy, its talks and published work have taken up the immigrant workers who now staff Addison County's dairy farms.

Then take the house on the guided tour — it is the only way in — and read it in the order it grew: the 1814 Federal front, the 1780s Cape behind it, the 1893 clerk's-office addition, and the rooms furnished as the family left them. Ask where the hired hands slept; the answer is the documentary one — the chambers over the kitchen, not a hiding place.

After the house, walk the farmyard on the self-guided sheet: smokehouse, creamery, privy, toolshed-slaughterhouse, granary, other house, tourist cabin — the whole apparatus of the sheep-and-dairy farm in one sheltered yard. Then take the path south to the sheep dip and the sheep barn foundation, and the green trail north to the Brick Academy foundation in the old orchard. Finish on the red and blue trails east through the returning woods, reading stone walls, a well, a pond, and a field-barn foundation as the farm's outer works.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The largest gap at Rokeby is between the story long told and the story the letters tell, and the Museum and the Landmark nomination both print it openly. From the late 1960s into the mid-1980s, visitors were told that fugitives had been hidden in the east chamber — the "Rokeby Slave Room." The nomination traces the claim: the partition that made that chamber was most likely built in the early twentieth century to make rooms for tourist boarders, and the hiding story entered the record through retelling, not documents. The letters point the other way — freedom seekers here lived and worked openly, were corresponded about by name, and were considered by everyone in the letters, including a North Carolina enslaver, out of reach in Vermont. The state's own Underground Railroad survey, cited in the nomination, found no substantiated incident of organized slave catching in Vermont. The hiding story is therefore not printed in §2 as fact; the chambers are described as what they were, the hired hands' sleeping rooms.

A second caution runs the same way. Rowland Evans Robinson's late Underground Railroad fiction — "Out of Bondage," "An Underground Railroad Passenger," "The Mole's Path" — put slave catchers and narrow escapes at Rokeby. Siebert cited the anecdotes as fact in his Vermont volume; the nomination states flatly that he cited no evidence for them, there is none in the collection, and Robinson told none of these stories in his own factual 1896 letter to Siebert. The fiction is literature, printed here as literature.

The size of the ground is stated two ways. The Landmark nomination fixes the nominated property at approximately 85 acres and bounds it by a verbal description; the Museum today describes a 90-acre site. Both figures are printed; no reconciliation is asserted. The size of the letter archive is likewise stated two ways by date: the 1997 nomination counts 10,000-plus family letters; Middlebury College, which now holds the Robinson Family Letters on extended loan from the Museum, counts 15,000 letters dated 1757 to 1962, filling 58 boxes.

Small discrepancies stand as found: the North Carolina enslaver's surname is spelled "Ephram Elliott" in the Landmark nomination's text and "Ephraim Elliott" in Middlebury's account; the exchange itself is not in doubt. The exhibition succession wants care: *Free & Safe* (2013) was the Education Center's founding exhibition, and the Museum's current pages name *Seeking Freedom* as its ongoing exhibition; the 2024 opening date for the newer show comes from the Museum's chamber listing, not its own pages, and is printed on that basis.

Finally, the nomination locates the property by verbal boundary and four UTM corner references in Zone 18 and prints no point coordinates; latitude and longitude in §10 are therefore blank.
