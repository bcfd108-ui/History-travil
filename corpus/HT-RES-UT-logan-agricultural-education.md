## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** agricultural education was not only about what happened in a field. Household work could become a subject of inquiry, and the visiting expert could discover that she was also a student.

USU's institutional exhibit publishes **Amy Lyman's** first-person account of meeting homemakers during extension work. Her description ranges across domestic tasks and concludes: **“if each had an open mind she learned from the other.”** [4] This is a short published primary-voice excerpt mediated through an exhibit and its cited history, not a newly inspected interview recording or original manuscript. No audio was heard.

Her account offers a more interesting story than one-way instruction from campus to countryside: practical knowledge could move both directions. That is this dossier's interpretive synthesis, not proof that every extension encounter was equitable or welcomed. It also does not license a visitor to seek out the households involved.

A second university exhibit reproduces a recollection attributed to **John A. Widtsoe's _In a Sunlit Land_**, in which the educator describes farmers bringing problems back to university specialists. Its heading misnames him “Joseph A. Widstoe,” while its collection citation identifies John A.; retain that editorial discrepancy rather than create another person. [5] The complete memoir was not examined here.

**Source conflict matters:** [4] dates Lyman's appointment July 29, 1913, but cites her account through _Our Living Legacy_ to _Student Life_, September 27, 1912. The earlier citation cannot silently become a verified account of a later appointment. The original newspaper and intermediate book must resolve the chronology before assigning an exact trip date to her words.

## 3. Then / now

The historical institutional story belongs to the Agricultural College of Utah and its successor, **Utah State University in Logan**, not Montana State University in Bozeman. A shared land-grant tradition does not make their personnel, collections or campuses interchangeable. USU's exhibit traces its foundation to 1888 and explains extension as taking education beyond enrolled students. [5]

The present library is a repository and interpretive setting. It is not claimed as Lyman's historical classroom or any visited household. The temporary atrium show supplies a contemporary material bridge to food history; no source examined establishes that it displays Lyman's own papers. [1]

An additional institutional exhibit documents extension-related photographic collections and the earlier combination of agricultural and household instruction. [6] Those records support an archival comparison, not a surviving-farm itinerary.
