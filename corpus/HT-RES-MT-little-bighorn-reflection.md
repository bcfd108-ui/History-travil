## 2. The story

The most useful question here is not who moved where. It is whose grief became public history, and whose words visitors were asked to hear. NPS acknowledges that early interpretation largely privileged the U.S. Army's perspective. Congress renamed the site and directed an Indian Memorial in **1991**; dedication followed in **2003**, with permanent panel wording completed in **2013**. [5]

**Enos Poor Bear, Sr., an Oglala Lakota elder**, supplies the central reflection in a statement reproduced by NPS: **“it must contain a message for the living.”** This is a published attributed statement, not an audio recording independently heard. NPS names Arikara, Apsaalooke (Crow), Arapaho, Cheyenne and Oyate (Lakota Sioux) in its memorial interpretation. Those names should remain distinct rather than be replaced by one invented Indigenous viewpoint. [5]

A separate source of authority is Little Big Horn College's **Joseph Medicine Crow Collection**. Its catalog explains that Medicine Crow listened to older Crow men and women, collected historical materials from 1932, was designated tribal historian in 1947, and donated the collection in 1987. Importantly, the finding aid distinguishes his own writing from material he collected. A document in his collection is therefore not automatically his statement or a Crow-authored account. [6]

These sources perform different work: an Oglala Lakota elder's public memorial message, an NPS interpretation of commemoration, and a Crow institution's description of its historian's archive. None authorizes us to narrate living Nations as interchangeable, vanished, or reconciled on our terms. The original narrative for this stop is a change in who is publicly heard, not a claim that a memorial resolves the consequences of violence.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** public commemoration was selective. **Now:** visitors can read a memorial account that explicitly confronts that selectivity. [5] This is an interpretation comparison, not a reenactment or invitation to reconstruct movements on the ground.

The 1927 portrait of **Wooden Leg** cataloged by NPS offers another kind of historical presence: a named person recorded decades after the conflict, not an anonymous figure in an illustrated battle. [7] It is a portrait, not a viewpoint match. No present-day camera alignment, surviving backdrop, or repeated pose is claimed.

The visitor-center rebuild is a present-day layer of interpretation. Its proposed future exhibits must not be described as something a visitor can inspect today. [1]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Short public-contact visit, conditional on the day-of gate:**

1. Before departure, read the attributed Enos Poor Bear statement and the college finding aid. Keep the two Nations and the two source types distinct.
2. If the currently open visitor-contact area is confirmed, begin and remain within that designated public area. Use available interpretation, not construction-zone access or a self-created circuit.
3. Compare the older single-perspective framing with the published account of the later memorial. An onward visit is not necessary to complete this reflection and is not routed here.
4. End with the strongest story moment: return to the elder's message and consider what a visitor owes the living after reading about the dead. This is a pause, not an activity to score or photograph as proof.

**Reading-only fallback:** use the same sequence with the published sources at home or another ordinary lawful setting. Do not substitute a nearby cultural location when park access is uncertain.

**Reflection question:** Which named voices did I actually read, and which voices am I still missing? No answer is solicited from community members; no contact has been made.
