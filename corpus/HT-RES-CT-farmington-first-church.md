# HT-RES-CT-farmington-first-church — First Church of Christ, Farmington: the 1771 meetinghouse that hosted the Amistad Mende
Division CT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

The white meetinghouse on Farmington's Main Street, raised in 1771–1772 by Captain Judah Woodruff, is the third church on its site — and in 1841 it hosted the Mende survivors of the Amistad, who lived in Farmington for roughly eight months after the Supreme Court ordered their release. It is a National Historic Landmark for both its architecture and that association.

## 1. Trip gate
The meetinghouse stands on Main Street in Farmington village and remains an active congregation. Exterior views are available from the public sidewalk and green. Interior access is typically during worship or by arrangement with the church; confirm current hours and any Amistad-related interpretive programs with First Church of Christ or the Farmington Historical Society before visiting.

## 2. The story
The congregation was organized in 1652 by settlers from Hartford, including connections to Thomas Hooker. Its first meetinghouse dated to the 1660s–1670s; a second stood by 1709. The present building, the third on the site, was raised in 1771–1772 by local builder Captain Judah Woodruff, who also constructed many of the town's early houses. It retains the traditional New England side-entrance plan, a tall slender steeple, and a largely unaltered exterior massing.

In 1841 the church hosted the Mende survivors of the Amistad mutiny. After the U.S. Supreme Court ordered their release, the group lived in Farmington for roughly eight months while funds were raised for their return to Sierra Leone. They attended services here (tradition places them in a gallery section) and participated in a public farewell service in November 1841. Farmington abolitionists, including members of this congregation, supported the case and later Underground Railroad activity. The building was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1975 for both its architecture and its Amistad association.

## 3. Then / now
- 1652: congregation organized.
- 1771–1772: present meetinghouse built by Judah Woodruff.
- 1830s–1901: interior alterations (gallery, pews, organ, pulpit).
- March–November 1841: Amistad Mende attend services and farewell gathering.
- 1975: National Historic Landmark.
- Today: active Congregational church; exterior and steeple largely as completed; interior retains 19th-century modifications.

## 4. What's still there
The white clapboard meetinghouse with its distinctive steeple dominates the village green. The side entrance, window arrangement, and overall colonial proportions survive. The surrounding Main Street historic district contains contemporary houses, some also by Woodruff. Interpretive markers and occasional tours note the Amistad connection.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Park near the Farmington village green or Main Street. Walk the sidewalk for exterior views of the steeple and elevations. If the building is open for services or a scheduled tour, enter to see the gallery and pulpit area associated with the 1841 events. The Farmington Historical Society offers seasonal walking tours that include this site and nearby Underground Railroad locations. Return via Main Street.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Exact seating arrangements of the Mende inside the church rest on oral tradition rather than contemporary diagrams. Precise population figures for the Amistad group while in Farmington vary slightly (approximately 35–36 survivors) across secondary accounts. Primary church records and contemporary diaries were not examined for this entry.
