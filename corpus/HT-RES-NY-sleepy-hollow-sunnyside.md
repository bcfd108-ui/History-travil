# HT-RES-NY-sleepy-hollow-sunnyside — The cottage its author invented twice, and the stone church his horseman rides home to
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Two survivals a few miles apart in the lower Hudson valley: Sunnyside, the riverbank cottage Washington Irving bought in 1835 and remade into the most famous author's house in America, and the Old Dutch Church of Sleepy Hollow, the rubblestone church of Philipsburg Manor whose churchyard Irving turned, in 1820, into the nightly destination of the Headless Horseman.

## 1. Trip gate
This entry is a pair, visited as one ground: Washington Irving's Sunnyside in Tarrytown, operated by Historic Hudson Valley, and the Old Dutch Church and Old Dutch Burying Ground in the village of Sleepy Hollow, a few miles north, held by the Reformed Church of the Tarrytowns. The operator's own page for Sunnyside states the current terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: the site is open May through December — Friday–Sunday, May 8–September 13 and November 6–29, and Saturday–Sunday, December 5–20, plus Memorial Day and Labor Day, with special Legendary Tours from September 18–November 8. Standard tour admission is $18 for adults, $15 for seniors (65+) and young adults (18–25), and $13 for children (3–17), with members and children under 3 free; Legendary Tours are $20 for adults, $18 for seniors and young adults, and $16 for children (3–17). Prices are $2 more for same-day admission. Costumed educators lead 50-minute tours of the cottage, after which visitors may explore the grounds at their leisure. The operator's GPS address is 3 W Sunnyside Lane, Irvington, NY 10533. The church is a separate gate: an active, consecrated building whose visitor page states that the burying ground is open daily until 4:30 p.m., with no entry after 4:30, no restroom facilities on site, and all groups required to register in advance with the church office. Sunnyside was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000583, per the NPS National Register record), and the church the same day (NRHP reference no. 66000581, listed as Dutch Reformed Church).

## 2. The story
The church is the older survival by more than a century. Frederick Philipse, first lord of Philipsburg Manor, held the manor from Spuyten Duyvil to the Croton River, and for his tenants he built a church at the southern end of an existing burying ground where the Pocantico River meets the Hudson. The National Park Service's nomination dates its construction **between 1697 and 1702** and names its builders as Frederick and Catherine Philipse (see §6 for the 1685 date the church itself carries). What the nomination describes still stands: a one-story church, nearly rectangular with a three-sided rear apse, built of high rubblestone walls, its roof a Flemish gambrel shaped, in the nomination's words, like a flared bell, topped by an open octagonal belfry holding a bell cast in Holland in 1685 and a wrought-iron weathervane donated by Frederick Philipse and cut with his initials. The congregation was organized in 1697. The pointed-arch windows a visitor sees are not original — they result from an 1837 remodeling, after a fire; the nomination is explicit that the church is an excellent early Dutch colonial building that also incorporates 19th-century alterations. Through the Revolution the manor was confiscated for the Philipses' Loyalism; the nomination notes the valley was the war's "Neutral Ground," roamed by Loyalist Cowboys and patriot Skinners, and that Major John André was apprehended not far from the church.

Into this ground came a fifteen-year-old New Yorker. Washington Irving (born April 3, 1783) was sent upriver as a boy — the Sunnyside nomination records that when he first saw the old Van Tassel cottage on the Hudson, a few miles below Tarrytown, he was a youth of fifteen, and the cottage was then a simple old stone house with a central chimney, much like a common colonial saltbox. He filed the whole neighborhood away: the Dutch families, the burying ground, the church on its knoll. In England in 1819–1820 he published *The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent.*, containing "Rip Van Winkle" and "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow" — the tale in which Ichabod Crane flees the Headless Horseman toward the church, and in which, as the NPS church nomination puts it, the church "received a measure of popular fame as the Sleepy Hollow church" following the Sketch Book's publication in 1820. Irving became, in Historic Hudson Valley's phrase, America's first internationally famous author, and the characters he set in this valley became global icons.

In **1835** Irving came back and bought the cottage he had seen at fifteen: a small 17th-century Dutch house on the Hudson below Tarrytown, part of the old Wolfert Acker property known as Wolfert's Roost, most recently the Van Tassel farmhouse. With his artist friend George Harvey — named in the National Register record as the house's architect — he enlarged and remodeled it into Sunnyside: a two-and-a-half-story cottage of stone ells, red tile roofs, and crow-stepped gables in imitation of Dutch houses, with a wisteria-covered entrance that became its signature. The plaque Irving set on the porch gable reads, in Dutch, that the house was founded in 1656 and improved by Washington Irving in 1835, with George Harvey as master builder — a founding date the nomination treats as Irving's own choice rather than a settled fact (see §6). For the last 24 years of his life, in the nomination's count, Irving lived and worked at Sunnyside, enlarging the house and landscaping the grounds, possibly influenced by his neighbor Andrew Jackson Downing, who praised Sunnyside in his 1841 *Treatise on the Theory and Practice of Landscape Gardening in America*. The tower wing, added in 1847, gave the house its second silhouette. He finished his five-volume *Life of George Washington* there and died at Sunnyside on **November 28, 1859**. He is buried not in the Old Dutch Burying Ground of his story but just up the hill in the separate, contiguous Sleepy Hollow Cemetery — a distinction the church's own visitor page is careful to print, because visitors constantly merge the two.

The house stayed in Irving family hands after his death and passed in the twentieth century to Sleepy Hollow Restorations — the owner named in the 1975 nomination — whose successor, Historic Hudson Valley, operates it today. The church was superseded as the congregation's main home in 1837, when a larger church was built nearby; the old building is still held by the Reformed Church of the Tarrytowns and used on special occasions. In December 1996 the village of North Tarrytown formally renamed itself Sleepy Hollow, taking the name of Irving's tale; the change is recorded in the Congressional Record of March 11, 1997. The story had, in the end, renamed the place it was set in.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a manor church of rubblestone raised for tenant farmers at the edge of a burying ground, its bell already old — cast in Holland in 1685 — when the congregation organized in 1697. **Now:** the same walls and gambrel roof on U.S. Route 9, the 1837 pointed-arch windows still in them, the burying ground open daily until 4:30 p.m., and the building still a consecrated church, not a museum.

**Then:** a plain stone farmhouse with a central chimney, seen by a fifteen-year-old visitor. **Now:** Sunnyside as Irving remade it — stepped gables, red tile, wisteria, the 1847 tower — kept as a house museum on its landscaped Hudson bank.

**Then:** a fictional horseman who, in Irving's 1820 tale, tethered his horse nightly among the graves of the churchyard and had to regain it before daybreak. **Now:** a village legally named Sleepy Hollow (1996), a Legendary Tours season at Sunnyside each fall, and two adjacent but distinct burial grounds — the Old Dutch Burying Ground of the tale, and Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, where Irving himself lies — that the visitor has to keep separate to read the ground correctly.

## 4. What's still there
- Sunnyside itself: the enlarged cottage of 1835 and after — stone ells, crow-stepped gables, red tile roofs, wisteria-covered entrance, and the 1847 tower — on 10 acres along the Hudson (NPS record; Historic Hudson Valley)
- Irving's study, dining room, parlor, kitchen, and bedrooms, holding his original furnishings; the study's furniture is original (Historic Hudson Valley)
- The grounds Irving laid out: paths, groves, and Hudson vistas described in the National Register nomination
- The Old Dutch Church: rubblestone walls, three-sided apse, Flemish gambrel roof, octagonal belfry with the 1685 Holland-cast bell and the Philipse weathervane (NPS nomination)
- The Old Dutch Burying Ground, about three acres, believed to predate the church itself, with colonial-era stones of residents tradition links to Irving's characters (church visitor page)
- Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, contiguous and separate, holding Washington Irving's grave up the hill from the church (church visitor page)
- The porch-gable plaque at Sunnyside recording Irving's own account: founded 1656, improved by him in 1835, George Harvey master builder (NPS nomination)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At Sunnyside's porch:** Start with the plaque — Irving's own compressed history of the house, 1656 and 1835 — and hold it as his claim, tested against the nomination's account in §6.
2. **Inside the cottage:** The 50-minute costumed tour, through the study where the *Life of George Washington* was finished and the rooms kept with his furnishings.
3. **On the grounds:** The paths and vistas Irving designed, down toward the Hudson — the picturesque landscape the nomination treats as half the property's significance.
4. **At the Old Dutch Church, U.S. Route 9, Sleepy Hollow:** The rubblestone church of 1697–1702 (nomination dating), its 1837 windows, its 1685 bell — read as a working church's building, with the burying ground open until 4:30 p.m.
5. **Between the two burying grounds:** Walk the boundary the sources insist on — the tale's burying ground to the south, Sleepy Hollow Cemetery to the north — and end at Irving's grave in the cemetery, not the churchyard.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The church's construction date does not settle, and both datings print. The church's own visitor materials date the building to **about 1685**, drawn from the year cast into its bell in Holland, with the congregation formally organized in 1697. The National Park Service's National Register nomination dates construction **between 1697 and 1702**, gives the significant year as 1697, and notes that a marble tablet at the church gives 1699 — a 19th-century tablet. The bell's 1685 date, the tablet's 1699, and the nomination's 1697–1702 range are three different kinds of evidence, and this draft does not merge them into one year.

Sunnyside's founding date is likewise the author's own assertion, printed as such. The HABS survey record states the original house was "a small 1½ story Dutch house built, according to Irving, in 1656 by the Van Tassel family" — Irving's attribution, carried in Irving's own plaque on the porch gable. The National Register nomination reports that locals guessed 1656 or the 1680s for the cottage and that Irving chose 1656 and set it in iron letters on the west gable. The 1656 date therefore prints only as Irving's chosen date, not as a verified construction year; what the records verify is the purchase and remodeling of 1835.

Sunnyside's address is carried two ways, and the operator's governs the visit. Historic Hudson Valley's current page gives the GPS address as 3 W Sunnyside Lane, **Irvington**, NY 10533, while the National Register record, the HABS survey (U.S. Route 9, Tarrytown), and generations of print place the house in **Tarrytown**. Both print here; the operator's GPS address is the one in the locator.

No hours or fees for the Old Dutch Church's interior are printed, because no operator source consulted states any: the church is an active congregation's consecrated building, opened for worship and special occasions, and only the burying ground's daily closing (4:30 p.m.) is stated by its visitor page.

The NPS National Register records for both properties (66000583 and 66000581) carry no latitude or longitude, and the church nomination marks its location "not for publication," so the locator below prints no coordinates for the pair — lead-list coordinates were not used. The first photo's records (§7) carry no capture date for the frame, so no year is printed for it.
