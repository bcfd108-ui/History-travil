# HT-RES-OH-hopewell-culture-mound-city — Twenty-five mounds inside a low wall, flattened by an army camp, raised again
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Above the Scioto River north of Chillicothe, a low earthen wall in a rounded square encloses the densest cluster of Hopewell burial mounds ever restored: every mound a building first — a ceremonial house with a clay floor where the dead were cremated, then dismantled and buried under layered earth. The National Park Service calls Mound City Group the only fully restored Hopewell earthwork complex, and asks visitors to remember it is still sacred to many people. Its modern history is as layered as its mounds: surveyed in 1846, plowed for seventy years, covered by a World War I camp that leveled at least half the mounds, excavated and rebuilt in the 1920s, and inscribed in 2023, with seven sister earthworks, on the World Heritage List.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a walk among restored burial mounds inside their enclosure wall: Mound City Group, the visitor-center unit of Hopewell Culture National Historical Park. The operator is the National Park Service, whose pages state the terms printed here. The Visitor Center is at 16062 State Route 104, Chillicothe, OH 45601, off S.R. 104 about 2 miles north of U.S. 35. Visitor center hours are 9:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. every day except Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Day, and New Year's Day. Park grounds are open every day from dawn until dusk. There are no entrance fees at any of the park's sites and no pass is required, as the fees page states. The park's planning guidance allows 30 minutes for the film and visitor center and 30 to 45 minutes to walk the earthwork and nature trail, 1 mile in all. The NPS page carries two standing instructions: there are no trails within the enclosure — visitors may stroll among the mounds but must not walk on the mounds or walls — and drones are not permitted within park boundaries at any time. Visitor center facilities, as the page lists them, are restrooms, a small gift shop, and an auditorium; the page notes the museum as temporarily unavailable. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP no. 66000119, per the NPS Register record; see §6 for a conflicting date inside NPS sources).

## 2. The story
The National Park Service dates the building of Mound City Group across **A.D. 1–400**: people of the culture archaeologists now call Hopewell raised the enclosure and its mounds over many gatherings. The park's Cultural Landscape Inventory widens that span, giving the period of significance as approximately **200 B.C. to A.D. 500**; the disagreement is printed in §6, unresolved. What the NPS page describes is consistent: this was not a town. There is no evidence anyone lived inside the enclosure full time, and the Hopewell did not live in large villages — no more than three Hopewell homes have ever been found in one place. Families lived scattered along the waterways and gathered here, at a walled ceremonial ground, for occasions both religious and social.

Each mound began as a building. In the sequence the NPS page lays out, the builders first raised a ceremonial structure of poles and bark with a plastered clay floor, inside which fires burned in clay basins and ceremonies were held. When the ceremony was a funeral, the dead were cremated; the ashes were entombed on the floor beneath a small mound of clay. About one hundred cremated remains were found at Mound City. At some point the building was purposely dismantled, and only then did mound-building begin — layer over layer of earth, often alternating clay and sand, work the NPS suggests may have proceeded stage by stage over many gatherings. The borrow pits outside the walls may have supplied some material. The finished group, in the page's measurements, is 25 mounds inside a low wall forming a slightly irregular square with rounded corners, about 260 meters across and covering 6.81 hectares (16.8 acres); the wall stands about 1.2 meters high on a base 4.5 meters wide, broken by gateways at the centers of its east and west sides. At the center stands the largest mound, Mound 7, the Central Mound; east of it lies the oblong Mound 3, the Elliptical Mound. Two further mounds stand outside the wall. The counts are disputed; see §6.

The objects in these mounds came from a continent. The park's brochure and Cultural Landscape Inventory describe a network that moved copper, mica, obsidian, and shells across eastern North America — from the Rocky Mountains and the Atlantic and Gulf coasts into the Ohio Valley — where Hopewell craftspeople worked them into the finely made pieces deposited in the mounds. That reach is part of what the 2023 World Heritage inscription recognized.

The group entered the written record in **1846**, when Ephraim G. Squier and Edwin H. Davis excavated and mapped it and gave it the name it still carries — Mound City, for its unusual concentration of mounds. Their report appeared in **1848** in *Ancient Monuments of the Mississippi Valley* (Smithsonian Institution); the collection they took from the mounds is now held by the British Museum, as the park's administrative history records. Then the ground went back to farms: the administrative history describes seventy years of clearing and plowing, with most Scioto Valley mounds worn down as obstacles in the fields.

War finished what the plow began. After the United States entered the First World War on **April 6, 1917**, Chillicothe became the site of Camp Sherman, a cantonment chosen in **June 1917** and built at extraordinary speed — the first draftees arrived on **September 5, 1917**, into a camp of some two thousand buildings housing up to forty thousand soldiers, in a city whose population swelled from 16,000 to 60,000. The camp's grid covered Mound City. Local antiquarian Albert C. Spetnagel and the Ohio State Museum's staff intervened: curator Henry C. Shetrone persuaded the Army to turn one barracks to spare a large mound, and the commander promised buildings would be set on the mounds without destroying them, though pipelines would run through some. Barracks, streets, and utility lines nevertheless cut the group heavily. Mills wrote afterward that camp construction had obliterated all trace of at least half of the original mounds.

What saved the site was the work that followed the camp's demolition. In the spring of **1920** the Ohio Historical Society — the Ohio History Connection's predecessor — began the complete examination Mills published in **1922**, and through 1920–21 the society excavated the group and began its reconstruction, based, as the NPS page states, on Squier and Davis's maps. On **March 2, 1923**, President Warren G. Harding proclaimed the group a National Monument (Proclamation No. 1653). The Park Service conducted further excavation and corrective restoration in the late 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s — the present contours owe most to that work, the nomination states — leaving Mound 15 unrestored, its building's floor plan marked with posts. In **1980** Congress expanded the monument to include part of neighboring Hopeton Earthworks. On **May 27, 1992**, Public Law 102-294 renamed the monument Hopewell Culture National Historical Park and authorized adding the Hopewell Mound Group, Seip Earthworks, and High Bank Works; a sixth unit, Spruce Hill Works, followed in 2009. On **September 19, 2023**, the UNESCO World Heritage Committee, meeting in Riyadh, inscribed the Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks — eight Ohio earthworks, five of them units of this park — as the United States' 25th World Heritage site; Mound City is component 1689-004.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a ceremonial ground built in stages — pole-and-bark buildings with clay floors where the dead were cremated, each dismantled and sealed under a layered mound, inside a low wall with gateways east and west. **Now:** the only fully restored Hopewell earthwork complex, in the Park Service's words — every mound and wall a modern restoration on intact base layers, rebuilt from a record stretching back more than 150 years, with Mound 15 left unrestored and its building's floor plan marked in posts.

**Then:** a famous excavation of 1846, a Smithsonian report of 1848, then seventy years of plowing, followed by an army camp of two thousand buildings laid over the group. **Now:** open grass inside the wall, the mounds in restored profiles — a reconstruction honest about itself, whose park page says the mounds visitors see are restorations and names the one left unrestored.

**Then:** a national monument of a single mound group, proclaimed in 1923 over ground an army camp had just vacated. **Now:** the visitor-center unit of a six-unit national historical park, and one of eight Ohio earthworks inscribed on the World Heritage List in 2023.

## 4. What's still there
- The enclosure wall: a low earthen wall in a rounded square about 260 meters across, about 1.2 meters high, with gateways at the centers of its east and west sides — restored, like all the earthworks here, on intact base layers (NPS)
- The restored mounds inside the wall, from under a meter to over five meters high, including Mound 7, the Central Mound, and the oblong Mound 3, the Elliptical Mound, east of it (NPS)
- Mounds 12 and 13, a conjoined pair covering a connected double structure (NPS)
- Mound 15, the one unrestored mound, its ceremonial building's floor plan marked with short wooden posts (NPS)
- Two mounds outside the enclosure (NPS)
- The borrow pits outside the walls, a likely source of the mounds' earth (NPS)
- The Mound City Group Visitor Center — the park's only one — with film, auditorium, gift shop, and restrooms (NPS)
- The nature trail and paved river walk along the Scioto River, and, on the trail, masonry stones from a lock of the Ohio and Erie Canal that ran along the park's west border in the 1800s (NPS)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the visitor center:** Start with the park's film and exhibits — 30 minutes in the park's planning guidance — including the standing instruction for this ground: it is still a sacred place to many people.
2. **Entering the enclosure:** There is no trail inside the wall, by the park's design. Enter as the park invites — strolling among the mounds, never on them or on the walls — and take the group's measure.
3. **At Mound 15:** The unrestored mound, where posts mark the floor plan of the building that stood here before any mound did — the one place where the §2 sequence is laid out on the ground.
4. **At the center:** Mound 7, the Central Mound, and the Elliptical Mound beside it — the point from which the density that named Mound City in 1846 is clearest.
5. **Outside the wall:** The borrow pits and two outlying mounds, then the nature trail and river walk, where canal-lock stones from the 1800s sit in the same landscape — this ground's later layers, read last.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The mound count is carried three ways, all printed here unresolved. The Park Service's current page describes 25 mounds within the enclosure, plus two outside it. Mills's 1922 report states that Squier and Davis reported 24 or more mounds, and that camp construction had obliterated at least half before the 1920 survey. Many park summaries give 23. No source consulted reconciles 23, 24, and 25 — the figures appear to count differently (original, surviving, restored), but that is an inference, flagged as one.

The enclosure's size is likewise carried two ways. The NPS page gives the walled area as 6.81 hectares (16.8 acres), about 260 meters across. The Cultural Landscape Inventory defines the landscape as the 13-acre area within the walls, plus two outlying mounds, and the nomination also describes an enclosure of approximately 13 acres. Both figures print; no source consulted explains the difference.

The Hopewell span itself is dated differently by the park's sources. The NPS page dates it to A.D. 1–400. The Cultural Landscape Inventory gives approximately 200 B.C. to A.D. 500. The National Register record (NRIS 66000119) carries periods of 499–0 B.C., 499–0 A.D., and, anomalously, 1000–500 A.D. The park brochure describes the Hopewell as living between 2,200 and 1,500 years ago. This draft prints the disagreement rather than a single span.

The Register listing date also conflicts inside NPS sources. The NPGallery Register record (NRIS 66000119) carries a published date of October 15, 1966, which §1 and §10 use. The Cultural Landscape Inventory states the site "was entered into the National Register in 1978." Neither is harmonized here.

No coordinates are printed in the locator, for a specific reason. The Register record consulted carries none. The NPGallery record for this entry's first photograph does carry a latitude and longitude — but the identical point appears, to the full precision given, on the record for a Seip Earthworks photograph, a different park unit miles away, which identifies it as a repeated park-level placeholder rather than a point verified for Mound City's ground. A point that cannot be trusted to be this place is not printed as this place. The locator rests on the street address the NPS itself publishes, and the gap stands open until a nomination, survey, or park asset record verifies a Mound City point on its own terms.

Finally, the visitor center museum is carried as the NPS page stated it — "temporarily unavailable" — with no reopening date in the sources consulted; a gap, not a settled fact.

## 8. Candidate reflection
**Reflection stop only. No hunts, scoring, answer keys, hidden targets or reenactments.**

Candidate reflection: Every mound in this enclosure is a building taken down on purpose — a house for ceremony and for the dead, dismantled by the community that raised it, and only then covered with earth. What changes in how this ground reads when each smooth green mound is held in mind as a roofline first, and a hill second?

This ground is a burial place, still considered sacred by many people. Nothing in this reflection asks a visitor to walk on a mound or a wall, or to treat the burials here as material for anything but quiet attention from the grass between the mounds, where the park itself invites visitors to stroll.
