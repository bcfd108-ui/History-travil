# John Deere Historic Site — the Grand Detour shop, the 1837 steel plow, and the excavated forge ground
HT-RES-IL-john-deere-historic-site · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

John Deere, born in Rutland, Vermont, in 1804 and trained as a blacksmith, left a depressed Vermont economy in 1836 and settled at Grand Detour, Illinois, a village on a bend of the Rock River founded by fellow Vermonter Leonard Andrus and others. A blacksmith was scarce labor on that frontier and Deere found work at once, repairing the tools prairie farming was breaking — above all its plows. The eastern cast-iron plow, built for lighter, rockier soil, would not scour in the rich, sticky prairie earth: soil clung to the moldboard, and farmers stopped again and again to scrape it clean with a wooden paddle, a task the Smithsonian's curator of agriculture, Edward C. Kendall, records as so frequent it seriously interfered with plowing. Blacksmiths across the prairie country were already attacking the problem — Kendall records steel strips nailed to wooden moldboards, and a first plow on record with steel strips attributed to John Lane in Chicago in 1833 — and any honest account of 1837 has to keep that field of experiment in frame. Deere's contribution was not the idea of steel alone; it was a plow that worked, sold, and kept being improved. [2, 3, 4]

The accepted fact, in Kendall's words, is that John Deere made his first steel plow in 1837 at Grand Detour. The construction details have been variously given, and the dossier prints the variance rather than a single heroic version. The company and museum tradition holds that Deere fashioned the plow from a broken steel saw blade — the operator's own history telling places the trial on Lewis Crandall's farm near Grand Detour — and Kendall's technical examination of the surviving early plow refines what "steel plow" meant: only the cutting edge, the share, appears to have been steel on the first Deere plows, with the moldboard of smoothly ground wrought iron, polished so the sticky soil could find no place to cling. The Smithsonian Magazine's later examination adds the curator's caution that Deere did not invent the steel plow in a single moment any more than Henry Ford invented the automobile: he established a process for perfecting it. The plow the Smithsonian holds — identified by the company as one of three made in 1838, obtained in 1901 from Joseph Brierton's farm by Deere's son Charles, and presented to the U.S. National Museum in 1938 — carries a provenance the magazine states plainly has been difficult to establish, with oral history on the farm holding that the family patriarch bought it from Deere in 1838. Kendall, comparing it against 1847 and 1855 examples, judged its cruder moldboard form the earliest of the three. All of that — the saw-blade tradition, the steel-share-only finding, the Lane precedent, the Andrus collaboration claim, and the Smithsonian plow's open provenance — stands in this dossier as printed evidence and conflict, not as a smoothed legend. [2, 3, 4]

Production at Grand Detour was hand-scale and the numbers show it. The Illinois State Archaeological Survey's account gives the shop's output as one plow in 1837, two in 1838, ten in 1839, forty in 1840, seventy-four in 1841, one hundred in 1842 and four hundred in 1843. In 1843 Deere entered formal partnership with Leonard Andrus — the surviving agreement of March 20, 1843, quoted by Kendall, made them "copartners together in the art and trade of Blacksmithing, ploughmaking and all things thereto belonging" at Grand Detour, under the firm name of Leonard Andrus — with Andrus contributing money and business experience and Deere his plow, shop, tools and outbuildings. Steel itself had to travel: the company history records special rolled steel arranged from England in 1843, shipped across the Atlantic, up the Mississippi and Illinois rivers by packet boat, and overland by wagon the last forty miles to Grand Detour, and the first slab of cast plow steel rolled in the United States made for Deere in Pittsburgh in 1846. Kendall also records the competing claim, made by the Case Company history after it took over Andrus's business, that Andrus conceived the plow's design and employed Deere to build it — and Kendall's judgment that at this distance the parts played by the two men cannot be cleanly determined. The dossier carries that attribution dispute open. [2, 4, 5, 6]

Grand Detour could not hold the business. In 1847–1848 Deere dissolved the Andrus partnership and moved to Moline, on the Mississippi River, for water power, coal and cheaper transportation, building a factory there in 1848 that produced seven hundred plows in its first year on the Survey's account. The Grand Detour years — 1836 to 1847 in the Register's significant years — are therefore the whole significance of this ground: the experimental shop, the first plows, and the partnership that turned a blacksmith's repair trade into a manufacturing business, before the business left. Deere lived at Grand Detour with his wife Demarius and their nine children in the house that survives on this site, in the wider site accounts. [1, 5, 6]

The ground's second life is archaeology, and it is unusually well documented. The original blacksmith shop did not survive. In 1962 a team of University of Illinois students approached Deere & Company about excavating where the shop had stood; within about a year the team located the exact shop site, using a magnetometer to find the forge's position. The excavation is preserved today under a building on the site, ringed by exhibits of the artifacts, clippings and photographs the dig produced, and the working blacksmith shop visitors enter now is a replica whose interior follows the excavated original. The Register record frames the formal significance: John Deere House and Shop, Grand Detour, Ogle County, Illinois and Clinton Streets, National Historic Landmark system ID 66000327, areas of significance Invention and Agriculture, period 1825–1849, significant years 1836 and 1847, criterion PERSON, published October 15, 1966 — among the first properties entered when the National Register was established, with landmark designation dated July 19, 1964 in the wider record. The only contributing property in the listing is the Deere House itself, on that record. A company-run museum on an excavated shop floor, anchored by an original 1836 house: that is what a visitor actually stands on, and the dossier routes it exactly that way. [1, 7, 8]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Grand Detour, an unincorporated village on the Rock River in Ogle County, near Dixon in Lee County — the postal address is Dixon because Grand Detour has no separate postal delivery under its own name in the operator's listing. The Register location is Illinois and Clinton Streets. Deere's shop, house and the Andrus partnership all stood on this ground between 1836 and 1847; the manufacturing story after 1848 belongs to Moline, not to this site. [1, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** John Deere Historic Site, 8334 South Clinton Street, Grand Detour (Dixon), IL 61021 — a staffed museum operated by Deere & Company, phone (815) 652-4551. Admission is free. In season (April–October) the visit comprises five components on the wider site description: the original John Deere House, the replica blacksmith shop with live demonstrations, the archaeological pavilion housing the excavated original shop site, a visitor center/gift shop in an 1843 neighbor's house, and two acres of prairie restoration outside the fence. On the checked date the site is closed for the season (from November 1, reopening April 1, 2027, per the operator page); outside the season there is no lawful interior visit and this dossier routes no visit onto the closed grounds beyond what the public road allows. [1, 8]

## 5. Public progression

Come in season, and take the site in its own order. Start in the archaeological pavilion, because it is the only place where the ground itself is the artifact: the excavated footprint of the 1836 shop, the forge position found by magnetometer, and the small, unglamorous finds of a working smithy. Read the production numbers there — one plow in 1837, ten in 1839, one hundred in 1842 — and let the scale stay hand-scale. Then take the replica shop's demonstration for process, knowing it is a replica built over knowledge the dig recovered, and watch what a polished, curved moldboard has to do that a cast-iron one could not. Take the house last among the buildings: a blacksmith's family home of 1836, kept as the listing's one contributing building, where Demarius Deere and nine children lived the domestic half of a business visitors usually meet only as a logo. Finish on the prairie restoration, because the plow's consequence was the prairie itself — broken, planted, and in large part gone — a consequence the Smithsonian's own account refuses to leave as pure triumph, raising the environmental cost of plowing up the grassland alongside the breadbasket it created. **Strongest story moment · The shop floor, not the statue.** The most honest object at Grand Detour is an excavated hole in the ground that a university dig team found in 1962 — proof, fixed in place, that the world-changing plow began as one man's repair bench on a river bend, making one plow a year. In the off-season there is no visit here; plan for April. [1, 2, 3, 8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Season and hours conflict across listings, printed in full and not merged: the current operator page (checked this run) gives April–October, Sun. 1–5, Mon. closed, Tue. 1–5, Wed.–Sat. 9–5, closed Easter and July 4, with the season closing November 1 and reopening April 1, 2027. The City of Dixon attractions page gives April 1–November 30, daily 9–5. An older company-blog listing gives March–December, Mon.–Sat. 9–5 and Sun. noon–4. A farm-press account gives May 1–October 31, Wed.–Sun. 9–5. The gate uses only the current operator page; all other patterns are superseded. [1]
- Admission conflict: the current operator page states free admission for all John Deere attraction sites. Older accounts give $5 for visitors 12 and older (and one gives $5 for all others with children 11 and under free). The gate uses free; the $5 figures are printed as superseded and are not relied on. [1]
- "Steel plow" composition conflict, printed in full: the popular and operator telling describes a plow fashioned from a broken steel saw blade. Kendall's Smithsonian examination concludes only the share was steel on the first Deere plows, the moldboard being smoothly ground wrought iron; later summaries variously describe a wooden moldboard covered with saw-steel strips (in the John Lane manner), a diamond-shaped single metal slab (the 1838 Smithsonian specimen), and a steel moldboard outright. The dossier carries Kendall's examined finding in the Story and prints the other constructions here; no metallurgical report beyond Kendall's was retrieved this run. [2, 3]
- Invention-attribution conflict, printed open: John Lane's steel-strip plow is on record in Chicago in 1833, four years before Deere's; the Case Company history attributed the Grand Detour plow's design conception to Leonard Andrus, with Deere as builder; Kendall judges the Deere–Andrus parts indeterminable at this distance. The dossier credits Deere with the first commercially successful self-scouring steel plow — the operator's and Smithsonian Magazine's shared formulation — and does not claim sole, first-ever invention of the steel plow. [2, 3, 4]
- Smithsonian plow provenance: the National Museum of American History's early plow (dated 1838, ID AG.38A04 in the collections search) is identified by the company as one of three 1838 plows, obtained from the Brierton farm in 1901 and gifted in 1938; Smithsonian Magazine states its provenance has been difficult to establish and that the museum's own account rests on farm oral history. The plow is currently not on view, on the magazine's note. The dossier asserts neither that it is the 1837 plow nor that it is certainly Deere-made; Kendall's comparative judgment (earliest form of three) is carried as judgment, not proof. [3, 4]
- Partnership-date variance: the earliest surviving Deere–Andrus agreement is dated March 20, 1843 (Kendall, from the unsigned copy whose terms match later executed agreements); one company-history summary dates the partnership's land purchase for a two-story Rock River factory to early 1843 / 1842 in different tellings, and one secondary account gives the partnership as 1841–1848. The dossier uses the documented March 1843 agreement and the 1847–1848 dissolution/move, and prints the variance here. Deere's move to Moline is given as 1847 (Kendall: "it was in 1847 that Deere moved") and as 1848 (partnership dissolution and factory building) across sources; both stand, as a move begun in 1847 and a factory operating in 1848. [2, 5, 6]
- Birth-date variance for John Deere: February 7, 1804 in the company and wider record; one magazine account in this run prints 1807 and another prints "born in 1904" (an evident typographical error). The dossier uses 1804 and asserts neither of the misprints. [5]
- County and address framing: the site sits in Ogle County (Register) with a Dixon (Lee County) postal address at 8334 S. Clinton St.; the Register location line is "Illinois and Clinton Sts." Listings that place the site simply "in Dixon" are postal, not jurisdictional. Both are printed; neither is merged. [1, 7]
- House occupancy detail: the wider site accounts give Deere's Grand Detour residence as eleven years (1836–1847) with Demarius and nine children; the Register's significant years are 1836 and 1847. No deed or census record for the house was retrieved this run; the family detail is carried on site accounts, flagged as such.
