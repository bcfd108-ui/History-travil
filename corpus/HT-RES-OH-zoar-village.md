# HT-RES-OH-zoar-village — A whole village that held everything in common, and kept its streets
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Most communal experiments in American history left a ruin, a marker, or nothing. Zoar left a village. In Tuscarawas County, along the river of the same name, the streets of a German Separatist commune still run where its members laid them out: the store, the hotel, the bakery, the meeting house, the numbered houses, and a formal garden at the center that its designer planted as a picture of paradise. The Society of Separatists of Zoar held all property in common from 1819 to 1898 — nearly eighty years — and when the members finally voted the Society out of existence, they divided the property among themselves and stayed. This entry's ground is that village: the Zoar Historic District, a National Historic Landmark, part living town and part museum.

## 2. The story
The Separatists were Pietists from the kingdom of Württemberg in southwestern Germany who had broken with the Lutheran church and were persecuted for it. In **1817** they emigrated for religious freedom, sailing to Philadelphia and moving on to Ohio under their leader Joseph Bimeler — born Baumeler, a teacher and pipemaker from Ulm, per the Western Reserve Historical Society's finding aid for the Society's own records. There they bought some 5,500 acres along the Tuscarawas River, land purchased from Godfrey Haga with the help of loans from Philadelphia Quakers; Bimeler promised to pay the remaining balance of $15,000 within fifteen years. They named the settlement Zoar, after the biblical town to which Lot fled from Sodom — a refuge name for a refugee people. How many came is disputed in the sources (§6): the Society's records trail at the Western Reserve Historical Society says three hundred; Ohio History Connection's Ohio Memory account says approximately two hundred.

Communal living was not the original plan; it was the survival plan. After two years of struggling to sustain the settlement, repay the debts, and care for the elderly and infirm, the settlers adopted it: on **April 19, 1819**, they signed the Articles of Association and formed the Society of Separatists of Zoar, each member giving his or her property to the community as a whole and receiving, in exchange for their work, everything they needed from it, per Ohio Memory's account of the Society. Bimeler was elected agent-general, in charge of all the community's affairs, and held that post until his death. For financial as well as religious reasons, the Western Reserve Historical Society's history of the Society records, the community in its early years required celibacy — children who could not work were a cost a commune in debt could not carry.

The canal paid the debt. Seven miles of the Ohio and Erie Canal were routed through the Zoarites' land, and in **1827** they contracted with the State of Ohio to dig the section that traversed their land, per the National Historic Landmark nomination. The contract enabled the Separatists to pay off the debt from their initial land purchase, and by the early 1830s Zoar was debt-free; the canal then became the village's road to market, and the Zoarites ran their own canal boats on it, shipped their surplus on it, and sold supplies and bread to other canal contractors. Bimeler laid out the village's centerpiece in the same years: the central garden of about two and a half acres, designed by Bimeler and planted by **1829**, its geometry drawn from the Book of Revelation.

Prosperity built the village a visitor still walks. The Canal Inn opened in **1832** to serve boat crews and passengers; the Zoar Hotel followed in **1833**, built — in the words of the Historic American Buildings Survey's significance statement for the building — by enterprising Zoarites to make money on the tourist trade, a building that marks the community's turn toward commerce with the outside world; the Zoar Store also dates to 1833, per the Community Association. The Number One House, a two-and-a-half-story brick and sandstone house built in **1835** as a home for the Society's elderly, became Bimeler's own residence when the older members chose to stay in their own homes, and he lived there until his death, per the Ohio History Connection. The brick Meeting House — the village's third — rose in **1853**, the same year the Society's center gave way: Joseph Bimeler died in **1853** (the Western Reserve Historical Society gives the date as August 31, 1853), and at his death the Society's holdings were valued at more than a million dollars per the Landmark nomination's account — or more than five million, per the Historical Society's finding aid; §6 prints both. At its peak the Society counted nearly 500 members and, by the 1850s, held more than 10,000 acres.

The decline was slow and then argued in print. No successor matched Bimeler's combined spiritual and business authority. In **1895** Levi Bimeler, a great-grandson of the founder, started the village's first periodical, *The Nugitna* — "anti-Gunn" spelled backward, aimed at a retired Cleveland businessman the trustees had taken up — and used it to attack the trustees and the principle of communal ownership itself, per the Western Reserve Historical Society's history. He was made to stop, but the discontent did not. In **1898** the remaining members dissolved the Society of Separatists of Zoar — in September of that year, per the Ohio Archaeological and Historical Quarterly's guide to Ohio's communistic communities — and divided the common property among themselves.

The village outlived the Society twice over. The Zoar Historical Society was formed in **1930**, and its supporters succeeded in saving the village from being flooded — the district's Archipedia account records the fight against a flood-control plan that would have drowned the town its founders built. The district was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on **June 23, 1969**, per the National Park Service's Register record, with later boundary increases; and on **October 31, 2016**, the Secretary of the Interior designated the Zoar Historic District a National Historic Landmark, per the nomination itself, which documents some 208 acres and 140 resources and gives the period of national significance as 1818 to 1898 (on that start year, against the 1817 founding, see §6).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a self-contained German commune on the Tuscarawas — church, communal bakery, tin shop, blacksmith shop, store, furniture shop, weaving and sewing houses, pottery, mills, and a brewery, per the Historic American Buildings Survey's summary of the village at its height, all of it owned in common and run by elected trustees. **Now:** the same street plan and dozens of the same buildings, divided among the Ohio History Connection's museum properties, the Zoar Community Association, and private residents — a landmark district that is still a hometown.

**Then:** a canal the Zoarites dug with their own hands across their own land, paying off their land debt with the state contract and floating their surplus to market on their own boats. **Now:** the canal's corridor survives as the towpath landscape at the village's edge, and the buildings it paid for — the Canal Inn, the Zoar Hotel, the Store — stand as the district's exhibits.

**Then:** a central garden planted by 1829 as theology in geometry — a representation of the Book of Revelation's paradise, tended in common. **Now:** the Zoar Garden, still the village's centerpiece and still planted in its formal pattern, the one piece of the commune's ground that has been continuously gardened ever since.

## 4. What's still there
- The Zoar Hotel (1833), Second and Main Streets — exterior restored; the Ohio History Connection's visitor coverage states the interior renovation is unfinished and the interior is not yet open to view (Historic American Buildings Survey OH-280; Ohio History Connection)
- The Number One House (1835), Bimeler's residence and now a museum of Zoar artifacts (Ohio History Connection)
- The Bimeler Museum (the Bimeler House), among the museum buildings operated by the Zoar Community Association (Zoar Community Association)
- The Meeting House (1853), now the Zoar United Church of Christ (Ohio History Connection)
- The Zoar Store (1833), the Community Association's base and the visit's starting point (Zoar Community Association; Ohio History Connection)
- The Zoar Garden, the Society's central formal garden (National Historic Landmark nomination)
- The museum craft buildings: Bakery, Blacksmith Shop, Tin Shop, Wagon Shop, Sewing House, Dairy, Kitchen and Magazine (Zoar Community Association's own list of the museum buildings)
- Bimeler's original log cabin, still standing and viewable from the outside (Ohio History Connection)
- The district as a whole: approximately 208 acres and 140 resources, per the National Historic Landmark nomination, including private homes that are not open to visitors

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Zoar Store, 198 Main Street:** Start where the operator starts visitors — the 1833 store, for the admission and the map of which buildings are open that day; the terms are the operator's, in §1.
2. **At the Garden:** The commune's center and its theology made visible. Stand at the middle of Bimeler's geometry before reading any single building.
3. **At the Number One House and the Bimeler Museum:** The Society's domestic core — the house built for the elderly that became the leader's home, and the museum of the village's own artifacts.
4. **Through the craft buildings:** Bakery, blacksmith, tin shop, sewing house — the division of labor that made common ownership work, one trade per building.
5. **At the Zoar Hotel, Second and Main:** End with this entry's first photograph in hand — the hotel as Carl Waite photographed it for HABS in 1936, northwest elevation — against the restored exterior standing there now. The hotel is the commune's argument with the outside world, in clapboard: built to take the world's money without joining it.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The size of the founding party is disputed in the sources consulted and is printed unresolved. The Western Reserve Historical Society's finding aid for the Society of Separatists of Zoar Records states that three hundred Separatists sailed from Germany in April 1817; the Ohio History Connection's Ohio Memory account of the village says the original settlers numbered approximately 200. Both figures print; neither is adopted here.

The Society's worth at Bimeler's death in 1853 is likewise printed as a dispute. The National Historic Landmark nomination's account, as carried in the Society of Architectural Historians' Archipedia entry, values the Society's holdings at more than a million dollars at his death. The Western Reserve Historical Society's finding aid states that at Bimeler's death Zoar "was estimated to be worth more than five million dollars." The five-fold gap is in the sources, not in this draft's arithmetic, and it is left standing.

The Zoar Hotel's own dates conflict inside the federal record. The Historic American Buildings Survey's record for the hotel (HABS OH-280) carries two different initial-construction dates for the same building — 1833 and 1835 — in its building/structure dates. Its expansion dates also conflict between federal sources: the HABS record gives subsequent work in 1850 and 1860, while the Landmark nomination describes the hotel as expanded in 1850, 1880, and 1892. This draft uses 1833 for the hotel in the story because the nomination and the Ohio History Connection both carry 1833, and prints the 1835 and the expansion conflicts here rather than resolving them.

The Landmark nomination gives the district's period of national significance as 1818 to 1898, while the founding it documents — the purchase and settlement — is dated 1817 by the Ohio History Connection, the Historical Society's finding aid, and the Register record's significant years (1817, 1898). Whether 1818 is a considered judgment or a nomination convention is not explained in the material consulted; the difference is flagged as a gap, not smoothed.

The operator's tour hours beyond September are a gap in this pass: the Ohio History Connection page as consulted states tour hours for April–May and June–September and retail hours for March–December, and no October tour schedule was verified from the operator's own page. Visitors in October are working from a gap this draft prints rather than fills. Which museum buildings are open on a given day is likewise set by the operator on the day and is not asserted here.

No coordinates were taken from any lead list. The locator's point is the single latitude/longitude pair the Landmark nomination itself prints — the starting point of its verbal boundary description — scoped in §10 as exactly that, not as a village center or a museum entrance.
