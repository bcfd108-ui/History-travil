## 2. Story

At Los Adaes on January 24, 1738, the people who signed a power of attorney used their own names when they could write. The University of Texas archaeological project reproduces the signature portion. The inspected facsimile has a cluster of signatures and witness lines. The project caption identifies Lt. Joseph Gonzales and Mateo Ybarbo among the signers. It also identifies people whose witnesses signed for them, including Juan de Mora. That is one way a largely official archive preserves different levels of access to writing and representation. By the time the garrison and settlement were being evacuated in 1773, collective representation had become a question of how displaced households might find a place to live. [4–6, 13]

Antonio Gil Ibarvo, also spelled Ybarbo, appears as the representative in the University of Arizona’s docket for the settlers’ 1773–74 petition. The document title describes the parties as “los vezinos del real presidio de los Adaes” (the residents of the royal presidio of Los Adaes, editorial translation). That quote is the archive’s transcribed document title. The underlying petition has not been read verbatim here. Archivist A. Nielsen’s 1989 summary says the petitioners told colonial officials that they had left property and improvements behind and found suitable farmland scarce around Béxar. The record tracks a request to resettle and the officials’ response. It reports the settlers’ claims about their circumstances, with no corresponding statement of Indigenous consent or perspective. [5–7]

The Spanish presidio once housed the administration for Spanish Texas. Juan Antonio de la Peña, who accompanied Aguayo, recorded that “The Marqués immediately set about selecting a site upon which to build the presidio” in the English translation excerpt printed by NPS. His account describes the choice of ground from the expedition’s perspective. A separate retrieval of the University of Arizona Press text returned only a privacy page, and The Huntington’s catalog record did not supply the relevant manuscript page. The quote is from the published translation excerpt. [2, 16–17] It depended in practice on relationships with nearby French and Indigenous people despite imperial restrictions. The NPS and Louisiana archaeology sources describe that daily exchange alongside the official rivalry. Archaeology can recover material traces of exchange that one executive order leaves out. Read the evacuation as loss of homes and work, and leave mission, archaeological and Tribal ground outside the itinerary. [2, 6, 8–9]

## 3. Then and now

Then — The 1738 document and 1773–74 petition index bracket two moments when people at or from the presidio needed an authorized representative. Papers written for colonial administration survive more readily than individual household accounts. [4–7]

Now — Excavation and collections inform public interpretation. NPS describes an on-site museum, and the National Register records both the original listing and a boundary increase. This dossier supplies no visitor pin while the separately lawful access boundary remains unverified. [1–3, 8–10]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The steward dates establishment to 1719 following the French attack. NPS places the attack in 1719 and Aguayo’s reestablishment in 1721. The state archaeological overview dates the fort to 1721. This draft keeps the attack and subsequent establishment chronology distinct. [2, 8–9, 14]
- Louisiana Division of Archaeology’s overview calls the site 1721–1773 but says the fort closed in 1772. The state educational guide uses 1721–1773. The University of Texas account places actual eviction and departure in 1773, alongside the petition’s October 1773 start. The wording may reflect a closure decision versus physical removal, but the cited overview does not say so. Keep these dates distinct. [5–6, 8–9]
- The steward’s operational page specifies grounds only, while its individual listing gives hours without that qualifier and the NPS trail page describes an open site with museum exhibits. Use the explicit narrower steward scope. [1–3, 14–15]
- The National Register has an original listing and a separately numbered boundary increase. Those records characterize historical property and do not fix public circulation. Confirm whether the steward offers a genuinely separate interpretive-only entrance before any route. [1–3, 10–11]
- Briscoe Center item 349000, a 1778 Croix translation record about a later settlement, returned HTTP 403 on direct web retrieval. The Arizona entry provides an archival summary and docket title, with no attached original petition images seen here. The published 1738 signature detail is an inspected excerpt with an editorial caption. The full instrument and the exact boundaries of the associated mission are not established here. [4–5, 12]
- The state’s named archaeology overview and register listing were recovered. Their historical descriptions do not establish an on-the-ground public boundary. No operator confirmation of that boundary was received in this return. [1, 8–11]
