## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A public museum can share history without making everything in that history public property.

**Named community account published by the museum operator:** Its featured-artist profile identifies **Dan Vallo** as an Acoma Pueblo member and recalls his learning from his grandmother **Helen R. Vallo**, a potter, and great-uncle **Clyde Hunt**, a silversmith. The profile attributes this short first-person statement to him: **“This is where I get my artistic inspiration”.** It is an operator-published account of artistic inheritance, not a personal interview conducted for this dossier. [1]

The useful historical thread is the relationship between a named maker and the people who influenced him. No collecting locations, material-gathering directions or technical weapon-making content from the broader profile is reproduced. Nor does the profile establish that his works are currently on view in the museum.

**Pueblo-government account:** The Pueblo's own cultural-preservation page describes community research into language use beginning in 1997 and establishment of its language-retention program and historic-preservation office in 1999. It presents this as work for future generations, rather than preservation of a culture that has ended. Its old nationwide language-loss projection is not adopted as a verified 2026 statistic. [4]

**Named primary public statement about custodianship:** A December 22, 2022 release from the **Pueblo of Acoma Office of the Governor**, republished in ICT's Press Pool, quotes former Governor **Brian Vallo** describing his personal cultural-practitioner and professional museum experience in helping shape the STOP Act. Governor **Randall Vicente** describes a long effort to protect culture and beliefs. These are named statements issued by the Pueblo, hosted by an external publisher, not independent interviews by ICT. [5]

The release reports the return of a ceremonial item in November 2019. This dossier deliberately gives no item-location detail, image or display expectation. A return to its people is not equivalent to a museum acquisition offered for tourist inspection. The release's historical legal account is not a current implementation or enforcement review. [5]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Read the museum and the sovereign government together without merging their authority. Artists decide what they share; museum staff interpret admitted public material; the Pueblo governs access and cultural responsibilities. A visitor can appreciate skill and continuity without assuming ownership of a design, story or image.

## 3. Then / now
The named artist's account describes family influence carried into contemporary creative work. [1] The government's preservation history describes organized efforts to sustain knowledge across generations. [4] The 2022 release adds the struggle to recover and protect material that was never simply a market commodity. [5]

These are related themes, not evidence that an individual artist participated in each government initiative. No family relationship is inferred between Dan Vallo and Brian Vallo merely because they share a surname.

No period photograph has been matched to a current museum view. This is a comparison of public accounts and purposes, not a reconstructed path through the community or a claim that a present display contains particular returned objects.

## 4. What's still there
The operator offers a museum concerned with Acoma history, creativity and revitalization of art forms. [3] That public invitation is specific enough to support a conditional museum visit. It is not a license to explore the surrounding Pueblo.

The operator's guidelines explicitly place Pueblo tours under Tribal Council privilege. They also require permission for photographing people or their artwork, describe a camera-permit system and prohibit commercial use of Acoma imagery for profit. [6] This dossier neither purchases a permit nor interprets those rules as a blanket gallery-photo license.

The publicly readable preservation page and Governor's release remain alternatives if the museum is unavailable. No administrative office, school, residence or religious building is substituted for the museum.
