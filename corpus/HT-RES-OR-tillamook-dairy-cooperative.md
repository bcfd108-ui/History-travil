## 2. The story
**Original interpretive proposition:** a cooperative brand can look like a single institution while the lives behind it remain particular households, marriages, inheritances and working decisions. The useful historical question is not whether the cheese tastes good. It is how one family continues farming while participating in something larger than its own name.

Tillamook's own history presents the association as farmer-owned since 1909. This is the company's account of itself, not an independent examination of founding minutes, membership rights or present supply contracts. Label it as such wherever used. [3]

A named local voice makes the question concrete. In Karl Anderson's *Headlight Herald* dairy feature, July 19, 2022, p. A9, Kurt Mizee says: **“The farm has been a Josi farm, a Fenk farm, and a Mizee farm,”** followed by a description of its generational continuity. The article identifies Kurt and his parents Bart and Terry as owners at that time. Its family account begins in 1918 with Fred Josi and passes through Rudy Fenk, his son-in-law. These are dated reported statements, not a verified 2026 ownership register. [4]

Denise Porter's January 12, 2017 local profile gives Rudy Fenk's perspective on continuity and change: **“I wouldn’t be here if it wasn’t for the new ideas.”** It relates the household through Rudy and Ruth and names the founder Alfred Josi. The publisher is a tourism organization and the profile is sympathetic community storytelling. That does not invalidate the attributed voice, but it does require context rather than promotion disguised as neutral history. [5]

The Library of Congress separately catalogs an August 25, 2022 interview of the Mizee family by Jared L. Schmidt in the Tillamook cheesemakers occupational-folklife project. That provides a substantial primary-record lead, not permission to invent testimony. Its audio and transcript were not retrieved in this pass; neither is quoted. [6]

**Visitor-facing original narration:** Before you see a product, notice the names. A family can stay connected to a place while its surname changes; a shared brand can endure while each household must decide how to carry on. At the visitor gallery, ask which kinds of work the display makes visible and which remain outside its frame. The public visit is an introduction to those questions, not a substitute for the workers' own records.

This entry does not establish a complete social history of dairying, land ownership, Indigenous displacement, wage labor or environmental consequences. It does not turn the family accounts into evidence that every worker is an owner, every ingredient comes from a member farm, or every marketing claim is historically verified.

## 3. Then / now
- **Company chronology:** the operator's 1909 cooperative origin claim belongs to institutional history; it is not the founding date of every associated farm. [3]
- **Household chronology:** the 1918 family-farm origin in the two local accounts is a separate event. Their Fred/Alfred naming difference remains unresolved. [4][5]
- **Recorded experience:** 2017 and 2022 profiles describe people and arrangements at those dates. They must not be silently updated into current ownership, residence or employment. [4][5]
- **Now:** the company offers a public interpretive visitor experience, not general public access to dairy agriculture. [1][2]

Interpretive comparison, not an established continuity claim: visitors can compare a household-centered historical account with the institution's present self-presentation. The public facility need not be the family's original workplace for that comparison to be useful. Do not label the present visitor building as a surviving 1909 factory without separate building evidence.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**One-site visit, not a farm circuit. Suggested 45–75 minutes is an editorial planning allowance, not an operator duration.** Food purchases and premium experiences are optional and not needed to complete the historical exercise.

1. **Before departure:** check the operator's current visitor and closure notices. Carry the two short attributed family statements as reading, not reproductions of the newspaper photograph. If closed, retain this as a reading exercise rather than seeking another entrance. [1][2][4][5]
2. **Begin at the designated public entrance:** establish that this is a company-operated interpretation venue. Read its account as the institution's chosen story, not a complete verdict on the industry.
3. **Start with the farm interpretation:** bring the household question into the visit. What can the public display tell you about inheritance, routine and changes in work? Do not assume that a pictured family or machine identifies the Mizee household without an explicit label.
4. **Continue only along the public visitor sequence to the viewing space:** distinguish watching an authorized display from entering a workplace. If the gallery is unavailable, omit it; do not replace it with a service entrance or perimeter search.
5. **End with the strongest story moment:** wherever permitted in the public visitor area, reread Kurt's succession of family names beside the idea of a single enduring brand. Ask: what does the institution preserve, and what must each household reinvent? This is the proposed emotional landing, not an assertion that a particular installation already tells it.

No photograph, staff interaction, purchase, factory access or farm visit is necessary. No route between the visitor center and private property is supplied. A visitor who wants deeper evidence should read the archival record when available, not approach the interviewees.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Fred / Alfred Josi:** newspaper and tourism profile differ. A nickname is plausible but unproved; do not silently harmonize. [4][5]
- **Terry / Terri:** different publications use different spellings for a family member. Use the spelling of the specific cited record, not a claimed corrected identity. [4][5][6]
- **Oral history:** official catalog metadata was available through indexed retrieval, but direct item/resource requests failed. Audio not heard, transcript not read, individual photographs not inspected. Catalog description is not testimony. [6]
- **Source quality:** the newspaper feature includes advocacy and unsupported health assertions unrelated to the family-history passage. Those assertions are not adopted. A named quotation supports what the speaker said, not every claim made in the article. [4]
- **Governance and labor:** membership terms, historical bylaws, pay, hired-worker experience and changes in bargaining power need primary records. Farmer ownership must not erase employee labor.
- **Current exhibit specificity:** operator pages support the visitor venue, not the presence of every historical subject proposed here. The family-specific interpretive overlay is editorial, not a promised museum exhibit.
- **Place history:** no Tribal-history account has been established in this narrow pass. Do not imply dairying began on unoccupied land; a broader land-history treatment requires relevant Tribal-published sources first.
- **Access and rights:** no entrance coordinates, detailed route audit, live road check or cleared publication photograph. These are honest blanks, not reasons to invent precision.
