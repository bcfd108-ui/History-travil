## 2. The story
**Start with a named Indigenous interpretation, not an empty-land premise.** Dr. Shane Doyle, Apsáalooke, writes “Land of Steam” for *National Parks*, Winter 2023. Yellowstone Peoples, which describes itself as a Native-American nonprofit including members of affiliated Indigenous nations, links to his article under his name. That is a community-published pointer to his own authored work, not a claim of endorsement by every Tribal government. [4][5][6]

Doyle states: **“Yellowstone National Park is an ancient Native homeland”**. He recalls visiting as a child without understanding his family's connection, then learning from elders, oral historians and written materials. His introduction places educational exclusion and interrupted transmission alongside his later recovery of knowledge. This is his published personal and cultural interpretation, not a federal exhibit script. [5]

For the administrative history, NPS records that President Ulysses S. Grant signed the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act on **March 1, 1872**. That is a date of federal designation, not the beginning of human history or a finding that the land had no existing relationships. [7]

**Original visitor narration:** A legal boundary can create a new institution without creating the place itself. Begin with the institution's founding date, then ask what that date cannot tell you: whose knowledge was already here, whose relationships continued, and whose words a later visitor has been taught to hear. The most important correction is not adding an ornamental “before” paragraph. It is refusing to let the administrative anniversary stand for the whole history.

This dossier does not retell Doyle's published sacred narratives, turn their locations into clues, or infer permission to perform ceremonies. It links readers to his own framing. His specific Apsáalooke perspective must not become a generic voice assigned to all Native nations. No legal opinion about present treaty rights or jurisdiction is supplied.

## 3. Then / now
- **1872:** federal park establishment is a documented administrative event. Keep it in its historical scope. [7]
- **1909 structure / present interpretation:** NPS identifies the Albright building's original Army residential function and its current visitor-center use. A building can carry several histories without making every room public. [2]
- **Contemporary authored interpretation:** Doyle's essay is living, named scholarship and recollection, not an anonymous relic of a vanished population. [5]
- **Community direction:** Yellowstone Peoples publishes a vision for intertribal cultural revitalization, youth mentorship and stronger Tribal participation in management. This is the organization's stated aim, not proof that a particular governance change or current visitor event has occurred. [6]

Original comparison prompt: *What changes when a place is introduced as an institution, and what changes when it is introduced as a homeland?* Both sources remain identified; they are not blended into an invented consensus.
