# Museum of Mississippi History and the people on the Greenville levee

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP08 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Public history galleries, Two Mississippi Museums, 222 North Street, Jackson. Use the staffed museum entrance. Posted hours are Tuesday–Saturday 9 a.m.–5 p.m. and Sunday 1–5 p.m., with free admission Sunday. [1]

## 2. Story

In April 1927, crews worked through days of rain to keep the Mississippi River out of Greenville and the surrounding Delta. Mayor John Aloysius Cannon recorded sending men from the town’s protection levee to reinforce another crew. On April 21 a levee north of Greenville gave way. The city had arranged a fire whistle as its signal. Cannon remembered having trouble finding anyone authorized to confirm the break. Teenager Mildred Shepherd remembered setting aside her Latin exam as the whistle sounded. These are different experiences of one alarm. [2,3]

## 3. Then and now

A tent camp grew along the Greenville levee. Displaced families first made shelters from what they had brought. The Red Cross supplied tents and food. Black residents also supplied much of the labor that kept supplies moving. Local leaders restricted evacuation and required men to work without wages. Historian Princella W. Nowell traces how relief and coercion operated together. The museum’s Promise and Peril gallery puts that history in its account of the years leading to 1927. [2,4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

The surviving manuscripts and photographs are held in collections. Jackson’s museum interprets the flood within a longer history that begins with Indigenous communities and continues into modern Mississippi. Its permanent galleries are distinct from the neighboring Civil Rights Museum. No manuscript, photograph or tent described here is represented as currently on view unless expressly identified by the museum. [1,3–5]

## 5. Public progression

At the museum, move from the gallery’s account of the labor on the levee to the question of who could leave. A photograph preserved by the Library of Congress records the tents. Cannon’s account preserves municipal decisions. Neither source alone tells what an unpaid worker could refuse. [2–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The archive’s description of Cannon’s narrative names Stops Landing, while Nowell identifies the April 21 crevasse at Mound Landing. The draft keeps the event north of Greenville without resolving the place-name discrepancy or assigning a visitor point. No journey to a working levee is part of the entry. [2,3]
