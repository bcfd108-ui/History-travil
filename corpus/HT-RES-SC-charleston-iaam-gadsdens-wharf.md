## 2. Story

Read two dates together. Congress approved the act prohibiting the importation of enslaved people on March 2, 1807. Section 1 applied the ban beginning January 1, 1808. Between those dates, Charleston’s trade continued. The law was a prohibition on foreign importation. Its later sections also regulated coastwise shipment of enslaved people and required manifests for qualifying vessels. The federal text records the government’s terms. It leaves the lives of the people listed by those terms to be found in other records. [3]

Charleston County Public Library’s history traces a public landing and Gadsden’s privately developed wharf as parts of the same changing shoreline. A College of Charleston historical and archaeological project traces a May 1807 group of arrivals through newspaper advertisements. The project’s report on printed page 18 cites a sale notice for the ship Alice in the Charleston Courier dated May 4, 1807. The report gives 405 captives taken from Africa and 364 people arriving at Gadsden’s Wharf. These are figures attributed by the project, from a notice whose original issue remains uninspected. May 4 is the cited newspaper issue date. The sale record identifies a ship while leaving the names of the people it carried open in this draft. [4–5, 9–10]

When the city announced the selected museum site in 2014, Mayor Joseph P. Riley Jr. spoke of the place where the wharf stood as a sacred site. The museum’s Atlantic Worlds gallery interprets the Middle Passage within wider histories of movement, resistance and cultural survival. Those voices approach the same ground from a different direction than a shipping record or statute. [2, 6]

## 3. Then and now

The wharf belonged to a working port. The museum now occupies part of the historic waterfront location. The city’s site announcement and the county library history agree on the association. They do not provide a field-verified reconstruction of the precise plank edge, landing line or historic passage beneath today’s building. Use the present museum entrance as the anchor and read the historical footprint as documentary context. [1, 4, 6]

## 4. What survives and where a visitor may stand

- International African American Museum: ordinary ticketed indoor galleries and signed entry from the public land-side approach. Its dedicated visiting page lists regular nonresident adult admission as $22.50 and prints other categories separately. Physical admission can change. [1]
- Atlantic Worlds: indoor interpretation of the Atlantic world, including the Middle Passage. Gallery or media availability can vary. [2]
- Original wharf and waterfront memorial ground: quiet documentary context. The dossier does not invite a shore visit or assign an exact pin to the original landing.

## 5. Strongest visit moment

Read the effective date of the 1807 law before entering Atlantic Worlds. In the gallery, give time to a person’s story that the museum has actually attributed. **The shift from a law’s categories to a life the record can name** is the strongest moment. The whole visit fits in the indoor interpretation space. [1–3]

## 6. Evidence limits and disagreements

The institution’s detailed museum-hours listing says noon Sundays effective July 2026. Its shorter widget says Tuesday–Sunday from 10 a.m. Follow the detailed schedule and verify on the day. The April 1807 local proclamation and May ship-sale notices appear in footnotes of freely accessible historical research. The May 4, 1807 Courier issue is a precise retrieval lead, including the report’s note 84. LOC catalogs the title and its microfilm holdings. The University of Illinois records a credentialed online collection. Neither supplies a freely accessible scan of that issue here. A named first-person account tied to the specific landing remains open. No claim is made about a single precise historic boundary or a total share of all enslaved arrivals. [1, 4–5, 7]
