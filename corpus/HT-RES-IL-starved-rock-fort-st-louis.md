# Starved Rock and the site of Fort St. Louis / Le Rocher
HT-RES-IL-starved-rock-fort-st-louis · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The high sandstone butte on the south bank of the Illinois River was known to the French as Le Rocher — the Rock. The National Register nomination describes it as a flat-topped formation of St. Peter sandstone, capped by coal measures, rising above the river in what is now Starved Rock State Park, six miles west of Ottawa off Route 71, its bluffs and canyons exposed by the LaSalle Anticline. The Illinois State Museum records human occupation on and around the rock reaching back more than 10,000 years, from the late Paleo-Indian period forward. That long Indigenous presence is the ground on which every later chapter here stands. [3, 4, 9]

Across the river to the north stood the Grand Village of the Kaskaskia, part of the Illinois Confederacy — the Illiniwek. The nomination records that Louis Jolliet and Jacques Marquette stopped at the Kaskaskia village in August 1673, finding 73 cabins, and that Marquette returned in 1675 to found the Mission of the Immaculate Conception, described in the nomination as the first mission in Illinois. By 1680, joined by other Illinois groups, the village had grown to an estimated 400–550 cabins and 6,000–9,000 people. In the fall of 1680 the village was destroyed in an Iroquois attack during the Beaver Wars, and its people left the site for about three years. The village, its people and its losses are recorded here as history in their own right, not as background to the fort that followed. [3, 9]

René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, first pointed to this rock in writing in March 1680, directing his lieutenant Henri de Tonti, then at Fort de Crèvecoeur on Peoria Lake, to consider a second fort on the high cliff. After La Salle's voyage down the Mississippi to the Gulf and his claim of the valley for France, the French returned in the fall of 1682. Working under Tonti through the winter of 1682–83, they built Fort St. Louis on the summit, finishing in the spring of 1683. The Illinois State Museum, quoting Robert L. Hall (1991), holds that the pattern of remains on the summit is consistent with this identification — Le Rocher as the location of the first Fort St. Louis built in Illinois. [3, 4, 6]

La Salle's own account, preserved in the nomination and in the Foundation for Illinois Colonial and American Studies summary of Margry, describes why the place was chosen and how it was held. The rock is steep on almost all sides, about six hundred feet in circumference, accessible on one side only, and close enough to the river that water could be drawn from the summit. That approach was closed by a palisade of white oak stakes eight to ten inches in diameter and twenty-two feet high, flanked by three redoubts of squared beams; the remaining rim carried a lower palisade, about fifteen feet, with further redoubts, a parapet of trees laid lengthwise and filled with earth, and an iron-pointed cheval-de-frise against climbing. Inside stood a chapel, a warehouse or magazine, living quarters, traders' cabins of about twenty feet square, and Indigenous structures, with buildings carried to the very edge of the cliff. Below, an island formerly cleared by the Illinois, within musket shot of the fort, was sown by La Salle's settlers. [3, 6, 9]

The fort's position did two kinds of work at once. It towered above the lowest rapids on the Illinois River and so controlled a main passage between Canada and the south, and it stood directly across from the returning Illinois villages — about 300 cabins in a series of contiguous settlements after 1683, by the nomination's estimate. La Salle intended it as an administrative and trading center for a wider enterprise: hides gathered in the Illinois Country carried south to an ice-free Gulf port for shipment to France, and a chain of posts that would hold French influence in the valley. Miami, Shawnee and Mahican communities also gathered near the fort in these years, most leaving in 1688 and 1689. Mission work ran from the fort under Claude-Jean Allouez (1684–88) and then Jacques Gravier (1688–1691). [3, 6]

La Salle himself left in late summer 1683, his trade patent expiring, to seek its renewal in France. Tonti held command, interrupted by Louis-Henri de Baugy from September 1683 to the spring of 1685. La Salle never returned to Illinois; he was killed by his own men in present-day Texas in 1687. Under Tonti the fort remained a warehouse, trading post and diplomatic center, and a base of French and Illinois resistance to Iroquois pressure. In 1691, as the Kaskaskia and Peoria left for Lake Peoria, the French abandoned Fort St. Louis on the rock and re-established downstream. The nomination notes brief later use — Pierre Deliette as commandant in 1714–18 — before permanent abandonment when the Illinois Country passed from Canada to Louisiana jurisdiction in 1718. No visible remains of the seventeenth-century fort survive on the summit today. [3, 4, 6]

**Lore — kept separate from the documented record above.** The name Starved Rock comes from a later telling, not from a contemporary record of the fort years. The telling exists in several forms, and the forms do not agree — see Gaps and conflicts for the variants in full. In its best-known form, carried by the Illinois Historic Preservation timeline, the Ottawa chief Pontiac was killed at Cahokia in 1769 by a member of the Peoria, and Ottawa and Potawatomi parties then sought retribution against the Illini, who fled to the butte, were trapped there without food or water, and starved. The Illinois State Museum gives a shorter, different form: about 1770, Peoria people under attack by a Potawatomi war party were trapped on the rock and suffered starvation. A modern scholarly reading cited by the Foundation for Illinois Colonial and American Studies traces the name instead to early nineteenth-century stories that grew and were embellished around a 1722 Mesquakie siege of the Peoria. This dossier labels all of these as lore: the telling is sourced, the event as told is not established by a contemporary written record in the sources consulted, and no version of it is presented here as documented fact. The people named in the telling — Peoria, Kaskaskia and the wider Illinois Confederacy, Ottawa and Potawatomi — are treated with the same dignity in the lore as in the record. [4, 5, 6]

The ground's later life is shorter to tell. After a failed nineteenth-century promotion of the rock as a western stronghold site, the bluff was developed as a vacation resort with a hotel, dance pavilion and pool. The State of Illinois purchased Starved Rock and adjacent acreage in 1911 to create a state park; the park's own lodge history states the park opened to the public in 1911. The Civilian Conservation Corps began building Starved Rock Lodge and its cabins in 1933; the lodge, designed by Joseph F. Booten, opened in 1939 and was listed on the National Register in May 1985 as Starved Rock Lodge and Cabins, NRIS 85002702. The rock itself — site 11-Ls-12, site of Fort St. Louis / Le Rocher — carries NRIS 66000325, with periods of significance 1650–1699 and significant years 1683, 1685 and 1691. [2, 7, 8, 10]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The summit of the sandstone butte on the south bank of the Illinois River in present-day Starved Rock State Park, LaSalle County, vicinity of Utica, held Fort St. Louis in 1683–1691. The Grand Village of the Kaskaskia stood across the river on the north bank, east of the rock; the two National Historic Landmark sites, Starved Rock and Old Kaskaskia Village (the Zimmerman site), lie about a mile apart on opposite banks and were occupied through much of the same 1673–1691 period. The nomination boundary for Starved Rock encloses the rock formation and a small setting of park land around it, about 25 acres, in the NW¼ of the NW¼ of Section 22, Township 33 North, Range 2 East. [3, 9]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Starved Rock State Park Visitor Center, mailing address PO Box 509, Utica, IL 61373, phone 815-667-4726, open 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. daily except listed state holidays. Use the visitor center first for exhibits, orientation and current trail conditions, then signed park trails only to the summit walkway and to Starved Rock Lodge at One Lodge Lane, Oglesby, IL 61348. No street address for the visitor center is published on the IDNR park page consulted; do not navigate by a lodge, campground or third-party address as if it were the visitor center's. Keep off-trail canyon walls, closed trails, the riverbank outside signed access, and all archaeological ground outside the public walkway outside the visit. The Grand Village / Zimmerman site across the river is documentary ground in this dossier; no public anchor for it is established here. [1, 3, 8]

## 5. Public progression

Begin inside the visitor center during staffed hours. Read the exhibits with two dates held apart: 1682–83, when the fort was built, and the later, undated-in-contemporary-record telling that gave the rock its present name. Take a current trail map and the day's closure list before walking — in 2026 that check is not optional. Follow signed trails to the summit. On top, there is no fort to find: what survives is the place itself, the flat summit inside its walkway, the river below, and the north bank opposite where the Grand Village stood. Read La Salle's palisade description there if you brought it, and let the absence of fabric carry the scale of what a wooden post on this rim once meant. **Strongest story moment · The empty summit.** A post that was meant to anchor French administration of the Mississippi Valley, built in one winter across from a village of thousands, left no visible timber, stone or earthwork on its own rock — the ground holds the story, the buildings do not. Continue on signed trails to the lodge to close the arc: a CCC-built public lodge of the 1930s, itself now on the National Register, is how this ground learned to receive visitors. Keep the Indigenous history of this river — village, displacement, return and the later lore — in quiet reflection throughout; nothing on this progression is scored or gamified. [1–10]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Visitor center street address — not published by IDNR; conflicting street addresses circulate.** The IDNR park page consulted gives only a mailing address, PO Box 509, Utica, IL 61373, and phone 815-667-4726, with no street address for the visitor center. The lodge, a separate facility, publishes One Lodge Lane, Oglesby, IL 61348 (GPS 41.3191202, −88.996302) and mailing PO Box 570, Utica, IL 61373. Other listings encountered during research gave the visitor center as 2678 East 873 Road, Oglesby, and the park as 2668 E. 950th Road, Oglesby; neither figure comes from the IDNR park page, and neither is adopted here. Navigate by IDNR park signage from Routes 178 and 71 and confirm the street address at the visitor center desk. [1, 8]
- **Height of the rock — three figures in the primary sources.** The National Register nomination gives about 150 feet above the river. The Illinois State Museum gives 38 metres, about 125 feet. The state park leaflet and the Foundation for Illinois Colonial and American Studies article both give 125 feet. This dossier prints all three and adopts none as a single verified height; the difference may reflect measurement point (river level versus base) but no source consulted states its datum. [3, 4, 6, 10]
- **Rock composition — one word differs.** The nomination describes St. Peter sandstone capped by coal measures and calls the formation sandstone throughout. The FICAS article opens by calling the bluff limestone, then treats the same French history without revisiting the term. The nomination and the park leaflet agree on St. Peter sandstone; the limestone wording is printed here as an unresolved conflict in a secondary scholarly summary, not adopted. [3, 6, 10]
- **What happened after 1691 — abandonment, reoccupation and fire do not line up into one account.** The nomination records abandonment by Tonti in 1691, possible brief use by Pierre Deliette in 1714–18, then decay and permanent abandonment in 1718. The Illinois State Museum records French abandonment in 1691 followed by reoccupation by the Peoria in the early 1700s, and dates historic burials on the summit to a Peoria occupation of 1711–1722. The FICAS account has some Peoria returning by 1712 and remaining, with a southern Illinois absence in 1722–1730, until about 1741. The state park leaflet states the fort was used as a refuge by traders and trappers until it was destroyed by fire around 1720. These accounts overlap but are not the same; no single post-1691 chronology is adopted here. [3, 4, 6, 10]
- **The Starved Rock telling — lore in at least three incompatible forms; no contemporary record produced.** (a) Illinois Historic Preservation timeline, dated 1769: Pontiac is killed at Cahokia by a Peoria member; Ottawa and Potawatomi seek retribution against the Illini confederacy; the Illini flee to the butte, are trapped and starve to death; the Fort St. Louis site becomes known as Starved Rock — the timeline itself heads the entry “According to legend.” (b) Illinois State Museum: an event that “allegedly” took place about 1770; Peoria people attacked by a Potawatomi war party suffer starvation trapped on the rock — no Pontiac, no Ottawa, no date of 1769. (c) National Register nomination, dated only to the 1760s: a group of Illini take refuge from an Ottawa–Potawatomi attack, are surrounded and “starved into submission” — submission, not death. (d) FICAS / Walczynski reading: the name grew from early nineteenth-century stories embellishing a 1722 Mesquakie siege of the Peoria. The state park leaflet gives form (a) with a 1760s origin date. No source consulted produces a contemporary written record of a siege on this summit. All forms are filed as lore. [3, 4, 5, 6, 10]
- **Register dates — designation and publication are different numbers and are often merged.** The NPGallery record for NRIS 66000325 shows Date Published 10/15/1966. The National Archives copy of the landmark file is titled with the date 10/09/1960, and the 1984 nomination form prepared by Margaret Kimball Brown updates an earlier National Historic Landmark inventory (Francine Weiss, 1974; Blanche Schroer, 1977), citing a National Survey of Historic Sites determination dated 6/29/58. This dossier records 1960 as the landmark-file date and 1966 as the NRIS publication date and does not collapse them into one “listed” date. Separately, IDNR's cultural resource list dates the Starved Rock (landmark) nomination work to 1984, which is the Brown form's preparation date, not a designation date. [2, 3, 9]
- **Village size and distance figures carry their sources' uncertainty.** The nomination gives the Grand Village as 73 cabins in 1673 and 400–550 cabins / 6,000–9,000 people in 1680, then about 300 cabins after the 1683 return, and flags an error in its own key quotation — La Salle's distance below the village reads six leagues in Margry but half a league in the Library of Congress transcript, which the nomination calls the correct reading. Cabin and population figures are printed as the nomination's estimates, not as counts. [3, 9]
- **Trail mileage and park figures are dated.** The 1986 park leaflet reproduced online gives 2,630 acres, a 582-acre nature preserve to the west, 18 canyons and 20 miles of trails. Current IDNR material consulted confirms the park, lodge and visitor center operation and the 2026 trail project but does not restate acreage or mileage in the pages opened; the leaflet figures are therefore dated, not current-verified. No current trail mileage is adopted. [1, 10]
- **Summit coordinates not adopted.** The lodge publishes GPS coordinates for itself (41.3191202, −88.996302); those are the lodge's, not the summit's. The nomination's UTM references survive in the scanned form in partly illegible form. No coordinate pair for the summit itself was verified from a clean primary source in this research, so the Locator leaves the summit latitude and longitude blank rather than borrowing the lodge's. [3, 8]
