# A county name in steel and the family history behind it

## 2. Story

At the Legacy Museum, the first piece of evidence is the address. EJI identifies the ground as the site of a cotton warehouse where enslaved Black people labored. Its research traces a different kind of traffic along nearby Commerce Street. Traders took people arriving by boat or rail up the street to depots or to auction near the Artesian Basin in present-day Court Square. One advertisement offered about 140 people for auction there on June 2, 1860. The advertisement tells us what the seller offered. It gives no account of what the people being sold said to their families that morning. [3, 4, pp. 33–35]

The museum and the memorial opened in April 2018. The expanded Legacy Museum opened on October 1, 2021. Today the museum uses first-person testimony and exhibits on slavery, racial terror, segregation and incarceration. At the separate six-acre memorial, EJI has given each county where it documented a racial terror lynching a steel monument. It commemorates more than 4,400 Black people killed between 1877 and 1950. The county is the organizing unit because the violence took place among neighbors, officials and witnesses in identifiable communities. [3, 5–7]

One such community was Henry County, Alabama. In February 1937 a mob took Wesley “Wes” Johnson, a Black teenager, from the county jail in Abbeville and killed him. EJI records the accusation made against him and his consensual relationship with a white woman. Two grand juries returned no indictments. For decades his cousin Faye Walker-Howell researched what had happened. She traced the actions of John Harper Oates, who tried to shelter Johnson and later helped his family bury him. Walker-Howell hosted the dedication of a marker near Johnson’s burial place. Another descendant, James Johnson, spoke of what it meant for the family to see that work completed. Their testimony restores a life and a family to a county record. EJI’s account and its film of a relative telling the story are available to read and watch from home. [8]

## 3. Then and now

Court Square documents a sale site. The museum stands on the former cotton-warehouse ground. The memorial is a later work built to commemorate people killed in counties across the country. Wes Johnson’s detention, murder, burial and community commemoration took place in Henry County. These places each answer a different historical question. [3–5, 8]

## 5. Reflection progression

At the museum, give a person’s account time before moving to the next exhibit. At the memorial, a county name provides a way back to that person’s community. Walker-Howell’s account is the strongest connection between the two places. Where a court record ends with no indictment, the family record continues with who helped, who remembered and who spoke in public. [3, 5, 8]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The operator publishes both 5 p.m. and 6 p.m. closing times. The visit page gives site-specific last admissions. Same-day ticket availability varies. The surviving advertisement establishes an offer for sale. The identities and later lives of the people in that advertised group were not established here. No county monument transcription for Johnson was independently verified, so his family’s account stands on its own. Separate annual visitor counts for these two sites were not established from an operator series. Coordinates remain blank for both entrances. [1, 2, 4, 5, 8]

## 7. Historical photograph

**Court Square, Montgomery, Alabama, circa 1909.** I inspected the Library of Congress image. A fountain rises behind the streetcar tracks, with pedestrians, wagons and commercial storefronts around the square. It shows the later streetscape at the documented market ground. It dates decades after emancipation and shows neither an auction nor either present-day Legacy site. Use only alongside that historical passage. [4, 9]
