# Minidoka National Historic Site

## 1. Trip gate

The National Park Service lists fall and winter closure for the visitor center at 1428 Hunt Road, Jerome. Friends of Minidoka records its final day of the 2026 season as September 27. When the building is closed, the restrooms close too. The park supplies brochures outside and keeps the designated walking route open from sunrise to sunset. [1–3,12,15]

The published walking route is 1.6 miles. Pets and recreational vehicles are prohibited on it; service animals and disability mobility scooters are allowed. [3] Allow roughly 60–90 minutes for a considered exterior stop as a planning allowance, not a timed accessible-route guarantee. Choose ample daylight, shorten or abandon the walk for unsafe winter surfaces, and arrange restroom/water stops beforehand.

NPS directs drivers via US93, Highway25 and Hunt Road; it identifies a small parking area by the guard tower and a larger lot at the center. Historical features also lie on neighboring private property: they are not public destinations. [2] The official conditions page links Idaho511 for road checks. No actual road-clearance, plowing or live weather check was completed; a published route is not evidence that it is passable today. [1]

## 2. The story

The federal government forcibly removed people of Japanese ancestry and incarcerated them without due process during World War II. More than 13,000 passed through Minidoka. [4] The setting mattered: the isolation was part of the institution, not merely a scenic backdrop. NPS describes hurried departures, economic loss and a remote site selected by the War Relocation Authority. [5]

Minidoka operated August 10, 1942–October 28, 1945. Densho describes people brought from Washington, Oregon and Alaska, including some Bainbridge Islanders transferred from Manzanar. Its peak count of 9,397 is not the cumulative number confined over three years. [6]

**Named primary voice:** Frank Yamasaki, interviewed by Lori Hoshino and Stephen Fugita at Lake Forest Park, Washington, August 18, 1997, remembered: “I gradually got used to the mixture of sand and food.” [7] His recollection also describes his family of five sharing a small room and the work of experienced cooks. These memories restore ordinary bodily experience to a landscape that can otherwise appear empty. They are one person's retrospective testimony, not measurements of every barrack.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a large, organized confinement landscape containing residential blocks, administrative functions and agricultural land, not simply a row of barracks. After closure, buildings and land were dispersed; some land went to veterans through postwar homesteading lotteries. [5]

**Now:** surviving traces and deliberate interpretation must be read together. NPS identifies a returned original barrack, a replicated Honor Roll and reconstructed fence. Those are three different kinds of evidence, not interchangeable claims of untouched wartime fabric. [4] The current center occupies former warehouse #5, used as an automotive repair shop during the camp period. [3]

Interpretive recommendation: the apparent emptiness should prompt the question of what was removed, rather than a claim that little happened here. A visitor's short walk cannot reproduce the confinement of families who did not choose to come.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Proposed sequence, subject to current signs, weather and access:

1. Orient at the visitor-center parking area. If closed, use the outside map and decide how much daylight and safe walking conditions remain.
2. Read one family or incarceree account before walking. Carry the Yamasaki excerpt as testimony, not as an acting prompt.
3. Follow only the signed route and distinguish original remnants, returned buildings and reconstructions as labels allow. Leave uncertain identifications unresolved.
4. At the entrance interpretation, consider how roads, boundaries and observation changed ordinary movement into controlled movement. Do not approach a canal edge or climb a reconstruction for perspective.
5. Finish at a quiet place on the designated path. In his 1997 interview, Frank Yamasaki recalled the grit that reached the food in the mess hall. Read his words in section 2, or listen to Densho’s segment. Leave time for silence. [7,8] **Strongest story moment.**

No role-play, confinement simulation or timed challenge. Descendants and other visitors may be grieving or remembering family. Allow silence and the option to stop.

- The 2026 closing date is documented. The NPS seasonal-hours block and its reopening prose give different spring boundaries. The next public opening date stays blank pending a dated NPS notice. [1,3,12,15]
- Densho's peak population 9,397 and the NPS landscape article's 9,500 are different published figures; no reconciliation attempted. The NPS cumulative figure exceeds 13,000. Do not substitute peak for cumulative population. [4][5][6]
- NPS register metadata gives listing July 10, 1979; search extraction of the old scanned nomination rendered its entry stamp differently. Use the metadata for catalog date, not uncertain OCR. [9]
- Original camp boundaries, the 1979 registered parcel and today's visitor-access area are not interchangeable.
- No same-day road report, snow/ice inspection, field accessibility audit, verified camera station or modern repeat-photo pair.
- This entry documents a named voice; it does not claim that one voice represents all incarcerees or all responses to military service and resistance.
