## 2. The story

**Original narration:** Thermopolis can be introduced through two kinds of work: the work of making a town function, and the work of deciding how its beginnings are remembered. Neither is adequately told by a miraculous-water slogan. Read the land document before treating a later celebration as a transcript; hear an ordinary worker before treating a townscape as a self-operating machine.

The **Eastern Shoshone Tribe's own history** describes a homeland continuing from time immemorial and places the Big Horn Hot Springs cession among subsequent reductions of reservation land. That is a living Nation's account, not a generic statement assigned to all Native people. Northern Arapaho history and sovereignty remain distinct; the cession document names both peoples, while the Tribe's page speaks for the Eastern Shoshone. [2]

The 1896 agreement, reproduced by the Jackson Hole historical institution, dates the negotiation **April 21, 1896**, and prints the congressional approval **June 7, 1897**. Article II promises $60,000; Article III distinguishes cash and cattle arrangements. The accompanying enacted text transfers a smaller parcel to Wyoming. These are a negotiated cession and a subsequent governmental action, not a single timeless gift. This is a published transcription with historical commentary, not inspection of the signed original. [3]

Keep **Fort Bridger, July 3, 1868**, separate. The Eastern Shoshone website reproduces that earlier agreement with the Eastern Shoshone and Bannock and its reservation provisions. It is not the 1896 agreement, and neither should be renamed the Fort Laramie treaty. This dossier makes no present-day jurisdictional determination. [4]

The named community voice is **Caroline Fuller**, interviewed by **May Gillies on June 14, 1973**, for the Hot Springs County Historical Society. WyoHistory publishes the transcript from Wyoming State Archives holdings. Discussing her Thermopolis county-hospital work, Fuller recalled bookkeeping and arranging sufficient nursing staff, then said: **“All new work to me.”** Her recollection gives public history an unglamorous center: care also depended on administration. It is retrospective testimony, not a contemporary hospital ledger, and it does not establish any therapeutic benefit from spring water. No recording was played. [5]

**Interpretive payoff:** A visitor can admire a place while refusing to flatten the terms by which it became a destination. A worker's remembered learning and a land agreement's written obligations both ask us to look beyond the postcard.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The historical article's “10 square miles—64,000 acres” is internally inconsistent. The institution's cession introduction says ten miles square, or 100 square miles. Do not propagate the smaller area or use either as a visitor boundary; this dossier supplies no cession polygon. [3][8]
- Fuller's editorial introduction associates Thermopolis with 1905, while the interviewer's later question dates her move to town to 1920. Those statements are not silently reconciled. Her Lost Cabin experiences must not all be moved into Thermopolis. [5]
- The Tribe's treaty transcription prints proclamation before ratification in February 1869. The July 1868 agreement date is the distinction used here; the later administrative dates require independent checking. [4]
- No current Northern Arapaho institutional account specifically interpreting this transaction was successfully incorporated. Neither a non-Native historian nor the Eastern Shoshone page substitutes for it.
