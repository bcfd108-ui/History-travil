## 2. The story

**A named worker, through a documented quotation.** The state-hosted study *Coal Mining in the Coal-Bearing Region of North Dakota, 1870–1945*, prepared by **Jeffrey A. Hess, Robert Hybben and William Casey**, September 1992, quotes former employee **George Moorman** on printed page **36**. Footnote 71 identifies an interview of **November 12, 1983**, held with a cultural-resource survey form at the State Historical Society. The quotation and citation were directly read. The original interview and audio were not retrieved. [4]

**His observation concerns people.** Moorman remembered a very small workforce with roles that shifted when another man was absent: **“One guy was the boss and the other was the helper.”** This is a recollection about employment relationships at one operation, not a statewide rule or permission to reenact mining work. [4]

**Households beyond the wage earner.** The state education account describes Wilton’s company boarding house, the Beanery, where local women and girls cooked and cleaned. It also notes miners who combined farming with seasonal employment and immigrants waiting for families to join them. Those are institutional descriptions; no named Beanery worker’s own testimony was recovered. [5]

**Original interpretation.** Coal households depended on linked forms of work: a wage, a bed, meals, cleaning and care. Calling the miner the only worker erases the labor that supported his day. The story is strongest when it keeps the domestic evidence gap visible rather than inventing a cook’s feelings or family routine.

## 3. Then / now

**Keep places distinct.** Moorman’s remembered small operation and the larger Wilton company town are separate settings. They are paired to show variation, not presented as one household, one employer or one labor contract. The archive is a modern research anchor, not a surviving workplace from either account.

**Pay claims deserve context.** The education account names **Nick Sologuk** and reports his recollection of earning **$2 per day in 1919**. It cautions that a local newspaper’s more generous wage picture came from an editor related to management. Neither account is a payroll ledger examined here; no average household income is calculated from them. [5]

**No uncomplicated company-town nostalgia.** Employer services could benefit families while also increasing dependence. The same educational account discusses paternalism and efforts to discourage unionization. Its favorable language about the employer is an interpretation to examine, not the dossier’s verdict on every worker’s experience. [5]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Start with the worker’s small sentence.** Read Moorman’s statement as a memory of relationships and responsibilities. Retain its interview date and the fact that it reaches us through a later report.

**Prepare for a public research visit only if useful.** Confirm the reading room’s opening and the material required. Follow staff retrieval procedures. No visitor needs to travel to a former operation to understand the evidence.

**Add the household layer.** Read the institution’s account of boarding-house labor beside the worker quotation. The absence of a named cook is meaningful: the next research need is a person’s voice, not an imagined scene.

**End in recognition, not spectacle.** The strongest story moment is that home comfort depended on work distributed across a community. Acknowledge the workers and families without dramatizing danger or treating hardship as a challenge to complete.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**Primary-source depth is qualified.** A directly examined quotation of Moorman’s interview is not a directly examined complete transcript. The study’s citation permits further verification but does not prove the full interview survives, is open or may be reused.

**Household voice remains missing.** The Beanery description and Sologuk wage statement were read in a state educational synthesis. No named spouse, cook, cleaner or child’s complete firsthand account was obtained. This is not a completed oral-history recovery.

**Different historical dates:** the study is from 1992; the interview is from 1983 and recalls earlier employment; the Wilton wage claim concerns 1919. None describes current working conditions or current compensation.

**No industrial survival inference.** A history page or preservation survey does not establish safe ground, accessible structures or public ownership today. No mine survey identifier is converted into a visitor pin.

**Remaining practical gaps:** day-specific winter hours, disability accommodation, original interview retrieval, image rights and a current local museum gate. No exhibit, reservation or operational access has been confirmed.
