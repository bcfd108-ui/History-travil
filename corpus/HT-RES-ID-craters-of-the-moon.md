## 2. The story

The central historical question is not who “discovered” an empty lava field. It is how people have understood, traveled through, represented and protected a place already within a living homeland. The most useful current public interpretation is the nine waysides developed jointly by NPS and the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes at Devils Orchard. In the 2024 dedication release, Nolan Brown of the Tribes' Language and Cultural Preservation Department described their purpose as showing visitors **“who we are and where they are.”** That is an attributed Tribal voice published by NPS, not a new interview or permission to reproduce ceremonial accounts. The release explains enduring homeland connections and travel for food gathering; it is a better starting point than repeating older assertions that everyone avoided the lava. [5]

The visitor then encounters a different kind of journey: Robert Limbert's effort to turn a difficult landscape into a destination. NPS's biography records his 1920 traverse with W. L. Cole: seventeen days, approximately eighty miles, heavy equipment and a camera. His dog's injured feet became a practical reminder that a line on a map is not an easy route over broken lava. Limbert followed existing human and animal routes and photographed the terrain, then brought civic and scientific visitors and published illustrated accounts. His own promotional phrase, reproduced by NPS, anticipated visitors **“from all America.”** This is evidence of ambition, not proof that his experiences were the first human encounters here. [6]

The original article is catalogued as *Among the “Craters of the Moon”*, March 1924, *National Geographic* 45:303–328, NPS DataStore reference **7785**. Catalog metadata verified; full article not read in this pass. Its title's “first expeditions” is retained as a historical title, not adopted as the dossier's claim. [7]

The public monument dates to **2 May 1924**, Presidential Proclamation **1694**, recorded at **43 Stat. 1947**. The statute compilation also documents later boundary and designation actions, including the 2000 proclamation and 2002 legislation. “Founded in 1924” therefore describes the initial federal designation, not the age of the landscape or the date its entire present extent became protected. [8]

A second transformation was the creation of a visitor system: roads, facilities and interpretation made a physically demanding landscape legible to people who would never undertake Limbert's expedition. A state-hosted nomination identifies the **Craters of the Moon National Monument Mission 66 Historic District**, reference **100006698**. This is a located archival lead, not a full nomination read: the PDF timed out. Do not yet attach unverified architect names, building dates or contributing-resource counts to individual visitor-center features. [9]

Finally, the apparent lunar landscape entered the space-age imagination. NPS distinguishes a 1969 astronaut geology visit from a blanket claim that astronauts “trained for the moon here.” Their study of volcanic terrain was a particular activity, not evidence that these formations reproduce every lunar process. [10]

## 3. Then / now

Then, Limbert's camera and prose helped an audience imagine a hard-to-reach landscape; now, road access and interpretive trails organize that encounter. The contrast is an original interpretive reading of the expedition account and current visitor plan, not a claim that every modern stop reproduces his route. [6,3]

The land is not a single extinct cone with one summit crater. NPS's brochure describes lava issuing along fissures of the Great Rift. The visitor sees different volcanic landforms, not the remains of a single giant mountain. Its geological time is not interchangeable with the monument's institutional chronology. [11]

The change in interpretation is tangible too: a contemporary visitor can encounter Tribal perspectives through a formally co-developed public trail, not only the explorer narrative. Do not use “then Indigenous, now national park” as though Indigenous relationships ended with designation. [5]

## 4. What's still there

- **Robert Limbert Visitor Center:** museum/orientation destination and current-mode fallback. The operator calls it the first stop. Exact opening hours were not exposed in this retrieval. Interior exhibits should be checked on arrival rather than advertised by an unverified inventory. [4]
- **Seven-mile Loop Road:** the developed sightseeing spine, not a year-round vehicle guarantee or a seven-mile walking assignment. The ordinary winter vehicle closure window is seasonal guidance. [3]
- **Devils Orchard:** paved half-mile loop through cinder beds, broken volcanic material and vegetation, with public interpretation. NPS estimates **30–45 minutes**. Although called accessible, its detailed profile reports portions steeper than a standard ramp; inspect the actual grades and surface conditions rather than promising suitability for every visitor or snow condition. [12]
- **Spatter Cone trail / Inferno Cone view:** identifiable features pictured by NPS, but no November access or physical capability guarantee. The public photo captions establish the feature, not a verified exact historic camera station. [13]
- **Caves:** no current access. Even in open season, only the specifically permitted caves may be entered with the required in-person cave permit; a park entrance pass is not that permit. [1,2]

Keep every visit on authorized routes. No collecting rocks, moving stones, entering archaeological features or finding a route around closure barriers. The compendium explicitly restricts resource disturbance and off-trail travel in named areas. [2]
