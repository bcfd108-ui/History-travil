## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** Care work was a livelihood, a family responsibility and a demand that could interrupt the rest of a working day. It belongs in labor history, not only in a medical chronology.

**Named published first-person excerpt:** Santa Fe Living Treasures' profile of **Doña Jesusita Aragón**, honored in **July 1987**, directly attributes her words: **“I wanted to go to school to be a nurse”**. The community organization's published profile was read, not a full interview recording. It describes a northern New Mexico midwife who also supported her family through domestic and other paid work. [2]

The same profile attributes her early learning to her grandmother **Dolores Gallegos** and reports a move to Las Vegas in **1942**. These are the profile's historical account and attributed recollections, not independently checked employment or education records. No practical methods, clinical cases or home locator are carried forward. [2]

**A later labor-history record, clearly separate:** UNM's *Working People's History of New Mexico* catalog describes **Coral Pitkin**, whose work in Taos included nursing and midwifery apprenticeship in the late 1970s. It identifies her 1978 union presidency and an effort to improve pay, benefits and working conditions. The item is credited to **Diane Pinkey and Coral Pitkin**, published **December 15, 2021**. Only repository description and rights metadata were read; the video was not watched, so no direct Pitkin quotation is invented. [3]

**Original interpretation:** Aragón's ambition and Pitkin's labor-history record invite a shift from romanticizing self-sacrifice to recognizing workers' time and support needs. They are distinct people and generations, not interchangeable representatives of a single tradition.

## 3. Then / now

The public record preserves more than professional milestones: it can reveal aspirations, overlapping jobs and the effort required to sustain care. That is the historical comparison here, not a recommendation for present practice.

The independent library offers a place to study history without entering a health-care environment. No present clinician, patient or family needs to become part of the visitor experience. Published recollection is enough for this initial entry; restricted records are neither sought nor needed.

Traditional midwifery, licensed nursing and union history should not be collapsed into a universal identity. Where the sources name a role, retain that attribution and its period. No current license or scope-of-practice conclusion is made.
