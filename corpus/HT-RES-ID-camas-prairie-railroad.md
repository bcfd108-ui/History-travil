## 2. The story

The Second Subdivision was an engineering response to elevation rather than a direct line drawn across a map. HAER documentation identifies its 1908 construction and a climb from roughly 800 feet near the Clearwater to above 3,700 feet on the prairie. Tunnels and major timber structures concentrated on the grade made the connection possible. Its recorded structure counts describe the documentation period, not today's inventory. [3]

**Named primary voice:** in a February 3, 1975 oral-history interview, **Lottie Rowan** remembered Denver, Idaho, losing out when the railway bypassed it: “But they didn't take it through Denver.” Frank Rowan qualified the explanation by pointing to competing nearby towns. Their exchange is useful precisely because memory contains argument, not a neat engineering verdict. Transcript read; audio not played. It does not prove a surveyed rejected alignment. [4]

**Contemporary operating record:** a November 25, 1925 bulletin, signed **Wm. McMurray, General Passenger Agent**, announced changes effective November 29. The catalog transcribes train 344 leaving Lewiston at 3:15 p.m. and reaching Grangeville at 7:10 p.m.; train 343 departed Grangeville at 7:40 a.m. and reached Lewiston at 11:10 a.m. This is a dated service instruction, not proof that a particular train ran on time. [5]

Together these records suggest the strongest story: railway access reorganized time and opportunity. A timetable could bring Grangeville into a wider network while another settlement remembered being left outside it. Neither record turns the surviving grade into a visitor route.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** HAER records the last Grangeville freight departure on November 28, 2000 with four canola cars and three empty tankers, followed by collection of cars farther along the subdivision the next day. This is the historian's sourced account, not a consist sheet independently inspected here. [3]

**Now:** the historic corporate system and current businesses are not interchangeable. Watco's current Great Northwest Railroad page describes an operating freight business, 103.48 track miles and interchange at Ayer Junction. That establishes active regional freight operations, **not a reopened Grangeville passenger arm**. Exact present operating limits and ownership of every Second Subdivision segment were not established. Do not label all remnants inactive or all arms open. [6]

Prairie history also predates the railway. NPS explains camas gathering and horse forage, then land loss through allotment and conversion to agriculture. Railroad prosperity cannot be narrated as though the wheat landscape had always been empty land awaiting transport. A tribal-authored local railroad account remains a gap. [7]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Archive-first proposal, with only a conditional landscape pause:**

1. Start with the historical subdivision map or HAER description and separate the Grangeville arm from Lewiston's depot and the other branches.
2. Read the 1925 service instruction. Ask what a precisely printed arrival time promised to a family, farmer or business.
3. Compare Lottie and Frank Rowan's recollections of Denver. Keep their different explanations visible rather than presenting a single cause of town decline.
4. If the published public pullout is open and safe, view the prairie from that visitor area only. Otherwise remain with the digital record. Do not leave the pullout searching for track.
5. End with the contrast: an engineering system that connected one town could redirect commerce away from another, on a landscape already transformed by loss of Indigenous land. The story works without physically entering the railway.

No inter-stop drive time, trestle circuit or winter backroad excursion is supplied. This is not a Route Group.
