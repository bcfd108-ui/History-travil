# Houston Ship Channel — The opening ceremony and the work of a deep-water port
Research draft · Texas OP07 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

**An opening you could hear.** The November 10, 1914 Houston Chronicle put “WITH BOOM OF CANNON” over its announcement of the opening. A reproduction of the front page appears in a Rice University project document. The edition describes decorated ships and a crowd lining the water. It also makes the ceremonial action easy to remember, putting President Wilson and his button in the main headline. Opening day could be staged in a few hours. Making the river take ocean-going ships was a different kind of work. [3]

## 3 · Then / now

**Then — a basin that could turn ships.** Port Houston’s engineering history traces a shallow surveyed channel in the 1870s, a federal deepening project that included the Turning Basin before 1908, and a 25-foot by 150-foot project completed in the 1912–1914 period. An early commercial postcard presented the Turning Basin as a marvel whose waterway had been made by deepening and widening an existing bed. The U.S. Army Corps and port expansion material describe subsequent deepening, including the 30-foot project in 1925. The opening in the paper belongs to that chain of work. [3–5]

**Now — ships still have to get past one another.** The Houston Ship Channel is an active waterway with restricted terminals. The county-operated Lynchburg Ferry crosses downstream of the San Jacinto River and Buffalo Bayou confluence. It gives travelers a public, at-water-level encounter with the channel. The opening ceremony belongs to the upstream Turning Basin and can be read separately. The Port Houston tour page describes current cargo operations, while the LOC catalogs longshoreman Michael Dickens’s first-person remembrance of gang hiring and union mergers. The direct item and transcript returned HTTP 403 in this pass. [1–2,6–7]

## 4 · What’s still there

- **Public vehicle and passenger route when operating.** County Lynchburg Ferry between signed landings. Boarding, waiting and getting off follow staff directions. [1–2]
- **View from public ground or public ferry service.** The channel at the crossing. The ride and the landing are subject to operator closures. [1–2]
- **Not accessible.** No working dock or landside Turning Basin entry under this dossier. Port Houston also describes a public reservation-required boat tour, which is separate from this drive-up plan. [6]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Historic location.** Houston’s 1914 opening is associated with the Turning Basin. The historic channel extends beyond both the opening ground and the ferry crossing. [3–5]
2. **Lawful anchor.** Use the signed Lynchburg Ferry north landing, at the county’s published Lynchburg approach, when county service is operating. Follow the staged route. If the ferry is closed, read remotely. [1–2]
3. **Strongest story moment — Two vessels using the same water.** If a ship is passing while you are on the public ferry, notice the difference in scale between the two boats using the channel. The 1914 headline marks its ceremonial opening. The water in front of you shows what a channel had to make possible. [1–4]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The original Chronicle issue was consulted as a reproduced front-page scan in [3]. The full issue and original opening correspondence were not retrieved. Port Houston dates the opening for ocean-going vessels to November 1914 [4]; the newspaper gives the ceremony November 10 [3]. Neither source makes the 1914 ceremony the first navigation of Buffalo Bayou. LOC item 2016655444 indexed a transcript of Dickens’s 2011 interview, but the direct item returned HTTP 403. No words attributable to Dickens are quoted. [3–4,7]
