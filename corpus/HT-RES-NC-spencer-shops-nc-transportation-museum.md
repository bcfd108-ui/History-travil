## 2. Story

Southern Railway began building its new shops in 1896. Its first roundhouse took locomotives in for shorter repairs. The Back Shop, completed in 1905, took on work that needed more time and space. The museum’s account describes inspections that determined where a locomotive went. The place was a series of decisions about work, parts, time and space. The 1977 engineering survey documents the erecting and machine shop in plan, giving a way to connect the surviving building to decisions made by the people inside it. [2–4]

Edward “Dick” Bostian told interviewer Daniel Ellison in 1980 that he started as a laborer at nine cents an hour. His first roundhouse job was helping fasten pipe clamps after repairs. He moved into storage, where he helped check the stock monthly so used materials could be replaced. He gave June 1907 as his starting date and remembered more than fifty years with the company. His account makes a building that could otherwise look like a succession of large locomotives legible as a place where clamps, counts and a dependable paycheck mattered. These figures are his recollections, with payroll verification still open. [5]

John Samuel Upton said he started in 1911 and described an apprenticeship that rotated workers through departments and included instruction. He later worked in both the machine shop and the Back Shop. He described laying out jobs for the machines and taking on cylinder and valve work in the erecting shop. Read his explanation next to Bostian’s. Two workers describe different careers. Together they show how the size and layout of the complex gave an apprentice, a machinist and a storehouse employee different kinds of responsibility. [4–5]

## 3. Then and now

The first roundhouse and the later roundhouse are distinct buildings in the site’s chronology. The museum dates the surviving 37-stall roundhouse to 1924. It was equipped with a 100-foot turntable. The Back Shop and roundhouse are interpreted today within the historic railway complex. The Barber Junction depot is the modern starting point for a ticketed visit. It should not be read as the historic shop employees’ original entrance. [1–3, 6]

## 4. What survives and where a visitor may stand

- Barber Junction Visitor Center and signed museum guest paths to open indoor galleries. Use the admission-only option for this visit. Where events close or divert access, use the operator’s instructions. [1]
- Back Shop and Bob Julian Roundhouse exhibits, viewed from posted public areas. The historic turntable can be read from designated guest ground if open. The operating plant’s former work zones and any modern restricted track are outside this dossier. [1, 3, 6]
- The 1977 HAER plan is a documentary route through the building’s former functions. It is not permission to enter a work area. [4]

## 5. Strongest visit moment

Stand on the marked guest side of the Back Shop. Read Upton’s account of doing one job in two buildings and picture where the work moved. Then read Bostian’s account of counting parts each month. **The distance between a repair bay and its supply of small parts** explains why the complex needed more than men who could work on engines. [3–5]

## 6. Evidence limits and disagreements

The oral-history material is a museum-edited 1980 transcript series. Bostian’s recalled wages and Upton’s apprenticeship rates and dates remain attributed. Personnel and payroll ledgers were not independently opened. The state fee report puts FY2025 total museum visitation at 164,461. That total exceeds the sum of the two displayed fee categories. The reporting basis of the balance was not established, and the total does not establish attendance at the roundhouse door. The museum’s 2026 holiday schedule overrides generic daily information on the same page. [1, 5, 7]
