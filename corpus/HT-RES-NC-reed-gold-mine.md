# Reed Gold Mine and North Carolina’s First Gold Rush

**1. Trip gate — Research draft. Access checked November 21, 2026 Pacific. Start at the state historic site’s free visitor center, 9621 Reed Mine Road, Midland. Use the signed public lot and walk to the orientation desk. The operator posts Tuesday–Saturday, 9 a.m.–5 p.m., with Sunday, Monday and state-holiday closures. Entry to the historic mine is by staff-led tour only with an on-site ticket. The indoor collection is the anchor. Check current hours before travel. [1–2]**

## 2. Story

Two discoveries on the same farm put the beginnings of the North Carolina gold rush in different light. The state’s account places the first in 1799. Conrad Reed found a yellow rock in Little Meadow Creek, and the family kept it for several years before a Fayetteville jeweler recognized it as gold. In 1803, Conrad’s father John made a partnership with Frederick Kiser, Martin Phifer Jr. and the Rev. James Love. Reed supplied the ground. The partnership relied on equipment and enslaved labor furnished by the other men. Peter, enslaved by Love, found a nugget that season which the state estimates at 28 pounds. The free account gives Peter a first name and records his discovery. It gives a much fuller account of the men entitled to the proceeds. [3–5]

The early operation recovered gold from the creek deposits. The state timeline credits Reed’s grandson Isaac Crayton with sinking the first shaft at Reed in 1831. Shaft work and the processing of quartz made this a different enterprise from searching stream gravels. The remains of pits, shafts and mill foundations in the nomination record give these changes a physical trace. They also belong to different periods. A photograph of a tunnel can illustrate underground work here without dating that entrance or implying that Conrad or Peter worked that spot. [3–5, 8]

A federal mint table gives the regional stakes. A retrospective statement printed for Congress records $110,000 in North Carolina gold deposited in 1804–1827. In the table, North Carolina is the sole state with a deposit in that first period. That is the amount the table attributes to the state as a whole. It gives no way to assign the whole figure to Reed or to identify individual diggers. [6]

## 3. Then and now

The land retains traces of stream working, shafts and processing. Some of the underground workings are equipped for a guided heritage visit. The operator describes a ten-stamp mill erected at Reed by 1896. The mill visitors see interpreted at Reed today has a separate history. ASME documents machinery from the Coggins Mine in Montgomery County moved to Reed in 1974, then restored as a working example. Keep its provenance with the exhibit. [2–4, 7–8]

## 4. What survives and where a visitor may stand

- Use the state visitor center, its public parking and signed path. The center has gold and equipment exhibits and an orientation film according to the operator’s site history. The state describes the visitor center as accessible. [1–3, 5]
- For an underground visit, book at the orientation desk under the operator’s current rules and go with site staff. Check mobility needs and current conditions with the desk. Stay off closed or unsigned ground, inactive openings and mine workings outside the guided visit. [1–2]
- Use the named Linker Adit image as an illustration of the historic workings. The visitor anchor is the orientation desk and museum. [1–2, 8–10]

## 5. Strongest visit moment

Start with Peter’s name at the visitor center. Read how the partnership furnished the equipment and people that made the second discovery possible. Then compare a recovered nugget with the museum’s examples of equipment used to break gold-bearing quartz. One side of the visit shows who made an early discovery and who controlled it. The other shows how sustained mining changed the work. [3, 5, 7]

## 6. Evidence limits and disagreements

The 1799 discovery narrative and the details of the 1803 partnership come through state research and the landmark documentation. The original partnership paper, a direct account by Peter and any record of what happened to him after the find were not established. The early nugget measurements stay approximate. The operator also describes a separate 1896 find with competing attributions. Some descendants credit A.M. Cox with finding it. The operator credits Jacob Shinn with washing the rock and recognizing gold, and reports a photograph naming Cox. The original print and inscriptions were not inspected. Credit the group and leave exact discovery attribution open. The state fee report records 34,560 total visits to Reed in FY2025, including 15,021 fee-paid and 19,539 fee-waived. Those use a site-level denominator. The visitor-center door count remains unknown. [3–6, 11]
