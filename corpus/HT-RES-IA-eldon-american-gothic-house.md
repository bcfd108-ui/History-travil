# Eldon — The window that made it into the painting

Iowa OP03 · Research draft for Dash

## 2. Story

**A deed before the painting.** Eldon’s little house had mortgage and business history long before it became a famous backdrop. The federal nomination describes an 1881 transfer involving mortgage assumption and records a candy and novelty business in the Dibbles' history in 1897. The state interpretation gives 1887 as its first recorded Dibble ownership date [11]. Those details tell us what kinds of records remain to test. They also return the building to a small railroad town where people borrowed, traded and made a home. The original deed book pages are still held for verification. [2, 3]

**How Wood described the two people.** The Art Institute's 1930 painting used Grant Wood’s sister Nan and his dentist, Byron McKeeby, as models. The operator and museum identify the small Eldon building as the architectural source. In a 1941 letter Wood described the figures as “small town folks, rather than farmers.” The words come from a published letter transcription. The museum uses a farmer-and-daughter reading in its educational material. Wood's remark supplies a distinction worth hearing while the real upper window is in sight. His models and the resident household are separate. [4–6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The house in Eldon, now identified by the state at 301 American Gothic Street. The painted scene arranges human figures and architecture. It supplies evidence of artistic composition and the recognizability of the window, with no historic camera stance. [2–6]

**Lawful public anchor.** American Gothic House Center, 300 American Gothic Street, Eldon. Drive-up and short walk to the operator’s public exterior viewing area and paths. Present house, visitor center and gallery that owns the original painting are separately situated and managed. Use the grounds only when the posted exterior area is open. [1, 3, 6, 7]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Public exterior** — exterior of the house and its pointed upper window, viewed from the signed public grounds dawn to dusk. Avoid porch or door areas outside posted permission, and skip exterior on unsafe ice or weather. [1, 2, 13]
- **Public visitor-center interior** — posted schedule from the county operator, subject to holidays. House interior requires an advance private group arrangement, which is outside this itinerary. [1, 7]
- **Source only at Eldon** — the painting, Art Institute object 1930.934, and the artist’s letter. The work itself is in Chicago. [5, 6]

## 5. A short visit

Start with the small upper window from the public viewing area. In the open visitor center or through the museum’s remote record, look at where Wood put that window behind the figures. Land on **The window and the two townspeople**. Read Wood’s own phrase, then look at the little space the pointed window actually occupies above the door. The walk works as exterior-only when the center is closed and the grounds are safely open. [1, 4–7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The National Register nomination’s ownership passage uses 1939 for Wood’s visit. The Art Institute dates the work to 1930 and the operator describes Wood’s Eldon encounter in 1930. Do not turn the inconsistent 1939 statement into a second visit. [2, 4, 6]
- The state and the nomination name different record milestones for how the property passed to the Dibbles. The 1881 transaction and the state’s 1887 attribution are both retained. Deed pages and exact construction sequence were not retrieved. [2, 3]
- State and dedicated center pages differ on center hours and use two addresses. The 301 address is the house and the county’s 300 is the visitor center. The operator rules govern the ordinary itinerary, including the prearranged ten-person minimum for house interior and closure of the center on Thanksgiving and Black Friday. [1, 3, 7]
- Wood’s published letter transcription was read. The original letter manuscript and separate dated words from both models were not retrieved. [5]
