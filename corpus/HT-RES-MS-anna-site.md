## 2. Story

Anna Site is the place archaeologists used to define the earliest named period of the Plaquemine tradition in this part of the Lower Mississippi Valley. Its name in a textbook comes from a real place made and remade by Indigenous people. The redacted national nomination describes a group of earthen platforms around a plaza and separates the archaeological span called the Anna phase, approximately 1200–1350, from later building and occupation. Those terms organize material evidence. The sources reviewed have yet to recover the builders’ names or their name for the settlement. [1–2]

Mississippi Geological Survey archaeologist Calvin S. Brown visited in 1917. In his 1926 report, he described the visible forms, drew four earthworks and recorded two smaller ones he did not plot. His conclusion was brief: “This is a magnificent group of mounds, and should be preserved.” His page documents the state of the place at the time, before later federal and state surveys described a larger complex. It also reports intrusive digging and observations of human remains. That history belongs in an account of the records and their limits. It gives readers no reason to seek out the ground. [2–3]

John L. Cotter’s team examined deposits in 1948. Cotter published the study in 1951. Its stated research objective was to investigate “the cultural identity of the builders.” The nomination reports a deposit left by earlier occupation under four construction stages. A later restudy of the pottery placed the earliest work in the Anna phase and two later stages in the Foster phase. The evidence for activity around 1500 rests in part on a single diagnostic fragment. The places where Indigenous people lived, worked and rebuilt become visible here at different depths, while the researchers’ names are the ones that survive in print. [2,4]

The Natchez Nation describes its history on either side of the well-known contact period and writes of “a modern history that followed it.” That contemporary voice belongs alongside the archaeologists’ chronology. The Nation’s general statement does not identify Anna’s builders by name or make the restricted site a public place. [5]

## 3. Then and now

Once, a built plaza and earthen platforms organized a landscape people used and altered over generations. The federal nomination records the earthworks and changes from later agricultural use. The current official record protects the address. The documentation supplies a broad history of the site. Present condition and access at individual features remain unverified. [1–4]

## 5. Reading progression

Read Brown’s overview, then the nomination’s account of successive construction and the Natchez Nation’s own statement. **Strongest story moment — The old ground under the first platform.** A settlement surface remained beneath later construction. It gives the site a human timescale longer than the name of any archaeological phase. [2–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

Brown drew four earthworks and described two additional small ones. The nomination’s later survey defines eight. The different mapping coverage matters, and the earlier published diagram should not be treated as a full count. The NPS listing marks its broad period in a catalog band of 1000–1499 and prints 1200 and 1350 as significant years. The nomination’s pottery reassessment also entertains activity around 1500. These are catalog bounds and overlapping archaeological interpretations. They do not yield a dated abandonment year. [1–4]

The full Cotter article was not obtained through the publisher page, which supplied an abstract and bibliographic information. Excavation detail here comes from the accessible nomination and original Brown report. No Anna-specific exhibit or a Nation-published account identifying this site directly was located. Preserve the federal address restriction. [1–6]
