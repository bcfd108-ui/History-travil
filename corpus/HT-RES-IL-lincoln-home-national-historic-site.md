**1. Trip gate.** Use the Lincoln Home National Historic Site Visitor Center at 426 S. 7th St., Springfield, IL 62701, as the lawful public anchor. The current National Park Service hours page lists the Visitor Center at 9:00 AM – 5:00 PM, the Dean House and Arnold House exhibit buildings at 9:00 AM – 4:30 PM, and the streets of the historic neighborhood as open to walking from dawn till dusk. Entry inside the Lincoln Home itself, at Eighth and Jackson Streets, is by free ranger-led tour only, and a ticket is required. Tickets are free, are obtained in person at the Visitor Center front desk, and can only be obtained on the day of the visit; they cannot be reserved online, by phone, or days in advance for individual visitors. Groups of 15 or more must arrange in advance through the Springfield Convention and Visitors Bureau. There is no entrance fee. The park parking lot south of the Visitor Center charges $2.00 per hour per vehicle, payable by credit or debit card only; cash is not accepted. The site is closed on New Year's Day, Thanksgiving, and December 25. Confirm the day's last tour time at the desk on arrival, because the park's own pages give different usual last-tour times (see Gaps and conflicts). Checked Nov. 22, 2026 Pacific. [1, 2, 3, 4]

# Lincoln Home National Historic Site, Springfield
HT-RES-IL-lincoln-home-national-historic-site · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The house at the corner of Eighth and Jackson Streets began in 1839 as a one-and-a-half-story cottage built for the Reverend Charles Dresser. Dresser was the minister who had married Abraham Lincoln and Mary Todd on November 4, 1842. After their marriage the Lincolns spent their first year together in rented quarters at the Globe Tavern in Springfield, where their first son, Robert Todd Lincoln, was born in 1843. [5, 6, 8, 9]

In January 1844 Lincoln drew up a contract to buy Dresser's house. The Papers of Abraham Lincoln annotation, drawing on the Sangamon County deed record and on Harry E. Pratt's study of Lincoln's personal finances, breaks the transaction down as $1,200 plus a small lot Lincoln and his law partner Stephen T. Logan held, valued in the park's account at $300. Lincoln, Logan, and their wives conveyed that lot to Dresser on April 23, 1844, and Dresser and his wife completed the sale by conveying the home lot to Lincoln on May 2, 1844. It was the only house Lincoln ever owned. [5, 9]

The family that moved in that spring was small and living closely on a lawyer's income. The park's summary notes that Lincoln himself did the heavy household work of the early years — cutting wood, hauling water, and tending the cow — alongside a growing household that Mary Lincoln managed. Three more sons were born in the house: Edward Baker, called Eddie, in 1846; William Wallace, called Willie, in 1850; and Thomas, called Tad, in 1853. Eddie died in the house in 1850, just before his fourth birthday. The one sustained absence in the seventeen-year occupancy was Lincoln's single term in Congress, from late 1847 into 1849, when the house was let to a tenant. [5, 6, 8]

As the family grew, the house grew with it. A brick retaining wall and fence were built along the front in 1850 and extended along the Jackson Street side five years later, and by 1856 the Lincolns had raised the original cottage to a full two stories, ending with twelve rooms. The fabric recorded in the 1959 National Survey form was of native woods — an oak frame, split laths of hickory and oak, and black walnut used for door frames and weatherboarding. [6, 8]

From this house Lincoln walked to the law office he shared with William H. Herndon, and from this house his second political life was built. After years focused mainly on law and family in the early 1850s, the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 drew him back into active politics, through the 1858 Senate campaign against Stephen A. Douglas and into national notice. On May 17 or 18, 1860 — the park's own records differ by a day on the notification visit, see Gaps and conflicts — a committee from the Republican convention in Chicago brought him formal notice of his nomination for president in the home's parlor. Through that summer and fall the house became a campaign headquarters in all but name: reporters, photographers, and well-wishers came to Eighth and Jackson, and Lincoln received visitors at home rather than traveling to campaign. He was elected the 16th President on November 6, 1860. [6, 8, 10]

On February 6, 1861, the Lincolns held a farewell levee at the house for Springfield friends and neighbors. They left for Washington days later, renting the house rather than selling it — the first tenant was railroad executive Lucian Tilton — and placing their best furniture in storage against an expected return. That return never happened. Lincoln was assassinated on April 15, 1865, and Mary Lincoln wrote that she could not bear to go back to the scenes of the family's happiest years without them. [5, 6, 8]

The house stayed a rental for more than two decades after 1861, passing through tenants who included, in the survey form's account, Tilton, Union League founder George H. Harlow, and collector O. H. Oldroyd, who for a time exhibited his Lincoln collection inside it. In 1887 Robert Todd Lincoln, the family's only surviving son, gave the house to the State of Illinois to be kept as a public memorial, on the express condition that it be well maintained and open to the public free of charge — the condition that still governs admission today. Illinois held and operated it, first as a state memorial, for the next eighty-five years. [3, 5, 6, 8]

Federal recognition came in stages. The home was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960. Congress established Lincoln Home National Historic Site in 1971 by Public Law 92-128 (85 Stat. 347), and in 1972 Illinois conveyed the home to the United States, with the National Park Service taking over preservation of the house and beginning the longer work of acquiring and restoring the surrounding four-block neighborhood. The park today preserves 14 houses dating from the Lincoln era within that neighborhood, which in Lincoln's time held neighbors across a wide range of occupations and incomes. A major restoration closed the Lincoln Home in May 1987 — the house was enclosed in a protective shell so work could proceed year-round, down to replicating the lightning rods Lincoln had installed — and it reopened to the public on June 16, 1988, presented to its 1860 appearance. [5, 6, 13]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The Lincoln Home stands at 413 S. Eighth St., at the corner of Eighth and Jackson Streets, Springfield, Sangamon County, Illinois, on Lot 8 in Elijah Iles' Addition, the lot conveyed to Lincoln on May 2, 1844. The historic district around it covers four blocks of the Lincoln-era neighborhood. [5, 7, 8, 9, 13]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Lincoln Home National Historic Site Visitor Center, 426 S. 7th St., Springfield, IL 62701, one block west of the home. Start at the Visitor Center front desk for orientation, the park film, and the free same-day tour ticket, then walk the pedestrian-only streets of the historic neighborhood to the home for the timed ranger-led tour. Vehicular access to the home itself is limited to National Park Service and emergency vehicles. The first floor of the home is reachable by a wheelchair lift that bypasses the nine front steps; tell the desk when obtaining a ticket if the lift is needed. The second floor is reached by stairs only — 14 steps up and 14 back down — and power scooters and other motorized devices are not permitted inside because of size and weight, though wheelchairs, walkers, and canes that fit the doorframes are welcome. Only service animals are permitted inside the home. Keep the visit to the Visitor Center, the ticketed tour, the exhibit houses during their posted hours, and the public streets of the neighborhood during daylight hours. [1, 2, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Start at the Visitor Center at 426 S. 7th St. Take in the park film and orientation first, then obtain the free ticket for a specific tour time at the front desk — on busy days, especially from March through November and on summer weekends, tickets can run out before the last tour, so the ticket stop comes before any other stop. With time in hand before the tour, walk the neighborhood streets and use the Dean House and Arnold House exhibits, which close at 4:30 PM, half an hour before the Visitor Center. Then walk one block east to Eighth and Jackson for the ranger-led tour of the home. **Strongest story moment · The parlor in May 1860.** In the front room of a house bought for $1,200 cash and a $300 lot sixteen years earlier, a Springfield lawyer who had held no office for a decade received the committee that told him he was his party's candidate for president — and then ran that campaign largely from this house, receiving the country at his own front door. Close the visit back on the neighborhood streets in daylight, reading the surviving houses as the Lincolns' actual neighborscape rather than as a set built around a famous address. [1, 3, 4, 5, 10]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Visitor Center opening time conflicts across the park's own publications. The current hours page, last updated May 26, 2025, and the current FAQ page both give 9:00 AM – 5:00 PM. The park's older Teaching with Historic Places lesson, still live on nps.gov, gives 8:30 AM – 5:00 PM daily. This dossier follows the current hours and FAQ pages; a traveler relying on the lesson plan would arrive half an hour early, not late. [1, 2, 10]
- Last tour time conflicts across current park pages. The fees page says tickets may run out prior to the last tour of the day, "usually at 4:00pm." The tour page says tours run 9:00 AM – 5:00 PM with the first tour generally at 9:00 AM and the last generally at 4:30 PM. No single last-tour time is stated as guaranteed in either source; the trip gate therefore directs visitors to confirm at the desk. [3, 4]
- Tour length conflicts. The FAQ page gives 20 to 25 minutes. The tour page gives an average of thirty minutes, longer if guests have more questions. Both are park statements; neither is reconciled on the pages consulted. [2, 4]
- Marriage year error in one park source. The park's "Lincoln Home Through the Years" photographic essay says the Reverend Charles Dresser married Abraham Lincoln and Mary Todd in 1843. The park's main park summary, the 1959 survey form, and the Papers of Abraham Lincoln record all give the marriage as 1842 — November 4, 1842 in the fuller accounts. The 1843 date in the photographic essay is treated here as an error and is not followed. [5, 6, 8, 9]
- Purchase price is stated in two forms that total the same but read differently. The park summary and the Papers annotation give $1,200 cash plus a lot worth $300, conveyed to Dresser on April 23, 1844, with the home lot conveyed to Lincoln on May 2, 1844. Older Springfield accounts quoted in the sources consulted give the price flatly as $1,500. The deed-based breakdown is followed here; the flat $1,500 figure is the same total without the breakdown. The survey form's "January 1844" purchase date is the contract date, not the May 2 conveyance date, and the two dates are not interchangeable. [5, 8, 9]
- Nomination notification date differs by one day. The Teaching with Historic Places lesson dates Lincoln's receipt of the official notification of his nomination in the home's parlor to May 18, 1860. The 1959 survey form dates the committee's visit to May 17, 1860. The dossier prints both and follows neither as settled. [8, 10]
- Register and establishment dates describe different actions and are conflated in secondary summaries. The National Register record for this resource, NRIS 71000076, shows a publication date of August 18, 1971, resource type DISTRICT, criteria PERSON, style Greek Revival, areas of significance Law and Politics/Government, and a period of significance of 1825–1849 with significant year 1844 — a period that ends before most of the Lincoln occupancy it commemorates. The park summary separately states that the site was established in 1971, that the home was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960, that it was transferred to the National Park Service in 1972, and that property within the site boundary was added to the National Register as a district in 1976, with the register district smaller than and contained within the historic site boundary. These are distinct actions with distinct dates; no single "listed in" date covers them all, and the 1825–1849 period in the NRIS record is printed here as found, unexplained by the record page itself. [5, 7]
- Address roles are easy to confuse and are kept separate here. 426 S. 7th St. is the Visitor Center and the ticket desk. 413 S. Eighth St. is the Lincoln Home itself, and is also the superintendent mailing address given in the older lesson plan as 413 S. Eighth Street, Springfield, Illinois 62701-1905. A visitor who navigates to Eighth Street first still has to return to Seventh Street for a ticket. [2, 4, 10]
- No coordinates are asserted. No latitude or longitude from a primary park or register source was inspected in this research, so the Locator coordinate fields stay blank rather than carrying a figure copied from a secondary aggregator.
- Furnishings provenance is only partly documented in the sources consulted. The survey form notes period furnishings including some original Lincoln pieces and reproduced interior decoration including wallpaper, and the park states the home is presented to its 1860 appearance after the 1987–1988 restoration, but no room-by-room furnishings inventory was retrieved in this research. No claim is made here about which specific objects in the house are Lincoln-owned originals beyond that general statement. [6, 8]

## 7. Historical photo records

Three photo records were actually inspected on their holding pages during this research. No photograph is adopted for use in this dossier; the records below are inspection notes only, with credit and rights exactly as the holding pages state them.

**Inspected — Whipple photographs, 1860 (NPS park page).** The park's "1860 Whipple Photographs" page reproduces and interprets two photographs by Boston photographer John Adams Whipple, catalogued on the page as LIHO-001 and LIHO-002, showing Lincoln in the front yard with his two youngest sons, Willie and Tad, neighbors on the front walk, and in LIHO-002 the blurred figure of playmate Isaac Diller on the sidewalk. The page credits the images "NPS" and uses them as restoration evidence for the shutters and venetian blinds, the Corneau fence style, the small elm in front of the house, a backyard apple tree, the back porch latticework, and the dirt streets and plank curbs of 1860. One of the pair, LIHO-001, is reproduced on the back of the Lincoln Home tour ticket. No separate rights or reuse statement appears on the page beyond the NPS credit; no cleared credit line for republication is offered there. [11]

**Inspected — Meserve Lincoln Photograph No. 16, 1860 (University of Illinois).** The University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign Library digital collections page for this item, held in the Illinois History and Lincoln Collections as part of the Frederick Hill Meserve Selected Photographs, describes a detail of an 8" x 10" photograph of the Lincoln home by an unknown photographer, dated 1860, showing Lincoln standing in the doorway with a crowd of neighbors watching a procession, identified in the description as probably the August 8, 1860 procession, with a banner reading "Won't you let me in? (Kansas)". Rights as stated on the page: "No Copyright - United States" — the image is in the public domain; the descriptions are in copyright, reprinted courtesy of the Meserve-Kunhardt Foundation. Credit for a cleared use would follow the holding page: *University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign Library, Illinois History and Lincoln Collections, Frederick Hill Meserve Selected Photographs, Meserve Lincoln Photograph No. 16*. The catalog page was inspected; the underlying negative and any larger original were not. [12]

**Inspected — park photographic essay frames, 1839–1988 (NPS park page).** The park's "Lincoln Home Through the Years" page carries a dated sequence of views of the house — captioned for the 1840s, the late 1850s, 1865, 1905, 1910, 1931, the 1960s, the 1987 restoration encasement, and the 1988 reopening — with text tying the 1865 view to the mourners and photographers who came after the assassination and the 1931 view to President Herbert Hoover's visit at the time of the Lincoln Tomb rededication. The page gives no per-image collection, photographer, or rights credit beyond its NPS hosting. The Teaching with Historic Places lesson separately credits its cover view of the two-story house "Courtesy of Lincoln Home National Historic Site" and a crowd view "Courtesy Abraham Lincoln Presidential Library." No image from either page is adopted here, and no earlier camera stance or present-day aligned view was verified. No Photo Point. [6, 10]

## 8. Optional photo-tag observations

Quiet witness only. Reading and planning are free and available without an account, location sharing or contribution. If a visitor photographs the home from the public streets of the neighborhood, the 1860 Whipple views from the Corneau house yard across Eighth Street are the documented historic camera position to think against — the house, fence, and front yard as the family itself was photographed in the nomination summer — without entering any yard or blocking the pedestrian streets to do it.
