# HT-RES-MI-whitefish-point-light — The oldest light on Superior, standing over the Shipwreck Coast
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

The oldest operating light on Lake Superior stands at the point where ships turn: Whitefish Point, where the shoreline runs west for eighty miles of wreck-strewn coast and every vessel bound to or from the St. Marys River makes its course change. The first stone tower was lit in 1849; the iron tower that replaced it in 1861 still works as a Coast Guard aid to navigation, and its station ground is now the campus of the Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum — where the bell of the *Edmund Fitzgerald*, recovered from her wreck in 1995, is kept as a memorial to her crew.

## 1. Trip gate
This is a working light station and a museum campus at the end of a single road; the visit is shaped by both. The Great Lakes Shipwreck Historical Society (GLSHS), the operator, states the terms on its admission page, checked November 26, 2026: the museum is open every day from May 1 to October 31, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m., and all buildings close at 5:00 p.m. Admission as the operator prints it: adults $15.00; children 17 and under $11.00; children 5 and under free; family (2 adults and 2 or more children) $50.00.

The light tower itself is not a museum exhibit to climb. The tower is still a fully operating U.S. Coast Guard Aid to Navigation (GLSHS visit page); visitors may stand in the tower base and look up the wrought-iron spiral staircase — that is the access the operator describes, and no tower climb is offered in the sources checked. The grounds' other standing rule is printed on the same page in capitals: "WHITEFISH POINT IS A NO DRONE ZONE," covering the museum property and the adjoining Fish and Wildlife Service and Michigan Audubon ground. The point is reached by one road: Whitefish Point Road, 11 miles north of Paradise, per the operator's directions. Outside the season the museum is closed, and the operator's pages describe no winter visiting program. The annual *Edmund Fitzgerald* memorial ceremony falls on November 10, after the season closes; in 2026 the Society held it as a private memorial for surviving family members and those connected to the ship and crew, livestreamed to the public (GLSHS Fitzgerald page) — a memorial service, not a public attraction, and this entry carries it that way.

## 2. The story
Whitefish Point was a stopping place long before it was a light station: the Michigan historical marker at the site (Registered Site L0272, erected 1974) records it as "an early stopping place for Indians, Voyageurs, Coureur des bois and Jesuit missionaries," and the point's work in the shipping era is the same geography — it "marks the course change for ore boats and other ships navigating this treacherous coastline to and from St. Mary's Canal." The Great Lakes Shipwreck Historical Society puts it plainly: "Whitefish Point marks the critical turning point for all ships entering or leaving the lake," and the waters west of it along an 80-mile stretch of rugged shoreline "have earned the ominous title, 'Lake Superior's Shipwreck Coast'" (GLSHS visit page).

The light came with the copper and iron trade. Congress appropriated $5,000 for the lighthouse in 1847, and 115.5 acres at the point were reserved for the station that April (U.S. Coast Guard Historian's record; Whitefish Point Management Plan, 2002). Construction of a stone tower began in 1848; the final cost was $8,298, and the light was exhibited for the first time in 1849 (USCG). It was among the first lights on Lake Superior, and the station is the oldest active light on the lake — the statement the state marker and the operator both carry. The Coast Guard's keeper list runs unbroken from James Starr (1848–1849) through the manned era; its longest tenure is Robert Carlson's, 1903–1931.

The stone tower did not last. The operator's account is blunt: the original stone tower "existed from 1849 until 1860 – the severe winds experienced here proved too much for its basic masonry construction." In 1860 Congress authorized new lighthouses for the copper and iron ore trade, and in 1861 an Ohio contractor built the replacement to the Lighthouse Board's exact specifications: an "iron-pile" tower of iron and copper — "steel was too new to be trusted" — its cylinders distributing the wind load to the foundation (GLSHS). The Coast Guard's chronology dates construction to 1861 and the new tower's entry into service to 1862 (the discrepancy is printed in §6); the National Register record carries 1861 as the significant year. A Fresnel lens replaced the original lamp array in 1857 (the Coast Guard's record gives the lens order two ways; see §6). The keeper's house was changed to a duplex in 1895 (USCG); the restored building the museum tours is the 1861 Lightkeeper's Quarters, a side-by-side duplex with period furnishings (GLSHS).

A light alone could not do the point's work, and the station grew the apparatus of rescue. A fog signal building was constructed in 1871, replaced in corrugated iron in 1905, destroyed in a storm in 1935, and rebuilt in brick in 1936 (USCG). The U.S. Life-Saving Service came to the Shipwreck Coast in 1876 and established four stations on this shoreline, "the most concentrated number of stations anywhere on the Great Lakes" (GLSHS). The Coast Guard kept a Lifeboat Rescue Station at Whitefish Point from 1923 to 1951, its crews "assisting countless mariners in distress and rescuing the entire crew of the sinking *Zillah* in 1926" (GLSHS). Two surfboat houses served the station, one for a motor launch and one for a hand-pulled, beach-launched boat; the second survives, restored, on the museum campus.

The manned station ended in the early 1970s. The Fresnel lens was removed in 1968 and a DCB-224 aerobeacon installed (USCG). The station was automated — the chronology gives 1971 — and since then, as the state marker puts it, "the light, fog signal, and radio beacon have been automated and controlled from Sault Ste. Marie."

The ground's second life began with recognition. The lighthouse was placed on the National Register of Historic Places on February 28, 1973, under the Event criterion for its significance in Transportation, with 1861 as its significant year (NRIS record 73000947); it was designated a Michigan State Historic Site in 1974, and the state marker was erected that year. The Great Lakes Shipwreck Historical Society took up the station's preservation; the Coast Guard's chronology dates the museum's establishment at the site to 1985. Ownership of the light station ground passed to the Society by federal patent in 1998, under State Historic Preservation Office covenants (Whitefish Point Management Plan, 2002); the point's remaining ground is held by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Seney National Wildlife Refuge, Whitefish Point Unit) and Michigan Audubon.

On November 10, 1975, the *Edmund Fitzgerald* was lost with her entire crew of 29 men on Lake Superior, 17 miles north-northwest of Whitefish Point (GLSHS). This entry states that once, in the operator's own terms, and does not retell the night. What belongs to this ground is what the Society did afterward: three underwater expeditions to the wreck, in 1989, 1994, and 1995, and on July 4, 1995, the recovery of the ship's 200-pound bronze bell, in a joint expedition with the National Geographic Society, the Canadian Navy, Sony Corporation, and the Sault Ste. Marie Tribe of Chippewa Indians. "The bell is now on display in the Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum at Whitefish Point as a memorial to her lost crew" (GLSHS). The Society holds a memorial ceremony at the point each November 10, with the bell at its center; the museum gallery holds a permanent exhibit dedicated to the ship (GLSHS admission page).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a station of stone and then iron at the end of a wilderness road — keepers living in a duplex on the ground, winding clockwork, burning coal for the fog whistle, launching surfboats by hand. **Now:** the same tower, automated since 1971 and still an active aid to navigation, standing over a museum campus the keeper's quarters anchor as an exhibit of the keepers' own lives.

**Then:** the point's work was warning and rescue: the light, the fog signal, the life-saving crews. **Now:** the work is memory and record — the museum gallery's artifacts from the wrecks of the Whitefish Point Underwater Preserve, the *Fitzgerald*'s bell kept as a memorial, and the November 10 ceremony, held in 2026 as a private service for the families and livestreamed (GLSHS).

**Then:** July 21, 1947, the Coast Guard's aerial photograph (§7) shows the whole manned station inside its fence — tower, duplex, fog signal building, and yard — with sand, scrub, and the tree line around it. **Now:** the same core buildings stand, joined by the museum gallery, the restored 1920 U.S. Navy Radio Building, the museum store, and a barrier-free boardwalk to the shoreline built in 1998 (GLSHS) — and the wild ground around the campus is refuge and bird observatory land.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The 1861 iron tower, in continuous operation as a U.S. Coast Guard aid to navigation; visitors may stand in the tower base and look up the staircase (GLSHS visit page, checked November 26, 2026)
- The 1861 Lightkeeper's Quarters, restored, with period furnishings and exhibits on the keepers and families (GLSHS)
- The 1923 Surfboat House, restored, with Life-Saving Service exhibits and a full-size replica 26-foot Beebe-McLellan surfboat (GLSHS — the operator's own spelling of the boat name)
- The 1923 U.S. Coast Guard Motor Lifeboat House, housing Motor Lifeboat CG 36381 and the museum's video theater (GLSHS)
- The 1920 U.S. Navy Radio Building, restored, with an exhibit opened in summer 2024 (GLSHS)
- The Shipwreck Museum gallery, including the permanent *Edmund Fitzgerald* exhibit and the ship's original 200-pound bronze bell, recovered July 4, 1995, displayed as a memorial to her lost crew (GLSHS)
- The barrier-free boardwalk to the Lake Superior shoreline, built 1998 (GLSHS)
- The Michigan historical marker for Whitefish Point Light, Registered Site L0272 (1974)
- The active light itself — automated, controlled from Sault Ste. Marie (state marker; USCG)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the progression follows the campus as the operator lays it out, within its season and hours:

1. **At the tower base:** Start with the working light. Stand where visitors are permitted — in the base of the 1861 iron tower, looking up the wrought-iron spiral staircase (GLSHS) — and read the frame against the 1947 aerial in §7: the same tower, then a manned station's center, now an automated aid.
2. **In the 1861 Lightkeeper's Quarters:** The duplex the keepers' families lived in, restored with period furnishings. The Coast Guard's keeper list (§2) runs from James Starr in 1848 to the automation era; the quarters are where that list lived.
3. **In the Shipwreck Museum gallery:** The artifacts of the Shipwreck Coast, and the *Fitzgerald* exhibit with the ship's bell. This is a memorial space as much as a gallery — the bell is displayed, in the Society's words, "as a memorial to her lost crew," and it should be met that way.
4. **At the 1923 Surfboat House and Motor Lifeboat House:** The rescue half of the station's story — the hand-pulled surfboat, the Life-Saving Service of 1876, and the 1926 *Zillah* rescue.
5. **On the boardwalk to the shoreline:** End at the water the station served. The boardwalk (1998) crosses the sensitive dunes to the Lake Superior shore; the course change the marker describes is the one ships still make past this point.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The Coast Guard's own record disagrees with itself at three points, and all three print unreconciled. Its header dates the station "Built in 1847" and "Station Established: 1848," while its chronology gives the appropriation in 1847, construction beginning in 1848, and the light first exhibited in 1849 — the sequence §2 follows. Its header states automation in 1970 ("Automated? YES 1970"), while its chronology gives "1971 – Station automated," as does the state marker; §2 uses 1971 with its sources named. And its header describes the original lens as "THIRD ORDER, FRESNEL 1857," while its chronology states that in 1857 the "lamp array changed to 4th order Fresnel lens" — §2 prints the 1857 Fresnel change without an order.

The iron tower's first year also prints two ways. The Coast Guard chronology gives construction beginning in 1861 and "1862 – New tower placed in service," while its header gives "Year Current Tower(s) First Lit: 1861," the National Register record carries 1861 as the significant year, and the operator describes the tower as "in continuous operation since 1861." Section 2 prints both forms rather than settling the year.

The museum's founding prints two ways as well. The Coast Guard chronology records "1985 – Great Lakes Shipwreck Museum established at site"; secondary accounts give 1986 as the opening year. The Society's own pages checked do not state a founding year, so the conflict stands: 1985 in the federal chronology, 1986 in secondary accounts, unverified against the Society's own record in this pass. The Society's visitor counts also conflict inside its own pages — "over 100,000 visitors each season" on the visit page, "over 75,000 visitors each year" on the admission page.

Two smaller gaps. The 1861 date in the Lightkeeper's Quarters' name is the operator's; the Coast Guard chronology records only that the keeper's house was "changed to duplex" in 1895, and no source checked dates the present building's construction independently — §4 carries it under the operator's name. And no current surveyed acreage for the campus is stated in the sources checked; the only acreage printed here is the 1847 reservation figure, 115.5 acres.

## 7. Photo record
**First photograph:** *Whitefish Point Light Station, aerial view, July 21, 1947* — photograph by an unknown U.S. Coast Guard employee, Wikimedia Commons file "Whitefish Point July, 21 1947.jpg," the Commons file record of the Coast Guard print. What the downloaded frame actually shows: a black-and-white aerial view of the whole manned station inside its fence — the skeletal iron tower with its lantern at the center, the keeper's duplex and station buildings below it, the fog signal building at the lower right, the fenced yard with its crossing gravel paths, a small outbuilding at the left, and sand, scrub, and the tree line around the cleared ground. The print's own caption strip, lettered at the top of the frame, reads "WHITEFISH POINT / JULY 21 1947." No people are visible at this height. It shows the station as a working Coast Guard post in 1947 — before the museum, before automation — and it is a photograph of the station ground only: no wreck, no wreckage, nothing of the lake's losses is in this frame.
