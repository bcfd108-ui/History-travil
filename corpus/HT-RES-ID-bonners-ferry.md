## 2. The story

The river was not simply an obstacle awaiting a bridge. It was also a transportation corridor, and successive crossings changed how people and goods moved. ITD's *Crossing the Kootenai* interpretation associates the 1864 commercial ferry with Edward L. Bonner and travel toward the Wild Horse goldfields. It records a 1905 low-water road bridge that still required ferry assistance during high water, a higher bridge in 1910, and a 1933 replacement with pedestrian provision. [4]

Rail and water transport overlapped. The same panel describes the Kootenai Valley Railroad's 1899 movable bridge allowing sternwheelers through, and the first train across the Spokane International bridge in 1906. These are separate railway and roadway structures; neither is the present visitor footpath. [4]

**Named primary recorded voice:** on October 1, 1952, **President Harry S. Truman** addressed Bonners Ferry from his train's rear platform at 4:10 p.m. Pacific standard time. His published remarks include: “Libby Dam Will solve forever the flood problem on the Kootenai River.” That is a campaign-era claim, not a present hydrologic fact or safety assurance. He invoked the 1948 flooding while arguing for federal water projects. [5]

The Truman Library catalogs the actual recording as **SR59-110**, duration 6 minutes 35 seconds, produced by the U.S. Army Signal Corps, restrictions “Unrestricted.” The transcript was read and recording metadata checked; the audio was not listened to. [6] This connects the railway to civic life: a train platform could become a place where the river's future was debated.

**Tribal account, from the Tribe itself:** the Kootenai Tribe of Idaho's Natural Resources site states that its people were not represented in the 1855 Hellgate negotiations and describes attempted removal, later allotments and the 1974 struggle that secured 12.5 acres of trust land. Its continuing emphasis is environmental stewardship and cultural-resource protection. This dossier uses that own published account, not an outsider's summary of Tribal beliefs. [7]

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a ferry, movable railway bridge, road bridges and steamboats formed a changing transport system rather than one timeless “old bridge.” ITD explains that later road crossings shifted eastward, leaving the bridge no longer aligned with Main Street. [4]

**Now:** the museum preserves and interprets records; the public park offers a lawful river-view setting. Neither establishes access to the historical ferry landing or train-platform location. A view of water is not a verified view from the original crossing.

A more intimate travel record survives through the museum: an anonymously authored account names **Mrs. J. B. Brody, Mrs. E. E. Fry, Mrs. J. F. McGreevy and Miss L. G. Walliss** on a June 10, 1914 automobile trip to Spokane, with Mrs. Brody driving. The museum's bridge caption says they **may** have crossed the old bridge. Retain that uncertainty; the named participants do not make the anonymous narrator identifiable. This is supplementary road-travel evidence, not a railway account. [8]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Conditional proposal, not an activated walking route:**

1. Begin at the museum during published opening. Separate road, rail and water crossings using available records rather than assuming every bridge photograph shows the same structure.
2. Read Truman's dated words alongside the recording's catalog. Ask what his promise reveals about flood memory and the politics of infrastructure, not whether the promise guarantees safety now.
3. If public park access is confirmed and conditions permit, make a separate daylight river-view pause on the designated grounds. No connecting walk across rail property or future trail is included.
4. End with the strongest contrast: the river that travelers paid to cross became the river a president promised to control, while the Tribe's own account frames an enduring responsibility to care for land and water.

Allow approximately an hour indoors and a brief separate exterior pause as editorial planning allowances, not operator-guaranteed durations. If winter park access is unavailable, the museum and digital archive still support the complete story.
