# Topeka’s Monroe Elementary School — before the first bell

Kansas OP01 · Draft research for Dash · November 19, 2026

## 2. The story

### Two distances in one child's school day

In 1951 Oliver Brown took the stand in federal district court. The published testimony transcription records his description of the distance to Monroe School as “15 blocks, approximately, from Monroe School.” In the same questioning he put Sumner at seven blocks. He described Linda walking before school to a bus pickup point, and described the rail switching and street traffic she encountered before boarding. Each number answers a different question. One is distance from home to the assigned school, one is distance to a nearer school and the bus walk is a separate piece of the day. [3]

The Kansas Historical Society’s oral-history collection identifies Linda Brown Thompson as a Monroe pupil during the organizing period. She and other children lived in neighborhoods where their playmates could be of other races. In its biographical account accompanying the oral history, the Society describes school assignments that separated those same children. NAACP organizers recruited parents and documented what the local arrangement meant for families. Monroe was one of four elementary schools designated for Black students. The people who taught there served their pupils within this system. The building can hold that account alongside what the district required of their families. [4,5,9]

### What two courts made of the evidence

The federal district panel’s August 3, 1951 opinion, preserved in NARA’s Civil Case Files, assessed the local schools and found that segregation had a detrimental effect. It nonetheless held for the school board under the precedent it took to be binding. The Supreme Court’s May 17, 1954 decision addressed Kansas alongside cases from other states and said: “Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal.” Read the printed district decision and the U.S. Reports decision together. Equipment and buildings could be compared. The constitutional question could not be settled by counting windows or classroom supplies. [6,7]

## 3. Then / now

NPS dates the current Monroe building to 1927. Its public exhibits use a surviving segregated elementary school to tell the history of the litigation, families and students. The paired National Register entry includes Sumner, but this itinerary uses the staffed Monroe site and its operator’s public approach. The testified walk, homes, rail yards and original bus stop are documentary ground in this dossier. [1,3,5,8,9]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The 1951 panel and 1954 Supreme Court reached different legal outcomes. The district court acknowledged injury and followed then-controlling precedent. The Supreme Court held public school segregation constitutionally unequal. The physical-condition evidence and traveler-distance evidence measure different things. [3,6,7]
- An older NPS brochure advertises daily opening. The current park hours and compendium specify Tuesday–Saturday and holiday closures. The dated Thanksgiving line is on NPS’s current visit page. Follow that schedule. [1,2,10,13]
- Oliver Brown’s testimony was read through a published transcription. The signed official transcript pages and Linda’s full audio were not independently checked. The Kansas Historical Society Monroe image page returned a JavaScript-required bot verification screen. No image facts were taken from that direct request. [3,4,11]
