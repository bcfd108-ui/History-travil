# HT-RES-WV-trans-allegheny-lunatic-asylum — A Kirkbride asylum in hand-cut blue sandstone, built for 250 patients and filled far beyond that
Division prisons-asylums-tours-001 • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Virginia authorized this hospital in 1858, before West Virginia existed. It was built across the West Fork River from Weston to the Kirkbride plan — long echeloned wings, light and air as treatment — in blue sandstone cut by hand. It opened to patients in October 1864, in the middle of the Civil War and in the middle of its own construction, and it did not close until May 1994. For most of those 130 years it held many more people than the 250 it was designed for. The building survives nearly whole, a National Historic Landmark, and the visit today is a tour of the wards where that overcrowding happened.

## 2. The story
In the decades before the Civil War, the treatment of people with mental illness was being argued over in public, and reformers such as Dorothea Dix and Dr. Thomas S. Kirkbride of Philadelphia pressed a specific answer: insanity was an illness, and the building itself was part of the treatment. Kirkbride's published plan called for a rural site, a central administration block, and long wings stepped back in echelon so that wards could be classified separately and every room could have light and outside air. Virginia had two older asylums, at Williamsburg and at Staunton, both east of the mountains and both crowded. Western Virginians had pressed Richmond for public institutions of their own for years. On March 22, 1858, the General Assembly passed "an act authorizing the establishment of the Trans-Alleghany lunatic asylum" — the first public institution of any size Virginia undertook west of the Alleghenies, per the National Historic Landmark nomination.

Commissioners visited candidate towns — Sutton, Fayetteville, and Weston — and Weston, the Lewis County seat, received the favorable report. A board of nine local directors was constituted over "The Lunatic Asylum West of the Alleghany Mountains," purchased 269 acres across the West Fork River from the town, and hired Baltimore architect Richard Snowden Andrews (1830–1903), whose other work included the Maryland governor's mansion in Annapolis and the south wing of the U.S. Treasury Building in Washington. The enabling act required the plans to be approved by physicians experienced in running such institutions; Andrews's design was reviewed by Kirkbride himself and by Dr. Francis T. Stribling of the Western Lunatic Asylum at Staunton, and their suggestions were incorporated. The optimum population written into the design was 250 patients.

Construction began late in 1858. The stone was a fine blue sandstone quarried at Mt. Clare in nearby Harrison County; bricks for the interior walls were burned on the site; artisans were hired for the skilled work, and the unskilled labor was done in part by prisoners — the Society of Architectural Historians' Archipedia entry records that the first construction crew, Black convicts from the state penitentiary at Staunton, arrived in September 1858. By June 28, 1861, the southern one-story wing was under roof and work had begun on the connecting three-story section. That day, Virginia's secession government ordered work suspended and the remaining funds returned to Richmond. About $98,000 had been spent; some $27,000 sat on deposit at the Exchange Bank of Virginia in Weston. The pro-Union Reorganized Government of Virginia, seated at Wheeling, moved the money out of reach on July 2, 1861 — the nomination's account — and in 1862 added a further appropriation (the West Virginia Encyclopedia gives $40,000). When West Virginia became a state in 1863, the institution was renamed the West Virginia Hospital for the Insane, and Dr. James A. Hall was appointed its first superintendent.

The first patients entered in October 1864 — nine of them, per the Archipedia entry — into a building that was still a construction site, and construction continued around the hospital's operation for another seventeen years. By the autumn of 1867 there were 45 patients in the south wing, and patients the state had been maintaining at Staunton were transferred to Weston that year; the West Virginia Encyclopedia puts the census at 200 by 1868. The main building was completed in 1880–81, essentially to Andrews's 1858 plan despite the interruption: nearly 1,300 feet along the facade, a four-story center block under a clock tower rising 200 feet, 902 windows by the nomination's count, all of it load-bearing blue sandstone. The hospital was meant to be self-sufficient, and largely was: its own farm and dairy herd, waterworks and reservoir, a nearby coal mine for fuel, a cemetery, and workshops that made the patients' clothing, mattresses, and most of the institution's furniture — the nomination presents this both as economy and as the period's belief that patients should learn a trade. It was also a segregated institution: the Encyclopedia records that separate rooms for Black patients were added in 1873.

The design figure of 250 did not hold. The compiled series from the hospital's reports, carried in most modern accounts, runs 717 patients by 1880, 1,661 by 1938, and over 1,800 by 1949. In 1913 the legislature renamed the institution Weston State Hospital; its function as the state's primary mental hospital did not change. On October 3, 1935, a fire started by a patient destroyed six men's wards in the main building and brought a cupola down through the roof; the damaged wing was rebuilt with Works Progress Administration funds and was afterward, by the accounts of a later investigation, noticeably better furnished than the rest of the complex. A 1938 survey committee organized by North American medical organizations reported a population that included people with epilepsy, alcoholics, drug addicts, and people classed at the time as non-educable — categories a Kirkbride hospital had not been designed to treat separately. In 1949 a series of reports in The Charleston Gazette found poor sanitation and insufficient furniture, lighting, and heating in much of the complex. In the early 1950s the hospital became a site of the West Virginia Lobotomy Project: the Encyclopedia dates the project 1952 to 1955 and records that numerous patients were lobotomized in those years, in the period when Dr. Walter Freeman was promoting the transorbital procedure across state institutions. The peak census came in the 1950s; how high it ran is disputed, and the dispute is carried in §6 rather than settled here.

The end came with the national shift away from large state asylums. Weston State Hospital closed in May 1994, replaced by the new William R. Sharpe Jr. Hospital in Weston, which the Encyclopedia prices at $27 million. The main building had been listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978 and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1990. Empty for thirteen years, the property was sold at public auction by the West Virginia Department of Health and Human Resources on August 29, 2007; contemporary reporting put the high bid at $1.5 million. The buyers restored the original name, Trans-Allegheny Lunatic Asylum, and reopened the building for tours in 2008. The historic tours described in §1 are the current form of the visit.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a Kirkbride ward in the 1860s — a classified ward in a stepped wing, each patient among strangers, in a room built to be flooded with light and air, in a hospital designed for 250 people in total and run as a self-sustaining farm community. **Now:** the same wings, toured in groups, with a restored patient ward shown on the first-floor south tour and unrestored sections shown on the north tour, per the operator's tour descriptions.

**Then:** a construction site that took patients anyway — the south wing receiving its first nine patients in October 1864 while masons were still raising the rest of a 1,300-foot building around them. **Now:** the completed facade the nomination describes — center clock tower, twin Jacobean gables, stepped-back pavilions and hyphens — standing as one of the largest hand-cut stone masonry buildings in the United States, in the Landmark nomination's assessment.

**Then:** 1949 — a state hospital holding over 1,800 people in a building designed for 250, with a state newspaper reporting poor sanitation and insufficient heat, light, and furniture across much of the complex. **Now:** a privately held Landmark whose tour income funds stabilization and restoration of the same wards.

## 4. What's still there
- The main building itself, 1858–1881, substantially as completed: blue sandstone facade of nearly 1,300 feet, four-story center block with clock tower, echeloned ward wings with one-story end wings, per the Landmark nomination
- The south one-story wing — the first segment completed and the section that received the first patients in 1864, interpreted on the operator's tours as the Civil War section
- Ward interiors in two conditions: restored patient ward space and unrestored ward space, both shown on the operator's first-floor tours
- The Medical Center building and the separate Forensics Building (the former criminally insane unit), both standing on the campus and both included in the operator's tour offers
- The 1935 brick auxiliary unit attached at the extreme southeastern end of the main building, inside the Landmark boundary as the nomination draws it
- The open front grounds between the facade and River Street — the entrance courtyard the nomination's boundary justification treats as integral to the setting since construction
- The West Fork River frontage opposite downtown Weston — the site as the commissioners chose it in 1858, on 269 acres purchased that year

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **On the front grounds, facing the facade:** Start with the whole building at once — nearly 1,300 feet of blue sandstone — and the nomination's basic fact: this was designed, deliberately, for 250 people. Everything else in the visit is measured against that number.
2. **In the south wing, the Civil War section:** The first-completed segment, under roof by June 1861, funded through a secession crisis, and receiving patients in October 1864 while the rest was still scaffolding. The state's founding and the hospital's founding are the same event here.
3. **On the wards, restored and unrestored:** The Kirkbride idea in physical form — classification, light, air — read against the census figures in §2, and against what the 1938 survey and the 1949 Gazette series found in these rooms.
4. **At the Medical Center and the Forensics Building:** The twentieth-century hospital — medical procedures, the lobotomy project's years, and the separate custody of patients held as criminally insane — as distinct buildings with distinct records.
5. **Back on the front grounds:** The afterlife — closure in 1994, thirteen empty years, the 2007 auction, and a Landmark maintained by tour admission.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The first patients' day in October 1864 is not agreed. The West Virginia Encyclopedia states that the first patients entered on October 22, 1864, as Dr. James A. Hall was replaced by Dr. R. Hills. Other current accounts give October 17, 1864. The Landmark nomination, the fullest primary-side record checked, gives no day at all — only that "it was not until late in 1864 that a portion of the hospital was opened to house patients." The month and year print; the day is carried here as a dispute. A related stray figure: an early institutional history dates the first admissions to October 22, 1859, with nine patients brought from Ohio — a year in which the building was nowhere near receiving patients, and apparently a conflation; it is noted here so the date is not picked up elsewhere as fact.

The first appropriation is given two ways. The Landmark nomination states that the board began "with an initial appropriation of $25,000." The West Virginia Encyclopedia states that "the General Assembly appropriated $50,000" before construction got under way by the end of 1858. Both are responsible records; the figures may reflect successive appropriations in the same session, but neither source says so, and both print here unmerged.

The peak census is disputed. The figure most often printed is 2,400 patients in the 1950s — it is the figure carried, for example, in the Library of Congress record notes for this entry's photograph. Other compilations of the same hospital-report series put the 1950s peak at 2,600. The dated waypoints (717 in 1880, 1,661 in 1938, over 1,800 in 1949) are consistent across accounts; the peak is not, and no single peak figure is asserted in §2.

The building's length is stated loosely in the sources. The nomination says the structure "measures nearly 1300 feet along the facade" and elsewhere that the end wings "bring the total length to over 1200 feet"; the Archipedia entry gives 1,295 feet; the Encyclopedia calls it "two-tenths of a mile long," about 1,056 feet. The nomination's figure is used here, and the variance flagged rather than averaged.

Two absences in the record should be stated plainly. No Historic American Buildings Survey documentation for this building was found in the Library of Congress's Pictures collection when checked on November 22, 2026 — the federal photographic record at the Library is the Carol M. Highsmith Archive material used in §7, not a HABS survey. And the nomination's UTM references are boundary-corner references for a 26.5-acre parcel, partly illegible in the scanned form; no point latitude/longitude is carried in the primary records checked, and none prints in §10.

## 8. Standing on the ground
What a visitor can check against the record, on site:

- The facade's scale against the nomination's description: nearly 1,300 feet of blue sandstone, a four-story center block, and a central tower the nomination puts at 200 feet, with the four lesser towers at 150 feet.
- The masonry bands the nomination describes: rusticated ashlar at the basement story, a more refined tooled surface at the first story, smooth ashlar above, divided by a projecting belt course — all readable from the front grounds.
- The stepped plan: pavilions and connecting hyphens setting back in echelon from the center, with three-sided bays at the far ends — the Kirkbride arrangement the plans were required to have approved by Kirkbride and Stribling.
- The south one-story wing as the oldest fabric: the segment under roof by June 28, 1861, per the nomination, and the section the operator interprets as the Civil War section.
- The window count as a standing claim: the nomination states 902 windows, and the fenestration's stated purpose — wards "flooded with light and air" — can be read directly off the elevations.
- The rear of the building: the wings at the extreme southwest end connected in 1879 to form an enclosed courtyard, and the brick service structures behind the main unit, both as the nomination's description records them.
- The setting: the open entrance courtyard between facade and River Street, and the West Fork River beyond it — the boundary justification in the nomination treats those grounds as part of the landmark, not as landscaping added later.
