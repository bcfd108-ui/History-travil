# HT-RES-OH-fort-meigs — A ten-acre log fort on a Maumee bluff that two armies could not take
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

On a bluff above the Maumee River rapids at Perrysburg, a full-size log fort stands reconstructed on its 1813 ground — seven blockhouses, five batteries, and earthen traverses inside a pointed stockade — where an American garrison held through two British sieges in 1813 and bought the time to build a fleet on Lake Erie.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a reconstructed War of 1812 fort and its museum: Fort Meigs, in Perrysburg, Ohio, an Ohio History Connection site managed locally by the Fort Meigs Association, on the Ohio History Connection's own statement. ## 2. The story
Fort Meigs was built because an invasion had failed. In August 1812 Brigadier General William Hull surrendered Detroit and most of the Army of the Northwest; Major General William Henry Harrison, rebuilding that army, needed a fortified advance post for a renewed move against Canada — and one that could hold the Maumee River line if the British came south instead. The fort was named for Return Jonathan Meigs Jr., the governor of Ohio. The site's own history, carried by the Fort Meigs Association at fortmeigs.org, states that ground was broken on **February 2, 1813**, under Harrison's orders, on a bluff overlooking the Maumee River rapids in what is now Perrysburg. The Ohio History Connection's Ohio Memory account adds the engineering of the place: on completion in April 1813 the installation contained seven blockhouses, five artillery batteries, two storehouses, and approximately twenty artillery pieces. Its walls, on the standard description carried in the site's histories, were logs cut to 15-foot lengths, set partially into the ground, with a steep earthen slope thrown against them against bombardment — and inside, Harrison's men threw up the great earthen traverses, banks running across the fort that gave shelter from fire coming over the walls. Despite horrid weather and disease in the camp, the Association's history states, the fort was completed by the end of April 1813 — just in time.

The British came down from Fort Malden in Upper Canada under General Henry Proctor, camped in the ruins of old Fort Miami on the north side of the river, and on the morning of **May 1, 1813**, on the Association's history, British artillery opened fire on the fort. The Ohio History Connection's account of the siege's ordnance describes what that meant: a weeklong artillery duel across the river, British batteries on the opposite shore in what is now Maumee; an estimated 2,000 British rounds between May 1 and May 5, audible, on contemporary report, forty miles upriver at Defiance and more than sixty miles away at Upper Sandusky; solid shot of 6 to 24 pounds, sometimes heated red-hot as hot shot; canister and grape; and hollow exploding bombs arced from mortars to burst over the parade. The fort was a target some 200 yards wide and a hundred yards deep, on that account; the Americans ran a bounty system for recovered British shot that could be fired back. Inside the traverses, the garrison held.

Relief brought the siege's catastrophe. On **May 5, 1813** — the bloodiest day of the siege, on the Association's history — some 1,200 Kentucky militia arrived along the Maumee. A detachment under Colonel William Dudley landed on the north bank, stormed the British batteries, and spiked the guns — the work it had been sent to do. Then, drawn into the woods after Tecumseh's warriors, the Kentuckians were ambushed in confused forest fighting and cut to pieces. The Association's history gives the day's cost in one figure: nearly 600 men lost to the combined force of British regulars, Canadian militia, and American Indian warriors. The American Battlefield Trust's account of the same day adds the scene at its end: prisoners in danger of being killed, and the killing stopped, on that account, only after Tecumseh intervened — forty Americans lay dead, the Trust states, in what became known as Dudley's Massacre. The numbers in these accounts do not agree with each other, and §6 prints the disagreement rather than a settled total. What is not disputed is the outcome: the fort held, Harrison's sortie against the battery on the fort's own side of the river succeeded, and on **May 9, 1813**, Proctor abandoned the siege and retreated toward Detroit.

The second siege, in **July 1813**, was a trick that failed. The fort was by then under General Green Clay. Instead of a bombardment, the Association's history states, Native warriors under Tecumseh hid in the woods and staged a mock battle — musket fire, war cries, and the sounds of a struggle — to fool the Americans into rushing out to a supposed relief column and into an ambush. The garrison, knowing no American force was near, stayed inside its walls. A strong thunderstorm then forced the British and their allies to withdraw a second time. The British moved on to Fort Stephenson, where their attack also failed.

After the second siege the great fort was deliberately shrunk: torn down, on the Association's history, and rebuilt smaller as a supply depot, garrisoned by 100 Ohio militiamen when the army marched north, standing until the end of the war and then abandoned. The Ohio History Connection's site history states the result already secured: the twice-successful defense ushered in a series of American victories, prevented further British incursions into Ohio, and bought the time to build the fleet that won on Lake Erie that September.

The ground was then kept, for a century, mostly by one family. In 1864, on the site histories' accounts, brothers Timothy and Thomas Hayes acquired the land and preserved it — grazing rather than plowing the earthworks, and showing the ground to visitors. The National Register nomination for the site records that in 1907 the Ohio Historical Society acquired part of the site, and that in 1908 the 82-foot-high Fort Meigs Monument — the granite obelisk that still stands inside the grounds — was erected by the Fort Meigs Commission; the published dedication volume dates the monument's dedication to **September 1, 1908**. The nomination further records a Pennsylvania Monument erected in 1923 in the cemetery area containing graves of the Pittsburgh Blues, and Ohio Historical Society management of the site from about 1931. The fort itself returned in the twentieth century: the Ohio History Connection's Ohio Memory account states that the Ohio Historical Society reconstructed the fort in the 1970s, with renovations in 2003, and that it stands today as the largest reconstructed wooden-walled fort in the United States — a claim about the reconstruction's scale that §6 examines, because the sources date the rebuilding itself differently.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a ten-acre log fort on a bluff sixty feet above the Maumee (Evers dedication volume) — seven blockhouses, five batteries, some twenty guns, and traverses across its interior, bombarded across the river for days in May 1813. **Now:** a full-size reconstruction on the same ground, its blockhouses holding exhibits on the two sieges (Ohio History Connection) — captioned everywhere in this draft as a reconstruction, not surviving 1813 fabric.

**Then:** a river that carried an army's supplies — Kentucky militia arriving by water on May 5, 1813, British batteries on the north bank, a British camp in the ruins of Fort Miami. **Now:** a 65-acre state memorial park on West River Road, the fort and museum inside it, and the river still running below the bluff.

**Then:** earthworks left to grass after the depot was abandoned, farmed in the site's southern half and grazed on the Hayes farm. **Now:** the reconstructed stockade on the site of the original fortification (nomination), with the 1908 obelisk, the Pennsylvania Monument of 1923, and the Museum and Education Center in the same park.

## 4. What's still there
- The reconstructed Fort Meigs: the full-size 10-acre replica of the 1813 fort within a 65-acre park (Ohio History Connection, Ohio Memory)
- Seven blockhouses and five artillery batteries in the reconstruction, matching the completed fort's April 1813 composition, with exhibits in the blockhouses on soldiers' lives, the fort's building, and the two sieges (Ohio History Connection)
- The traverses — earthen banks against cannon fire — represented in the reconstruction and named in the operator's visit description (Ohio History Connection)
- The Fort Meigs Monument: the 82-foot granite obelisk erected in 1908, dedicated September 1, 1908 (nomination; Evers dedication volume)
- The Pennsylvania Monument of 1923, in the cemetery area containing graves of the Pittsburgh Blues (National Register nomination)
- Surviving original earthworks of the 1813 fort, in part, distinguished in site accounts from the reconstructed stockade above them (National Register nomination)
- The Museum and Education Center: 14,000 square feet, with 3,000 square feet of exhibits on Ohio's role in the War of 1812 and artifacts recovered in excavation for the reconstruction (site histories; Ohio History Connection)
- The Maumee River bluff and rapids the fort was built to overlook and command (Fort Meigs Association history; Ohio History Connection)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Museum and Education Center:** Start with the exhibits the operator describes — soldiers' letters, uniforms, weapons, artifacts dug from this ground — and with the fort's intended job: a supply depot that became a defensive stand.
2. **Inside the stockade:** Walk the reconstruction as a plan, not a relic: seven blockhouses, five batteries, the quartermaster's building, and the traverses, each answering the May 1813 bombardment in §2.
3. **At a traverse:** Stand in the lee of an earthen bank and reckon with the Connection's figures — some 2,000 British rounds in five days, against a target 200 yards wide — and why the garrison dug across its own parade.
4. **At the 1908 obelisk:** Eighty-two feet of granite raised by a later generation, and the Pennsylvania Monument of 1923 by the Pittsburgh Blues' graves — the memorial layer between the war and the reconstruction.
5. **At the bluff edge:** The Maumee below, and the north bank opposite, where the British batteries stood and Dudley's men landed on May 5, 1813. The river is the reason for every wall behind you.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The losses of May 5, 1813, are printed here as a disagreement, because the sources consulted do not give one number. The Fort Meigs Association's history states that during the fighting on May 5, nearly 600 men were lost to a combined force of British regulars, Canadian militia, and American Indian warriors — a sentence whose "lost" is not broken down by side or by killed, wounded, and captured in that account. The American Battlefield Trust's account states that more than 600 Kentuckians were either killed, wounded, or captured that day, and that forty Americans lay dead in the killing of prisoners that followed, stopped, on that account, by Tecumseh's pleas. A further account of the siege carried in the reference summaries states that of Dudley's 866 officers and men, only 150 returned to the fort, and that nearly 550 were captured. These figures cannot all be the same count, and this draft prints all three phrasings, with their sources, and adopts none as a total.

The first siege's opening date is also stated two ways. The Fort Meigs Association's history and the Ohio History Connection's ordnance account both date the start of the bombardment to May 1, 1813, and the Association dates the British arrival and investment to late April. A reference summary of the siege dates the siege itself April 28–May 9, 1813, and describes an 11-day siege. Whether the siege is counted from the investment in late April or from the first shot on May 1 depends on the source; both conventions are printed here, and §2 narrates from the May 1 bombardment because the operator-side histories do.

The ground-breaking date carries a one-day variant. The Fort Meigs Association's history gives February 2, 1813, for the start of construction under Harrison's orders. An Ohio History Journal extract of Colonel John O'Fallon's 1840 speech — O'Fallon had been Harrison's aide — states that it was on the first day of February 1813 that Harrison's army pitched its tents on the ground where Fort Meigs was erected and commenced construction. February 2 (Association history) and February 1 (O'Fallon extract) are both printed; the draft does not resolve them.

The reconstruction's date is stated differently by the site's own sources. Ohio Memory states the Society reconstructed the fort in the 1970s, with renovations in 2003. Other site histories state the reconstruction was a late-1960s project and the museum opened in 1974; a University of Toledo thesis describes efforts culminating in the 1960s and 1970s. This draft prints the range — begun in the late 1960s, opened in 1974, described by Ohio Memory as a 1970s reconstruction, renovated 2000–2003 — and picks no single year. The "largest reconstructed wooden-walled fort in the United States" description is likewise printed as the Connection's own characterization, not an independently audited ranking.

No coordinates are printed in the locator below. Coordinates for Fort Meigs circulate in lead-list data derived from the National Register Information System, but no coordinate was verified for this draft from the nomination itself or from an agency asset record scoped to a defined point: the National Register record consulted carries the location line "1.3 mi. SW of Perrysburg" and no coordinate this draft can scope. Under this package's locator discipline the fields stay blank rather than borrow the lead-list point.

A final gap: the smaller depot fort is described by the Association's history as standing until the end of the war and then abandoned; one reference summary adds that it later burned. The burning is carried only as that summary's statement, because no operator or nomination source consulted confirms it.
