The main falls lot opens at 7 a.m. and closes at dark. There is no admission charge here. The boardwalk has a step-free approach. # Little River Canyon and the road above the falls

*On Old Ground · Alabama opening · Tier 1 · Labeled research draft · Reflection · Hunts DISABLED.*

## 2. Story

From the platform the river appears to divide the landscape neatly into two sides. The old roads tell a more particular story. The river flows across the top of Lookout Mountain. At the falls a sandstone ledge gives way to the canyon. A road approaching from the Cedar Bluff area crossed upstream of that break. The 2009 Alabama removal study places the crossing in its section on the Fort Lovell–Fort Payne roundup route. Its evidence includes nineteenth-century survey plats and maps alongside modern topography and a field photograph. The present bridge helps identify the stretch of river. The platform does not mark an exact ford. [2, 4, pp. 337–349, 5]

In 1838 soldiers and militia gathered Cherokee families in northeastern Alabama before their forced removal west. Mustering people at Fort Payne and moving a detachment west from the Wills Valley were different stages of the operation. The preserve’s interpretive account credits Cherokee conductor John Benge with leading the Fort Payne group and associates the group with the crossing above these falls. The detailed Alabama study treats the approach to the fort as a roundup road. Both records connect removal with this landscape. Their wording does not establish that everyone in the westbound detachment crossed at one spot on a single departure day. [4–6]

An original letter makes the logistics human. On September 29, 1838, John Benge, George C. Lowrey and George Lowrey wrote to Principal Chief John Ross about shoes, blankets and tents for the people leaving Wills Valley. The Gilcrease Museum describes three handwritten pages on one folded sheet. The National Register study traces the army’s threat to cut off rations, the departure of a first group with fifteen wagons, and the following groups still being assembled. It also identifies enslaved Black people among the travelers. There was no single morning on which everyone simply set out together. [7, 8, sec. E, pp. 3–4]

Another document survives in the same John Ross collection. It is an expense account for Benge’s detachment, dated May 18, 1840. The public catalog describes writing on both sides of the sheet. Read beside the September letter, it marks the distance between the urgent need for shoes on the ground and the accounting that continued afterward. The record does not say which traveler received a particular pair. [7–9]

The falls also powered a mill operated by the Hill family. NPS places it here, but its falls description does not identify machinery surviving on the platform approach. On October 21, 1992, Congress established Little River Canyon National Preserve in DeKalb and Cherokee counties and expressly included the protection of cultural resources among its purposes. That language helps explain why letters, roads and the working river belong beside the geological story. [2, 10]

## 3. Then and now

Water still passes over the ledge and enters the canyon. Today a public boardwalk ends at a viewing platform on the east side. NPS describes how rainfall changes the flow. The historic road, the documented mill and the modern visitor facility belong to different periods. The museum’s holding in Tulsa preserves the September letter. Its item record is available to a reader anywhere. [1, 2, 4, 7]

## 5. Reflection progression

The falls explain why a road used this part of the river. The letter explains what a map cannot show. Someone responsible for moving families under threat had to ask for shoes. Read that request alongside the later expense account. The documented human need is the strongest surviving connection to the landscape. No removal-route itinerary is assigned. [4, 7–9]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The NPS visitor page gives a 45-foot waterfall and a separate NPS place record gives 30 feet. Each describes Little River Falls. The sources do not explain the measuring points. The National Register study also distinguishes the departure dates reported by the conductors from an employee roll. The records support a staged departure. The precise order of passage at the falls and the ground-level position of the old crossing remain unresolved. The original letter’s catalog record and the published excerpts are available. A complete verified manuscript transcription was unavailable. Coordinates for the public platform remain blank pending two independent positions agreeing within about ten meters. [2, 4, 5, 7, 8]

Visitation signal for the Finder tier is **781,518 recreation visits in calendar 2025** for the entire preserve. NPS reports visits to the park unit. It does not report that many different people at this boardwalk. [11]

## 7. Historical photograph

Historical photo slot open. The 2009 Alabama report credits a field photograph of these falls to Lamar Marshall on printed page 348. Its credit does not establish item-level public-domain rights, and it documents the modern scene. The archive’s nineteenth-century manuscript is a different kind of record.
