# Jackson Square and St. Louis Cathedral — the Place d'Armes and its church
HT-RES-LA-jackson-square-st-louis-cathedral · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The square came first, as a plan. In August 1721 French engineers laid out the new colonial capital of La Louisiane at the Mississippi River bend Bienville had selected in 1718 with the assistance of local Native Americans who knew the site as one end of a portage between river, bayou and lake. The Historic New Orleans Collection's history of the square describes a fortified town on the latest engineering principles: a grid of streets around a central square fronting the river, the Place d'Armes, intended as the town's main public gathering place and, in the French fortification tradition the Collection quotes, the place where the great church, the town hall and the governor's house would open their principal doorways. Two L-shaped barracks were erected on either side to house colonial soldiers and their officers. For its first decades the square was a wide-open, grassy, often muddy mustering ground where militia drilled, governors inspected troops, crowds gathered after church and on ship arrivals — and where authorities also carried out public punishments and executions, a use the Collection's account includes and this dossier does not soften. [3]

A church has stood at the head of the square since the town's first years, in three buildings. The cathedral's own visit page gives its origins as dating to 1720 and calls it the home of the oldest continually active Roman Catholic cathedral community in the United States. The Preservation Resource Center's history is more specific about worship on the site: Mass has been celebrated there since 1727, in the first permanent church. That church was destroyed in the Great Fire of 1788. The Spanish-era church that replaced it was the work of Don Gilberto Guillemard, the French engineer in the Spanish king's service who also designed the Cabildo and the Presbytère flanking it; begun in 1789 in the usual dating, it was raised to cathedral rank in 1793 when the Diocese of New Orleans was erected, and dedicated in 1794. Benjamin Henry Latrobe, identified in the Preservation Resource Center account as America's first professional architect, gave the stucco-over-brick building a new clock tower in 1819. The bell from that 1819 tower, in the lead summaries inspected, was reused in the present building and remains there; the tower detail is carried here on the Preservation Resource Center's account, and the bell's survival is printed in the gaps as a lead-level claim. [2, 4]

The square was the stage for the transfers that changed the continent. The Collection records ceremonies on or near the Place d'Armes for the transfer of Louisiana from France to Spain in the 1760s and back to France in 1803, and quotes the French commissioner Pierre-Clément de Laussat on the American transfer that followed the signing in the Cabildo beside the square: spectators filled the balconies, the commissioners moved to the city hall's main balcony, the French colors were lowered and the American flag raised. The ground itself did not change hands in a document signed in the open air — the papers were signed in the Cabildo — but the square is where the flags, and the crowd, were. [3]

Eleven years later the square made the general whose name it now carries. In mid-December 1814, with British troops threatening the city in the last campaign of the War of 1812, Andrew Jackson met and reviewed local troops and volunteers in the Place d'Armes. The National Park Service account gives the large review to December 18, 1814: a procession of 6,000 troops entering the square to drums and applause, and Jackson's speech, translated by his aide Edward Livingston, urging men who differed in language and national pride to forget those differences against a common foe. The Collection adds who was in that muster: various companies of volunteers, including two battalions of free men of color, accepted as defenders of a shared home. The American victory at the Battle of New Orleans in January 1815 made Jackson a local hero, and the thanksgiving that followed belongs to the cathedral's continuing life as much as to the square's military one. [3, 5]

For a generation after the battle the old square declined. Latrobe in 1819 called the Place d'Armes infinitely superior to anything in the Atlantic cities as a water view, and drew a plan for enclosing it with decorative iron railings, gates and a fountain; the city council rejected it and implemented a cheaper scheme by city surveyor Joseph Pilié — a fence and a central fountain, never completely realized, with sycamore trees planted along either side. By the late 1840s the Baroness Micaela Almonester Pontalba, in the city to check her real estate holdings, found a muddy, neglected parade ground. She personally paid for extensive landscaping improvements and built the two Parisian-style rowhouse buildings — the Pontalba Buildings — on either side of the square at a cost of over $300,000. Her work, and the new mansard roofs and cupolas the city added to the Cabildo and Presbytère in 1847, changed the scale of the whole front. [3]

The cathedral was rebuilt to answer that new scale. The wardens had known since the 1830s that the Spanish cathedral was failing; funds were being gathered by 1838 for repairing or rebuilding it, and a design by the French-born architect Jacques Nicolas Bussière de Pouilly was approved in 1839, with drawings, specifications and agreements finalized only in February 1849. De Pouilly's adopted design was modern French Romantic work: a mélange of Greek Revival, Gothic and Renaissance motifs in one homogenous whole — Gothic spires and towers, a Renaissance central section, and a framework of arches, pilasters and ornament drawn from classical architecture — with a distinctive central spire of open wood and wrought-iron framework, slated over in 1859. The contract of March 12, 1849 with builder John Patrick Kirwan was called a restoration. It became a new church. The wardens had hoped to keep Guillemard's side and front walls; the New Orleans Weekly Delta reported in June 1849 that the side walls were cracked and out of line, a supplemental contract demolished them, and the building was widened to its present size. In January 1850 the central tower, still being erected, collapsed, taking part of the roof and severely damaging the new walls. An expert panel, in the Preservation Resource Center's account, laid the fault on the contractor, but de Pouilly was dismissed along with Kirwan, his reputation tarnished, and the work was completed to his design under Alexander Sampson's superintendence in the archdiocesan account. Very little of the Spanish colonial structure survived. Archbishop Antoine Blanc blessed the new cathedral on December 7, 1851. Contemporary reaction was divided: the design was generally acclaimed, and detractors called the exterior garish and mourned the venerable relic it replaced. [4, 6]

The square took its present name in the same season. In January 1851 the renovated Place d'Armes was officially renamed Jackson Square, and a committee began raising funds for a monument. Sculptor Clark Mills received the commission; his first Jackson statue, erected in Washington in 1853, was the first equestrian statue created by an American and the first anywhere to balance a horse unsupported on its rear legs. His second casting, for New Orleans, was unveiled in February 1856 before a reported 10,000 onlookers. The National Park Service's shorter account dates the renaming differently — to 1851, when, it says, the current statue was erected — and the discrepancy is printed below: the statue in the square is an 1856 unveiling in the Collection's detailed account, and 1851 is the renaming year in both accounts. [3, 5]

The cathedral's later distinctions are brief in the sources inspected and are stated only as far as they go. It was designated a minor basilica by Pope Paul VI in 1964, and Pope John Paul II visited in September 1987; the promenaded block of Chartres Street in front of it now carries the name Place John Paul II. Those two facts are carried here on lead-level summaries and are flagged in the gaps for verification against archdiocesan records in a future pass. What is firm from the cathedral's own pages is the present life: daily and Sunday Mass, the archbishop's cathedral and an active parish, open to visitors daily within stated hours, closed to touring at times for weddings and special events, with exclusive-access tours offered Monday–Saturday at 9 a.m. before general opening in its current visit copy. [2, 7]

Today the square the city manages is 2.5 acres, bound by Chartres and St. Ann, Decatur and St. Peter, seeing over two million tourists and locals a year in the city's count, designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960 — the city gives the landmark number as 66000375 — and chosen in 2012 by the American Planning Association as one of its Great Places in America. The Collection closes its history on the same ground the engineers drew: for all the people and history that have passed through it, the square still occupies the same modest two-and-a-half acres laid out three centuries ago. The cathedral, the Cabildo and the Presbytère still front it as a set; the Pontalba Buildings still flank it; the Jackson statue still stands at its center; and the river it was laid out to face still runs beyond Decatur Street, behind the levee that now blocks the open view Latrobe praised. [1, 3]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The Place d'Armes of 1721, at the riverfront head of the French Quarter grid in New Orleans, Orleans Parish — mustering ground, transfer-ceremony stage, Jackson's December 1814 review ground, renamed Jackson Square in January 1851. St. Louis Cathedral stands at the square's head on Chartres Street, between the Cabildo and the Presbytère, on the site of the churches of 1727 and 1789–1794; the present cathedral is the 1849–1851 building. [1, 3, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Jackson Square, City of New Orleans Parks and Parkways, bound by Chartres and St. Ann Streets, Decatur Street and St. Peter Street, New Orleans; and St. Louis Cathedral, 615 Pere Antoine Alley in the address used by visitor sources — the cathedral's public front is on the square itself, and its own pages give no street address in the text inspected, so the square frontage governs for wayfinding. Use the square's gated garden within city park hours and the cathedral interior within its visitor hours, outside services and special events. The surrounding pedestrian streets, the artists' colony along the fence, Washington Artillery Park and the riverfront across Decatur Street are separate public ground and are not part of this dossier's gate. [1, 2]

## 5. Public progression

Enter the square from Decatur Street in park hours and stand at the river side first, facing the front the engineers intended: cathedral at center, Cabildo and Presbytère as its pair, Pontalba Buildings closing the sides, Jackson at the center of the garden behind you as you turn. Walk to the statue and read the ground in order — 1721 parade ground, 1803 flags, December 18, 1814 review — then cross to the cathedral and go in within visitor hours, quietly, as a guest in an active church: what you are standing in is de Pouilly's 1851 building, modern in its day, on the ground where Mass has been said since 1727, and almost none of the colonial church survives inside it. Finish back in the garden and look at the whole front once more as an 1851 composition — renamed square, new statue ground, heightened flanking buildings, rebuilt cathedral — laid over a 1721 plan that never moved. **Strongest story moment · December 18, 1814.** Six thousand troops in the square, a speech in translation urging men of different languages to stand together, and two battalions of free men of color among the volunteers for a city that would not grant them equality — the square held the whole contradiction in one muster, a month before the battle that named it. Reflection posture applies: this is battlefield-adjacent and civic ground, read with dignity and without scoring language. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Cathedral origins dating.** The cathedral's visit page dates its origins to 1720; the Preservation Resource Center dates Mass on the site to 1727; lead summaries variously give a first wooden church in 1718 and a first permanent church completed in 1727. The 1727 worship date is the one carried in the story, on the Preservation Resource Center's account; 1718 and 1720 are printed here as unverified variants, and no founding-year claim beyond them is made. [2, 4]
- **Renaming and statue dates in the NPS account.** The National Park Service page says the square was known as the Place d'Armes until 1851, when the current statue was erected and the name changed. The Historic New Orleans Collection's detailed account separates the events: renaming in January 1851, Mills's Washington casting in 1853, the New Orleans unveiling in February 1856. The Collection's sequence is followed in the story; the NPS compression is printed here so a reader matching that page is not misled. [3, 5]
- **Tower collapse month.** The Preservation Resource Center gives January 1850 for the central tower's collapse. Lead summaries agree on 1850 without a month. January 1850 is carried on the Preservation Resource Center's account alone. [4]
- **Basilica and papal-visit facts.** Designation as a minor basilica by Pope Paul VI in 1964 and the September 1987 visit of Pope John Paul II are carried in this pass on lead-level summaries only; no archdiocesan or Vatican record was opened, and both are flagged for verification before any publication. The Place John Paul II name for the Chartres Street promenade is likewise lead-level here. [7]
- **1819 bell.** The reuse of Latrobe's 1819 tower bell in the present building is stated in lead summaries; the Preservation Resource Center account verifies the 1819 Latrobe clock tower but not the bell's survival in the text inspected. Printed as a lead, not a verified feature. [4, 7]
- **Cathedral address.** Visitor sources give 615 Pere Antoine Alley; the cathedral's own visit page inspected in this pass prints no street address. The alley address is recorded in the locator as source-given, with the square frontage as the governing wayfinding. [2, 7]
- **Cathedral visitor hours conflict.** The cathedral's official visit page gives 9:30 a.m.–4:00 p.m., last visitors 3:45 p.m. Secondary visitor guides give longer or different hours. The official page governs and is the only schedule printed in the trip gate. [2]
- **Jackson Square landmark date.** The city page gives National Landmark status in 1960 and number 66000375, without a day. Lead summaries give October 9, 1960. The day is not printed as verified in this pass. [1]
- **Haunted lore.** Tourism copy attaches ghost lore to the cathedral and square, including tales of clergy burials and chanting. No contemporary or archdiocesan source for any such claim was retrieved in this pass; the lore is recorded here only to be kept out of the documented record, and it has no place in the progression. The cathedral is an active place of worship and is treated as such throughout.
