## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** a port is not simply a place where a boat appears. It is where one person's arrival depends on other people's repeated physical work, and where an outward journey may become a return journey. The interpretive question is who disappears when a river economy is reduced to a picture of a steamboat.

**Named primary voice:** Leonard Whiting Gilchrist kept an 1866 journal while traveling aboard the *Agnes* toward Fort Benton. James E. Potter published an edited version in *Nebraska History* 58 (1977), pages 266–300. The edition's introduction says Gilchrist briefly built flatboats at Fort Benton for returning emigrants and miners. That employment claim is Potter's editorial biography, not a surviving work order examined here. [4]

In the June 26 entry, discussing the journey at Cow Island rather than work on the Fort Benton levee, Gilchrist writes: **“The crews of both boats were employed in gathering up the scanty wood that is here to be found.”** The wording on printed page 296 was visually checked against the downloaded scan. [4]

**Original visitor narration:** The picture invites us to watch the boat. The sentence asks us to notice the crews. A machine can dominate a photograph while the people sustaining it become almost invisible. Read the sentence again without imagining a heroic captain at its center. What changes when the work, rather than the vehicle, becomes the subject?

This interpretation does not establish individual crew members' names, wages or working hours. It also does not turn Gilchrist into an employee of the *Agnes*. He is the named witness; the crew members in the quoted observation remain unnamed. The named witness's later building work and the observed crews' work are different kinds of evidence.

**Geographic and chronological limit:** the journal ends June 28, two days before the edition dates the vessel's arrival at Fort Benton. It cannot be presented as Gilchrist's firsthand diary of unloading there on June 30. [4] Cow Island is source context only, not a second itinerary stop or mapped target.

The edition contains outsider descriptions of Native people. They are not adopted as an authoritative Tribal voice. This narrowly scoped labor draft does not provide a comprehensive account of Indigenous trade, sovereignty or displacement; that requires appropriate Tribal-published interpretation before expansion. No sacred-place search is proposed.

The Gilchrist journal describes work within a voyage. S. J. Morrow’s 1872 photograph of the *Nellie Peck* supplies the view from shore. Start with the piles of freight in the foreground. The vessel has brought the goods upriver, but their journey overland still requires hands, wagons and time. In the same book, the *Nile* buys cordwood from Beidler and Johnson during its 1869 Fort Benton voyage. The two accounts expose different jobs usually credited to a single captain. [5]

The shallower *Chippewa* accompanied the deeper *Spread Eagle* on the 1859 expedition. The university study distinguishes the clerk tracking goods from the eighty deck hands who left St. Louis aboard the *Spread Eagle*. In 1860 the *Chippewa* and *Key West No. 2* reached Fort Benton on July 2. The eighty belong to the earlier departure, not the later landing. [6]

## 3. Then / now
- **Then:** a travel journal records what its author noticed. It is not a payroll, crew roster or complete port ledger.
- **Then, a separate evidentiary layer:** an editor supplies biographical context for the witness. The reader must be able to distinguish that layer from the dated entries.
- **Now:** a public interpretive building can offer an accessible place to read and discuss such evidence when its operator actually opens it. A functioning historical destination and a currently closed visitor door can coexist.

Original comparison prompt: *What work would a pretty picture leave out?* This can be answered from a documented text at home. It does not require locating a lost worksite or claiming to stand where a particular crew stood.
