# Spearfish hatchery workers and the spring supply

## 2. The story

### A hatchery runs on dependable water

The federal crew did more than tend eggs. In the commissioner’s printed 1902 report, the Spearfish entry headed with Superintendent D. C. Booth’s name records “a decrease in the volume of the spring-water supply.” The station used its regular force when people could be spared from fish work and hired temporary help to develop the springs. The contributor’s credit in the heading does not identify the individual who wrote each sentence. [3]

The Service says the site was chosen for cold springs high enough to feed the hatchery by gravity, plus access to the regional railroad. When the federal government accepted the hatchery building and icehouse in July 1899, the crew still had to make trays, set troughs and install faucets before receiving eggs. The agency’s chronology records that 100,000 eggs were being incubated by July 29. It also describes a storm that sent refuse from the old sawmill operation into the spring reservoir and through the ponds. Limestone and cement channels, bridges and bulkheads became part of the response. [4]

### The same workers had to keep two kinds of water apart

In the 1902 report the crew describes building a stone bulkhead 82 feet long across the canyon outlet and connecting a new channel to earlier storm drainage. Even incomplete, the channel carried off ordinary runoff. The station credited keeping surface water away from the hatchery supply with improving the proportion of eggs that hatched. At the same time the crew had to increase the spring flow that fed the ponds. The stonework a visitor sees is evidence of moving unwanted water away and carrying useful water where it was needed. Individual surviving walls still need identification before they can be called the 82-foot bulkhead. [3,4]

### The work continued between stations

The report describes eggs from Yellowstone being packed in refrigerator cases, carried 74 miles by wagon to Cinnabar, Montana, and sent by rail to Spearfish. It reports 382,000 fry distributed or planted from that sequence. Its separate figures for eggs held at Yellowstone, transport batches and fry cannot be used as a verified survival rate for one shipment. The visitor railcar is a replica used to interpret this transport system. [2,3]

## 3. Then / now

The building now used as a museum was built for hatchery work. Public paths pass the ponds and surviving water-system features. Federal archives now preserve fisheries records and equipment, and the Service describes continuing production. Early rail and wagon logistics are interpreted in the replica car. [1–4]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Use the signed hatchery entrance from town and start on the public pond path. [1,2]
2. Compare pond edges and water-control masonry with the operator’s 1899 and early-1900s views. If staffing permits, visit the museum and replica railcar. [1,2,4]
3. **Strongest story moment — The water they needed and the water they fought.** Read the spring-supply passage beside the ponds and look for the difference between a holding basin and a channel carrying water away. Use the federal photograph and report from home during an exterior closure. [3,4]

## 7. Photo record

- FWS’s 1899 hatching-room photograph is credited to the National Fish & Aquatic Conservation Archives. The record identifies the room and the troughs. The historic room setup cannot be matched to an established public standing point from the evidence retrieved. No Photo Point. [4]
- FWS’s other 1899 photograph places earthen raceways in front of the hatchery and icehouse. The site history places those raceways south of the hatchery, but the raceways are poor modern control points. No frame-specific Photo Point. [4]
- Early-1900s FWS pond image. **APPROXIMATE Photo Point.** The relative position is on the pond side south of the 1899 hatchery, facing generally north toward the hatchery and the cliff behind it. The surviving building is the far anchor. The stone-walled ponds and the bridge between ponds are near anchors, and the FWS site history locates the ponds south of the building. Confidence moderate for the side and general facing, low for the exact camera spot. Compare only from signed public walks. Field verification required. [1,4]
