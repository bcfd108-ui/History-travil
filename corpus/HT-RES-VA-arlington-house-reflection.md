# HT-RES-VA-arlington-house-reflection — The house above the river, kept by everyone it displaced
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Reflection only. This entry covers Arlington House, The Robert E. Lee Memorial, as the National Park Service presents it — the mansion, its two surviving quarters for enslaved people, the museum, and the grounds the Park Service holds inside Arlington National Cemetery. The cemetery around the house is a burial ground in active use; it is not treated here as scenery, and no part of this entry directs a visitor toward any grave.

## 1. Trip gate

**Arlington House, The Robert E. Lee Memorial** is a National Park Service unit, administered through the George Washington Memorial Parkway, on a ridge above the Potomac inside Arlington National Cemetery in Arlington County, Virginia. The Park Service lists its address as **Arlington House, The Robert E. Lee Memorial, Arlington National Cemetery, Arlington, VA 22211**. [1]

The Park Service's Plan Your Visit page states the terms as printed here: **all buildings at Arlington House are free and open to the public, and no tickets are required.** The same page directs visitors to the Arlington National Cemetery website for current conditions and how to enter the cemetery, because the house is reached through the cemetery's own entrance and screening. [1] The Park Service's operating-hours listing gives the house and grounds, the museum, and the North and South quarters as open daily from **9:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m.**, last entry into the house at 4:00 p.m., closed on New Year's Day, Thanksgiving Day, and Christmas Day. Conditions inside the cemetery can change those hours. [1]

## 2. The story

In 1778 John Parke Custis — Martha Washington's son and George Washington's stepson — purchased 1,100 acres across the Potomac from the future site of Washington. On his death in 1781 the land passed in trust to his infant son, **George Washington Parke Custis**, raised largely at Mount Vernon by George and Martha Washington. [3]

Custis began his house in **1802**, on the estate's highest ground, and built it in stages until **1818**. The Park Service's history pages state what it was for: both his residence and the nation's first memorial to his adoptive grandfather, George Washington, and the repository of hundreds of Washington relics from Mount Vernon. [4] Its great east portico — a Greek temple front scaled to be seen from the capital — is that purpose in architecture; the Historic American Buildings Survey (HABS VA-443) calls the house one of the earliest and most important Greek Revival examples in the United States. [2]

The house was not built by its owner alone, and the Park Service's current interpretation says so at the front of its account. Over the sixty years before the Civil War, Arlington was home to nearly 100 enslaved African Americans — its telling elsewhere puts the figure as at least 100. Fifty-seven enslaved workers were brought to the plantation in 1802 and forced to begin building Custis's house. The Park Service names the families it now interprets alongside the Custis and Lee families: the **Syphax, Burke, Parks, and Gray** families — Charles Syphax oversaw the dining room, Selina Gray was the housekeeper, Daniel Dotson the carriage driver — and enslaved people at Arlington married and raised children there, in marriages the law did not recognize. [3][5]

One family carries the estate's deepest entanglement. Maria Carter Syphax, born enslaved at Arlington in 1803 to Arianna Carter, was, as the Park Service's land-claims account states, Custis's daughter by an enslaved woman — a relationship in which, the Park Service writes, enslavement negates the possibility of consent. She grew up as maid to her half-sister Mary Anna; in 1825 Custis gave her 17 acres in the estate's southwestern corner, later called Syphax Corner, and an 1866 act for her relief confirmed her family's hold on it. [3]

In **1831** Mary Anna married Second Lieutenant **Robert E. Lee** in the house's parlor, and for thirty years the house was the Lees' home. When Custis died in **1857**, the estate passed to Mary Custis Lee, with Lee as executor. Custis's will directed that the people he enslaved be freed once his granddaughters' legacies were paid and the estates clear of debt, and in any case within five years. Lee kept them enslaved for the full five years. His manumission deed as executor is dated **December 29, 1862**, recorded at Richmond on January 2, 1863, and names the people freed at Arlington, among them Charles Syphax, Selina and Thornton Gray and their children, and the Parks family. The Park Service adds the sentence the document alone does not carry: freedom for the people enslaved at Arlington ultimately came with the Civil War. [5][6]

In April 1861 Virginia seceded. Lee resigned his Army commission on April 20, 1861, and left Arlington two days later; in May the family departed, and the Army crossed the Potomac and occupied the estate. The Park Service's occupation account gives the reason plainly: the views that made the house a landmark made it a strategic concern, less than three miles from the White House, within range of a rifled cannon. The Army turned the plantation into a stronghold and cleared its forests so completely that Selina Gray wrote after the war that there was not a tree to be seen except in the cemetery. The first commanders undertook to protect the house and its relics — when Gray told General Irvin McDowell that a locked cellar room of Washington relics had been broken into, McDowell took the artifacts into his own custody — but Mary Custis Lee still wrote that it had never occurred to her she could be forced to sue for permission to enter her own house. [7]

In **May 1863** the Army chose a second use for the estate. Washington's camps for people who had escaped or been freed from slavery were overwhelmed after the Emancipation Proclamation, and on May 5, 1863, Lieutenant Colonel Elias M. Greene and Danforth B. Nichols selected the estate, a half mile south of the mansion, as the site of **Freedmen's Village**, dedicated that December. The Park Service's museum account puts some 2,000 newly free men and women there at its growth; it had homes, churches, a school, and a hospital, and the Park Service does not hide the intended symbolism: a community for freedpeople on the Confederate general's estate was, to many in the North, appealing precisely because of whose land it was. After the war the government tried repeatedly to close it; residents resisted and petitioned — John Syphax petitioned in 1888 that residents had a valid claim to part of Arlington — and the village endured until **1900**. [8][9]

The third use came in 1864, and it surrounds the house today. The estate had been taken for taxes: Mary Custis Lee, unable to appear in person as the tax law was administered, sent payment by an agent and was refused; the tax due was $92.07, and in January 1864 the government bought the estate at auction for $26,800. That spring, with Washington and Alexandria burial grounds overwhelmed, Quartermaster General **Montgomery C. Meigs** — a Georgian who had stayed with the Union, served under Lee before the war, and regarded Lee's course as betrayal — proposed Arlington. The first burial was made on **May 13, 1864**; on **June 15, 1864**, Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton approved 200 acres as a military cemetery. Burials were placed close around the house itself; the Park Service's memorial history states the purpose plainly — the Army made the grounds a cemetery in an effort to ensure the Lee family would be unable to return. [10][11]

The law then reversed the taking. George Washington Custis Lee sued for the property in 1874. In **December 1882** the Supreme Court ruled in his favor in *United States v. Lee*, holding the estate had been taken without due process. In **March 1883** the government purchased it from him for **$150,000**, and the graves stayed where they were. [10]

The house's public life followed. A joint resolution of Congress in **1925** authorized the War Department's restoration of the mansion as a memorial to Lee. In **1933** stewardship passed to the National Park Service. In **1955** Congress formally recognized the restored home, then called the Custis-Lee Mansion, as a national memorial to Robert E. Lee, and in **1972** it was renamed **Arlington House, The Robert E. Lee Memorial**. A major rehabilitation completed in 2021 installed new exhibits carrying the enslaved families' stories as part of the house's central interpretation. [4][10]

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a 1,100-acre working plantation on a wooded ridge, built and run by enslaved labor, its temple-fronted house holding the Washington relics and facing the capital. **Now:** a memorial holding of a fraction of that acreage — the Park Service's rehabilitation reporting puts the present site at 16 acres — inside a national cemetery that grew out of the estate's wartime taking. What a visitor stands in is the fraction, not the plantation. [2][12]

**Then:** two brick quarters west of the main house, homes of the Gray family and other enslaved people, defining the work yard. **Now:** both quarters stand restored, the South holding a museum exhibit on the enslaved people of Arlington and their descendants' community. [9]

**Then:** a temporary camp of freedpeople a half mile south of the mansion, founded May 1863 and meant to close when the war ended. **Now:** no village stands; it was cleared in 1900, its ground within the cemetery and its surroundings. What remains is the Park Service's account of it, and Section 27 of the cemetery, where more than 3,000 village residents and other formerly enslaved people were buried under headstones marked, in the Army's word of the time, "civilian" — named here as a fact of the record, not as a destination. [8][13]

## 4. What's still there

All of the following is the memorial's own public ground, as the Park Service presents it:

- **The mansion (1802–1818)** — Custis's Greek Revival house, built as his residence and the first memorial to George Washington, with its monumental east portico; the Lees' home 1831–1861; restored under the 1925 joint resolution, held by the Park Service since 1933, rehabilitated most recently in 2021. [4][10]
- **The North and South quarters** — the surviving brick quarters for enslaved people west of the main house, built contemporaneously with it (about 1803–1818, per HABS VA-443-A); the South quarters now hold the museum exhibit on enslavement and freedom at Arlington. [9][14]
- **The grounds and Arlington Woods** — the remnant of the estate around the house, including the flower garden ground east of it, and the museum exhibits installed in the 2021 rehabilitation. [12]
- **The view** — the ridge prospect over the Potomac to the National Mall that made the house a memorial in 1802, a military necessity in 1861, and a cemetery in 1864. [4][7]
- **Excluded, by this entry's own rule:** the graves of Arlington National Cemetery as visitor content. The cemetery is the house's setting and its consequence, and an active burial ground; this entry treats it as context, never as an itinerary.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

The public anchor is the house itself, reached through Arlington National Cemetery's entrance, as the Park Service directs, and then uphill to the ridge. The visit is self-guided through the house's public rooms, the museum, and the two quarters, with ranger talks. Begin at the east front, because the building begins there: the portico was built to be read from Washington, and standing under it reverses the intended view — the capital lies below, framed the way Custis framed it for the city, not for himself. [1][2][4]

From the front, the route runs through the family rooms and Lee's office, then west to the work yard, where the visit changes register: the South quarters' exhibit carries the people the front rooms were built on — the Grays, Syphaxes, Parkses, and Burkes, named, with their work and their lives after 1863. [5][9]

End on the grounds, with the whole sequence in view at once: the memorial house of 1818, the quarters beside it, the village ground to the south that no longer stands, and the cemetery that began, in the Park Service's own words, as a means of keeping the house's family from ever coming home. Nothing in this progression leaves the memorial's public ground or requires approaching any grave, and it can be shortened at any point. [1]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The Park Service's own pages date the house's memorial designation two ways, and both are printed rather than merged. Its Basic Information page states that in 1925 the United States designated Arlington House as a national memorial to Robert E. Lee. Its memorial history page gives the fuller sequence: a 1925 joint resolution authorized restoring the mansion as a memorial to Lee; Congress formally recognized the home as a national memorial to Lee in 1955; and the Custis-Lee Mansion was renamed Arlington House, The Robert E. Lee Memorial in 1972. This entry follows the fuller sequence in §2 and prints the compressed 1925 statement here as the conflict it is. [10][15]

The name itself remains a live question in public discussion. Proposals to redesignate the site as a national historical site, without Lee's name in its title, have been advanced by descendants of both the Lee family and the enslaved families and supported by preservation organizations. No Park Service source consulted announces or enacts such a change; the Park Service continues to carry the site as Arlington House, The Robert E. Lee Memorial, and this entry uses that name. [16]

The count of the enslaved community is carried two ways in the Park Service's telling: its history overview says nearly 100 enslaved African Americans over the sixty years before the Civil War; its enslaved-people account says at least 100. The December 1862 manumission deed names 63 people freed at Arlington at that date. The figures measure different things and are printed as their sources state them, not reconciled. [4][5][6]

Freedmen's Village also carries two closing dates in retellings — 1888, the year of John Syphax's petition, appears in some accounts as if it were the end. The Park Service's accounts are consistent that the village survived the 1887–1888 closure attempt and endured until 1900; this entry follows the Park Service. The Army's own history summary gives the village's span as 1863 to 1888 on the 17.5 acres associated with the Syphax claim, which appears to describe the Syphax parcel rather than the village's full life; the disagreement is printed, not resolved. [8][9][13]

## 8. Reflection

**Reflection stop only. No hunts, scoring, answer keys, hidden targets or reenactments.**

Candidate reflection: The portico was built to be seen from the capital, by a man memorializing Washington with other people's labor. The graves were placed to be seen from the portico, by a government keeping the house's family from returning. What does the view from this ridge ask of a visitor standing where both intentions can be held in sight at once?
