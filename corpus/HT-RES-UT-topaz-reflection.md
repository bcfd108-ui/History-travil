## 2. The story
Start with the family, not the camp grid. **Seiji Aizawa**, in **“A Family Torn Apart,” as told to Ann Tamaki Dion**, remembered his father's December 1941 arrest and the loss of communication. His mother, siblings and he were later confined at Tanforan and Topaz, while his father was detained elsewhere. When his mother and sister joined his father at Crystal City, Seiji and his brother **Hatsuro** stayed at Topaz to finish school. His summary is spare: **“so our family remained separated.”** He recalled unexpectedly meeting his brother while traveling to visit their parents before college. This is a retrospective, community-published firsthand account, not a contemporaneous transcript or an official case file. [3]

**Ann Tamaki Dion's own 2019 account** explains another generation's relationship to Topaz. As a child she resisted a family visit; as an adult she returned with a different understanding and helped form the Friends of Topaz Committee. Editor **Ruth Sasaki** describes the story project's purpose as preserving varied survivor and descendant voices. Their role matters: this is not merely a museum speaking about a community from outside it. [4]

**Interpretive conclusion:** being counted in a camp population does not mean a family was together. A visit should make the difference between a household on a form and people living with separation legible. Do not make college departure a simple happy ending or imply that a brief reunion undid the government's actions.

## 3. Then / now
- **1942–45:** the operator dates Topaz's operation September 11, 1942–October 31, 1945. It was a War Relocation Authority confinement center; different detention systems must not be flattened into a single institution. [1]
- **1982:** the museum's history begins its local collecting narrative with **Jane Beckwith's Delta High journalism students**, who interviewed people who had worked at Topaz. That origin does not replace survivor testimony. [2]
- **2015 / 2017:** the community project's history distinguishes the museum's 2015 opening and 2017 grand opening. These are different milestones rather than an assumed contradiction. [4]
- **Present:** the public museum and community-published stories offer a way to learn without traversing the incarceration ground. The story can remain meaningful indoors; proximity to a surviving foundation is not required.

The historic camp and the present museum serve different purposes and have different visitor boundaries. A recreated room, artwork or object, if encountered, should be read with its label and provenance, not treated as proof that the whole institutional experience has been reproduced.

## 4. What's still there
**Public museum:** the operator describes a core exhibition using artifacts, photographs and personal accounts to examine exclusion, incarceration and civil liberties. This verifies a public interpretive anchor, not the display of the particular Aizawa story or any named family object on the day of arrival. [2]

**Community record:** the Topaz Stories project preserves accounts from survivors and descendants. Its about page distinguishes oral retellings, submitted narratives and excerpts from memoirs. That variety requires source-by-source attribution rather than calling everything an oral-history recording. [4]

**Excluded:** historic camp roads and deposits, monuments associated with death, burial areas, privately reused buildings, staff rooms, collection storage and private homes. No public walking or driving permission is inferred from a historic photograph, surviving structure or online tour link.

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Before departure:** confirm the museum door and conditions for the intended day. Bring the specific story link rather than searching for an outdoor pin.
2. **Begin inside public visitor areas:** establish that Topaz was a place of forced confinement, not a voluntary settlement. Follow the museum's interpretation and current staff directions.
3. **Read one family account:** use Seiji's published story with its named teller and reteller. Do not substitute a fictional composite child or ask visitors to impersonate family members.
4. **Look carefully at an available labeled object:** distinguish what its provenance actually establishes from what a visitor imagines. Do not promise an Aizawa possession or interview station is on view.
5. **End with the family relationship:** reflect on what separation meant for ordinary decisions such as school, travel and seeing a parent. The strongest moment is understanding that administrative categories did not describe a whole human life.

This is an editorial progression within an authorized museum visit, not an activated route. No confinement simulation, staged arrest, suitcase challenge, scavenger game, points or completion proof.
