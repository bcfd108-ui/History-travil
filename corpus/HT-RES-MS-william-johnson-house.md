# William Johnson House — who laid the brick

Labeled research draft · Regional March · Mississippi OP01 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

NPS pages disagree on hours, so confirm with the park before visiting, especially Sunday or around noon. Thanksgiving, Christmas Day and New Year’s Day are closed. Use the signed public entrance and avoid travel during severe weather. [1–2]

## 2. Story

William Johnson’s diary follows the work of making a house as closely as some people follow the finished facade. The NPS landscape report transcribes one short entry, “Phill works to day at the Building.” Nearby diary entries record Johnson setting Stephen and Phillip to work and dealing with a builder named Brown. Johnson also spent time acquiring and cleaning salvaged brick. The paper trail makes the brick wall a record of labor and financial decisions. Johnson’s own voice survives. We can name Stephen and Phillip through his account without claiming that he records their experience in their own words. [3]

Born enslaved and freed while young, Johnson trained as a barber. He built a business in Natchez and kept a diary covering much of his adult life. His papers survive together with family and business records. NPS and LSU describe a household that owned property and enslaved other people. These documented aspects of Johnson’s life belong in the same account. The diary captures what he thought worth recording and what having a home, shops and credit meant to him; it is especially useful when read alongside the identities and labor it reveals only indirectly. [3–6]

The house still stands as a material counterpart to that paper record. Read the ground-floor interpretation and look at the three-bay brick front from a lawful sidewalk. That combination makes it possible to read the building even while the family quarters upstairs remain unavailable. [1–3, 7–9]

## 3. Then and now

Then — Johnson’s 1840s notes record labor, materials and negotiations while the house and grounds were taking shape. The adjacent building is a separate structure. [3, 7]

Now — The NPS uses a signed entrance and a ground-floor visitor center and exhibit hall. The upper floor remains closed under the park operating-hours notice. The historic photograph documents the front during a federal survey in 1992. The present direction is governed by the posted signs, regardless of the historic image. [1–3, 7–9]

## 5. Public progression

Start at the signed ground-floor door. Read the exhibits if open. View the brick elevation from a lawful State Street sidewalk, weather permitting. Use the archive links remotely if the public interior is closed. [1–3]

**Strongest story moment — Phillip at the building.** Pause with the diary line and the brickwork in view. The person who owned the house recorded a person who worked on it; the building preserves the work more visibly than the worker’s own words. [3, 7–9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Park-level hours say daily 8:30–5 with upstairs closed; the activity entry says Monday–Saturday 9–5 with a midday closure and Sunday noon–5, and still describes an upstairs self-guided visit. This draft takes the more restrictive access notice and asks readers to confirm public hours. [1–2]
- The quote is the NPS landscape report’s printed transcription from the published diary. The original diary page was not independently inspected here. The archive is a source and does not form part of the visit. [3–6]
- The earliest physical chronology within the building and identification of the exact salvaged bricks remain open. The image establishes survey-era appearance. [3, 7–9]
