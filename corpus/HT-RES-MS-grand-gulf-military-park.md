# Grand Gulf and the difference between holding a fort and holding a river

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP16*

## 2. Story

On April 29, 1863, a Union naval force led by David D. Porter attempted to silence Confederate river batteries at Grand Gulf. The plan required more than bringing boats within sight of the town. Union infantry under John A. McClernand waited aboard transports and barges for a usable landing. Fort Wade’s lower batteries fell silent in the NPS account, but Fort Cobun’s position remained effective. Crews aboard the ironclads kept working under fire. The transports passed the batteries later, and Union troops landed downstream at Bruinsburg. That sequence explains how Confederate soldiers could hold their position through the bombardment while Union forces still established a crossing. [4][5]

John S. Bowen wrote while the action was unfolding. “I cannot tell the effect of our shots,” he reported early on April 29. Hours later he estimated the duration and reported that Colonel William Wade, his artillery officer, had been killed at his post. Bowen recognized his men’s continuing work after the firing, and next morning asked for more ammunition and bags. Those are transcribed official dispatches, with the first one’s limits intact. The NPS sequence, which draws on a later view of the whole operation, can describe boats and effects he could not yet measure. [4–6]

Another kind of work made the physical story readable. Historical archaeologist William C. Wright led successive excavations at the lower and upper positions. Miller L. Brock, Marc C. Hammack and others worked alongside student and volunteer crews. Site administrator Phillip Cox helped with both projects. The state reports document the Confederate magazine at Fort Wade and the upper battery as distinct archaeological investigations. The museum opened in 1967, after the park's 1962 dedication, and the operator says part of the exhibit space is devoted to artifacts and maps. Read the photographs, plans and excavated objects within their stated contexts in that room. [1][2][7][8]

## 3. Then and now

A wartime river position could command a landing then in use near the town. The modern operator explains that the wartime river ran below the Fort Cobun bluff. The park now contains separated historic earthworks and town remains, an admissions museum and a cemetery. The museum is the sole visitor anchor for this draft. Its interpretive maps document earlier water and land relationships. [1][2][4][7–9]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

From the signed museum entrance, read the town displays and then the battle exhibits within the public rooms. **Strongest story moment — Two batteries, one crossing.** Compare the exhibit's two positions with Bowen’s dispatch. The men at the upper position could retain command of the immediate riverfront during the bombardment, and yet the landing that mattered took place farther downriver. Read that as a change in where people bore the consequences of the operation. [2][4–8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Bowen saw six gunboats during the action and said he could not yet measure the effect of outgoing fire. NPS identifies seven ironclads in Porter’s attacking force and that the Tuscumbia was put out of action. The reported numbers have different vantage and scope. Bowen gives six and a half hours of firing. Modern narratives use rounded durations. [4][5]
- Bowen’s early report gives two deaths to that point, followed later by three killed and 12 or 15 wounded. These are successive provisional Confederate returns with no independent rank-and-file roster in this draft. [5]
