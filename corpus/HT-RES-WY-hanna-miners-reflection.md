## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** a disaster statistic cannot tell you who still had to put food on the table afterward. Hanna's history belongs both to miners and to the households that kept going.

Tom Rea's published account identifies Charles M. Stebner's privately published *Letters to Dear Dan* (1989) as its principal family source. It recalls Mary Hughes hearing the 1908 disaster as **“like thunder under the house.”** Her father Charles Hughes died; her husband Bruno Stebner subsequently died after a workplace injury in 1914. Mary supported her children through boarding-house work. This is a documented family-memory chain, not an interview conducted for this dossier: Stebner memoir → Rea's 2014 account → this short interpretation. The memoir itself and any original recording have not been inspected. [6]

A second household is independently named by the museum: Harriet and John Crawford raised six children in House 11, Two Town. Daughter **Muriel Kitching** remembered Harriet managing the household with a tub, washboard and outdoor plumbing. The museum publishes that recollection in paraphrase, without a recording or manuscript locator; do not turn it into a fabricated direct quotation. [7]

**A later community voice:** Nancy Anderson's history quotes **Ruth Boam**, whose husband lost work when the Union Pacific mines closed in 1954: **“We never thought for a moment what it meant for us.”** Her recollection describes enthusiasm for diesel locomotives before the employment consequences became clear. This is a published remembered voice, not contemporary audio heard here. Anderson also documents miners' union organization and community institutions, so the town should not be reduced to helpless victims or to a company success story. [8]

## 3. Then / now

The museum occupies a former social hall whose uses included an African American Methodist congregation, classrooms, a library and community events. Its operator says the Community Club House was dedicated in 1931, and that original wrought-iron lights remain in use. [1] The interpretive opportunity is a place where people assembled, not an invented surviving disaster scene.

House 11 was moved to the museum after donation. Its displayed household objects come from several Hanna families. [7] Therefore it can illuminate company-town domestic life, but must not be labeled Mary's boarding house or a time capsule containing only the Crawfords' possessions.

Bob Leathers' community memorial project continues identifying people and updating commemorations, with a September 2025 memorial reported on the site. [9] That ongoing work is part of the present story. It does not establish public access to any burial or mine location, and those locations are deliberately excluded here.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

This is a **single museum encounter**, not a multi-site disaster trail. Suggested pacing is editorial, not a timed operator program.

1. **Before arrival:** resolve the conflicting winter schedules and check actual travel conditions. If the public interior cannot be confirmed, keep this archive-only rather than driving between possible memorials.
2. **Begin with the living town:** orient to the museum's community-building function, then choose whatever public displays staff identify as appropriate. Do not assume the building's original fixtures are labeled or available for touching.
3. **Move from work to household:** if House 11 is open under museum supervision, consider how domestic labor supported wage labor. If closed, use the operator's online account rather than trying another entrance.
4. **End with the strongest story moment:** return to Mary's continuation as a boarding-house keeper, rather than ending with the machinery or the death total. Offer quiet time; nobody has to read a loss account aloud or complete a task. The proposed interpretive question is original: *What disappears when a town's history counts jobs and deaths but not the work of keeping a household alive?*

No claim is made that these sources are mounted together in the museum. This progression can be read alongside public exhibits without mislabeling them.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The two main disaster totals supported by the community historian and family-based history are 169 in 1903 and 59 in 1908. [6][9] Early reporting and later retellings vary; do not convert an early headline into a final count. Leathers' 1908 page itself says about 33 widows in its introduction while its reproduced inspector passage says 31. [10] Family circumstances and counting dates need reconciliation before publishing an exact household total.

Museum-building dates also differ: county tourism gives 1890, while WyoHistory's museum field-trip page gives 1894 and a 1930 remodel. [3][11] These are not resolved by the operator's 1931 dedication date. Keep construction/remodel/dedication distinct.

No complete original memoir, named interview recording, onsite exhibit inventory, live gate observation, memorial maintenance notice or entrance accessibility assessment was obtained. The Crawford page was readable in indexed operator text, but repeated direct opens failed. No original mine-inspector document was inspected; the community website supplies a transcription. No technical mine content is carried into this visitor dossier.
