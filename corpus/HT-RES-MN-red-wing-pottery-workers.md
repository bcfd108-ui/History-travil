## 2 · Story — The last glaze

Start with a piece the workers made on their own time. The museum calls them lunch-hour pieces. On the factory clock a worker might shape, cast, glaze or finish the same form repeatedly. A piece made for a friend or family member offered room to change the shape and use the material for somebody known. The practice is recorded by the museum, which displays examples. [4]

Early production used local clays. The trade grew from utilitarian wares such as crocks and jugs into factory lines that included art pottery and dinnerware. Minnesota Stoneware Company and the other Red Wing producers have separate names and histories in the record. The company files describe a 1936 name change from Red Wing Union Stoneware Company to Red Wing Potteries, Inc. Red Wing’s preserved plant, museum collection and ranges of marks should be read carefully for dates and company names. [2–5]

On November 9, 1967, *Daily Republican Eagle* reporter Ron Freeberg described men and women in the plant finishing stock. Here are the worker voices as printed in the newspaper and reproduced by the Red Wing Collectors Society. Dave Anway, after 42 years on the job, said, “I could stand a couple more years.” Hazel Engle, after 20 years, said, “There's no other spraying job around.” The cooling factory still had a glazing kiln in use. Workers were chipping off small marks from the supports that had held pieces in it. The paper describes two office workers still closing accounts and paying the last employees. [6]

The manuscript finding aid identifies Local 6-430 and an August 24 shareholder vote to liquidate after the strike that began June 1. The workers quoted in November show why the vote and end of production need separate dates. Their short statements are direct views from the floor, carried by a newspaper. The detailed union record and employers’ agreements are located by the finding aid, pending reading. [5–6]

## 3 · Then / now

Then, local factory yards held the shops, kilns, storage and rail access that allowed many workers to produce pottery in quantity. Today the museum interprets those workplaces from a different building. The operator describes a museum move from the former Minnesota Stoneware building to Harrison Street, where its present museum opened July 9, 2014. The registered Minnesota Stoneware structure stands on Old West Main Street. Its listing and factory photographs identify historic fabric. The public museum visit remains at Harrison Street. [1–4,7–8]

## 5 · Trip progression

Enter at the museum. Compare an early utility vessel with a later dinnerware piece. From the public gallery, look for a displayed lunch-hour object described by the museum. **Story landing — The last glaze.** Read Anway and Engle beside a finished piece. Consider the hands that did the last few minutes of work, from spraying to taking off the stilt marks. The visit can stay indoors when weather makes exterior walking impracticable. [1,4,6]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- **1967 timeline.** The local heritage site says a strike and nine months of failed negotiations closed production. The MNHS finding aid and November newspaper place the start of the strike on June 1 and the liquidation vote on August 24. The November paper still found finish work in progress. Earlier bargaining may account for some interval, but no verified bargaining record establishes the nine months. [5–6,9]
- **Claims of cause.** The finding aid’s editorial introduction treats import pressure as a factor. The contemporary news story traces the strike and liquidation and gives a reporter’s limited view of the factory near closure. No union minutes or full company accounts were read. Allocation of cause is held. [5–6]
- **Photos and factory approach.** The assigned draft needs a cataloged photograph with reuse rights and a contemporary visual comparison from a lawful position. Factory interior access and present tenant boundaries are unverified. [2–3,7–8]
