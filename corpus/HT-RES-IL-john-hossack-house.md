# John Hossack House — the Ottawa Underground Railroad station and the Jim Gray rescue of 1859
HT-RES-IL-john-hossack-house · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

John Hossack (December 6, 1806 – 1891; the death date itself is in conflict across the record — see Gaps) was a Scotsman who left Scotland at about twelve, spent years in Quebec working in an uncle's confectionery and bakery trade, and landed in Chicago twenty-two years before his 1860 trial, on his own account in that trial speech, to work on the canal then building that connects Chicago with the great river of the West — the Illinois and Michigan Canal. When the State failed to procure money to carry on the public works, he opened a prairie farm to feed his family and, in his own words, shipped some of the first grain exported from Chicago. The 1860 pamphlet that preserves his speech describes him as a prominent dealer in grain who had acquired a competency by enterprise and industry and was considered one of the most upright citizens of his community. In 1849 he moved to Ottawa, at the confluence of the Illinois and Fox rivers, entered the lumber trade, and became one of the largest dealers in lumber and grain in the Midwest on the wider record; he also played a large role in having a bridge erected over the Illinois River at Ottawa. It was during the canal years, at the grove that took his name, that he first became involved in the abolitionist cause, and the grove became a refuge for people escaping slavery. [2, 3]

In 1854–55 (the Historic American Buildings Survey data carried in John Drury's *Old Illinois Houses*; a National Park Service canal-corridor inventory gives 1855–1856 — both stand in Gaps) Hossack built the house that survives at 210 West Prospect Avenue, on the south bank of the Illinois River overlooking the city. It was designed by Sylvannus Grow of Chicago (the Register spells the name Grow, Sylvannus; wider sources give Sylvanus and Sylvannus) and built by Alonzo Edwards. The Register record describes a Greek Revival building — Drury calls it one of Ottawa's most beautiful houses, an example of the Southern Colonial manner then prevailing in Illinois, with a full two-story front gallery whose columns rise against the horizontal lines of gallery and roof; the Park Service inventory describes an elaborate two-story wood-frame Greek Revival house with a full-facade two-story front porch, a rear two-story porch with arches, six-over-six windows and an elaborate central entry with sidelights and transom. The Register lists the house's areas of significance as Commerce, Law, Architecture and Social History, period 1850–1874, significant year 1854, under criteria for architecture and for Hossack himself. [1, 3, 8]

The house was a station on the Underground Railroad. The received figure, carried across the record, is that as many as thirteen freedom seekers were quartered in the house at one time; a family-history telling credits Hossack with saving hundreds across his years of work, a figure this dossier carries as a telling, not a count. Hossack was outspoken in his denunciation of the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, calling it infamous and contrary to the laws of God, and he moved in the company of William Lloyd Garrison, Owen Lovejoy, Gerrit Smith and John Wentworth. In August 1858 he stood among the dignitaries on the platform at Ottawa for the first of the Lincoln–Douglas debates. [2, 3]

The case that made the house national ground began in Missouri. In 1859 three enslaved men fled Richard Phillips's plantation in New Madrid County, Missouri. One of them, Jim Gray — the name is also spelled Jim Grey in family and later tellings; the contemporary pamphlet and the House Divided record use Gray — was captured by slave catchers in Union County, Illinois, on September 4, 1859 and held under a state law about runaway slaves. The abolitionist Benajah G. Roots demanded Gray be taken before the county judge at Jonesboro, then carried a habeas petition to John D. Caton, Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Illinois, sitting at Ottawa. Caton held the state law unconstitutional but ordered Gray delivered to the United States Commissioner at Springfield for proceedings under the federal Fugitive Slave Act. On October 20, 1859, at Ottawa, as that transfer was being effected from the custody of the U.S. Deputy Marshal, the abolitionists present called on Gray to run; Hossack was among those who aided the rescue, others blocked the officers, and Gray got away — and, on the marker record's summary, reached Canada. The pamphlet's own headnote states the charge exactly: Hossack and Joseph Stout of Ottawa were convicted of having aided in rescuing a fugitive slave from the custody of the U.S. Deputy Marshal at Ottawa, October 20, 1859. [2, 4, 7]

At the February term of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois, in Chicago, Judge Drummond presiding, Hossack and Stout were convicted of violating the Fugitive Slave Law and sentenced to a fine of one hundred dollars and ten days' imprisonment. Before sentencing, Hossack addressed the court in a speech the American Anti-Slavery Society published in New York in 1860, and the speech is the primary document of this ground. He told the court he had no sense of guilt; that he had come from the tyranny of the Old World at twelve to find a place where men of toil would not be crushed by the property-holding class; that Scotland was a land where a slave never breathed; that the word slave is not to be found in the Constitution; and that if Jim Gray owed Phillips a day's work or a dollar, he would be the last man to prevent Phillips obtaining his due — but that the question of whether Gray owed his whole life's toil was a question for due process, which the Fugitive Slave Act denied. A contemporary *New York Herald* account of October 13, 1860, preserved by Dickinson College's House Divided project, reported the Ottawa convictions from the opposing side, as a vindication of the Fugitive Slave Law, and called the sentence very mild — the dossier prints that hostile contemporary reading alongside Hossack's own, because both are the record. Hossack served his sentence, returned to Ottawa, and remained a leading citizen; in 1873 he became totally blind and retired from business, and he died at Ottawa in 1891. [2, 7]

The house passed to his daughter and son-in-law, Mr. and Mrs. John Edwin Scott, and then through private owners — Drury, writing in the HABS tradition, found it the well-preserved home of Mr. and Mrs. Thomas R. Godfrey, a real-estate man — and it has remained a private residence ever since. The Illini Chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution marked it with a tablet (erected 1947 on the marker record; a second date of 1967 appears in the same record and stands in Gaps) reading, in its period wording, that here Hossack "directed the underground freedom of slaves 1859." The house was added to the National Register of Historic Places on March 16, 1972 (NRIS 72000462). A second marker at the same corner records the Register listing itself and names the then-owners, Mr. and Mrs. Rich Mennecke. The National Park Service's Underground Railroad materials have long presented the house as a documented station; whether it appears in the current public Network to Freedom dataset was not verified this run and is carried as a gap, not a claim. [1, 3, 4, 5]

The Register record gives the address as 210 W. Prospect St.; the street's current name is Prospect Avenue, and both forms stand in this dossier. [1, 3, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Do the reading first, because at this ground the document is the destination. Read Hossack's 1860 sentencing speech whole — it is short — and hold two of its facts in mind on the sidewalk: that the man speaking had eleven children, all natives of his adopted country, and that he told the judge plainly he had no sense of guilt. Then walk West Prospect Avenue to the Christie Street corner and read the two markers in the order they were placed: the DAR tablet of the 1940s, with its compressed period phrasing, and the Register marker of the listing era. Only then look up at the house — from the street, without entering the yard — and read what the architecture is doing: a Southern-plan gallery house, built by a grain-and-lumber fortune in a free state, whose rooms sheltered as many as thirteen people at a time on their way to Canada. Set the *Herald*'s hostile 1860 reading beside the speech before you leave: the same rescue that Ottawa's abolitionists counted as honor, a national paper counted as a crime properly punished, and the ten days and hundred dollars were the price of the difference. **Strongest story moment · "I have no sense of guilt."** A 53-year-old immigrant businessman, convicted in federal court of rescuing Jim Gray from a marshal's custody, used his one speech before sentencing not to plead for mercy but to argue the Constitution — and then went home to this house, on this bluff, and lived thirty more years as one of the town's leading men. Reflection posture: the people sheltered here were escaping enslavement; their names, beyond Jim Gray's, are not in the retrieved record, and this dossier does not invent them. [2, 3, 4, 7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Access conflict — the governing gap of this dossier, printed in full: the sweep list describes the Hossack House as a "House museum (tours by appointment)." No source retrieved this run supports that. The History List's Underground Railroad itinerary states the house "is a private residence and not open to the public"; Wikivoyage's Ottawa guide states "Private residence now"; the marker record names private owners; Drury's account is a succession of private family owners. The dossier files the ground as exterior-only and flags the sweep-list note as an error for correction. If a future owner opens the house, that change belongs to a later verification, not to this dossier. [4, 5, 6, 9]
- Build-date variance: 1854–55 on the HABS data carried by Drury and on the Register's significant year 1854 (period 1850–1874); 1855–1856 in the National Park Service's Illinois and Michigan Canal National Heritage Corridor inventory as quoted in the Jeremiah Strawn House nomination. Both stand. [1, 3, 8]
- Address-form variance: the Register record gives 210 W. Prospect St.; the markers, Drury and current listings give 210 W. Prospect Ave. (postal 61350). Both stand; Avenue is the current street name. [1, 3, 4]
- Architect-name spellings: Grow, Sylvannus (Register); Sylvannus Grow (Drury/HABS); Sylvanus Grow (wider record). One man, three spellings, printed unresolved. The builder, Alonzo Edwards, is consistent across the record. [1, 3]
- Death-date conflict for John Hossack: the wider biographical record's infobox gives December 8, 1891 (aged 85); a second rendering of the same record gives November 8, 1891. The year 1891 and the place, Ottawa, are consistent; the day is printed as an open conflict and the Story asserts neither. His birth date, December 6, 1806, is consistent across the record. [2, 3]
- Jim Gray's name: Gray in the 1860 pamphlet, the House Divided record and the Register-era accounts; Grey in family-history tellings. The dossier uses Gray, the contemporary spelling, and prints the variant. The rescue date, October 20, 1859 at Ottawa, and the sentence — $100 fine, ten days — are consistent across the pamphlet headnote and the *Herald* account. [2, 7]
- DAR tablet dating variance within a single record: the HMdb transcription gives "Erected by Illini Chapter D.A.R. 1947" in the inscription and "Erected 1967 by Illini Chapter, Daughters of the American Revolution" in its erected line. Both stand; the tablet itself was not inspected in person this run. [4]
- Numbers carried as tellings, not counts: "as many as thirteen" freedom seekers quartered at one time is the received figure across the record and is carried as such; the family-history claim of hundreds saved across Hossack's career has no retrieved documentary basis and is labeled a telling in the Story. No Underground Railroad passenger list for this house was retrieved, and none is implied. [3]
- Network to Freedom status unverified: the Park Service's public NTF listings page was retrieved this run, but the Hossack House's presence or absence in the current public dataset was not established from it, and older NPS Underground Railroad web materials presenting the house could not be re-verified at their original addresses. The dossier claims documented-station status on the Register record and the contemporary trial record, and leaves the NTF listing question open. [1]
- No HABS photograph or drawing set for the house was located and inspected this run, although Drury's account rests on HABS-compiled data and the Park Service inventory cites the HABS/HAER Division's corridor survey. The photo section below records exactly what was and was not inspected.
