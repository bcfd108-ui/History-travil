## 2 · Story — What the dredger left for the shovels

From the museum bank a ship appears to follow a remarkably simple line between pier heads. That line had to be invented. Before the cut, the bay’s natural entrance favored Superior on the Wisconsin side. The federal engineering record documents a local canal built by Duluth and railroad interests while the federal government was beginning separate harbor improvements. Work began in the fall of 1870. The survey gives a city-and-railroad project cost of $43,791.84, to be read as its reported cost. [3]

In spring 1871, according to MNHS, frozen gravel stalled the steam dredger near the lakeshore. Residents worked by hand at the last obstruction, and water flowing from the bay helped enlarge the opening. The engineering survey reproduces the later story of April 30: “Every citizen of Duluth was called out with shovels and picks.” The quotation is the tradition reported by HAER. The known fall excavation and steam machinery put that sentence in perspective. MNHS points to Jerome Eugene Cooley’s 1925 reminiscences for the embellished injunction story. The first boat is generally identified by MNHS as the little steam tug *Frank C. Fero*, May 6. [3–4]

The court date matters. HAER gives June 13 for the injunction, when the cut already carried water. Dredging and disputes about how the cut affected Superior continued. Federal navigation engineering later made the channel with its hard-sided edges that a visitor sees today. The cut also interrupted the road connection to Minnesota Point. The Detroit Publishing Company catalog dates an early bridge view to 1905, decades before the present vertical-lift operation. At the visitor center, a scale model or pilothouse replica helps put the deck crew’s problem alongside the engineers’ problem. [1,3–6]

## 3 · Then / now

Then, a steam dredger and manual labor opened an artificial route through Minnesota Point. As channels, piers and crossing equipment changed, the cut became part of a federal navigation system. Today visitors can watch a working approach from the museum side. The NOAA Coast Pilot cites 33 CFR 117.661 for drawbridge operation. A vessel passing the bridge is incidental to a visit. [1,3–4,7]

## 5 · Trip progression

Start with the center’s steam engine or models. In dry conditions, walk to the open grounds on the museum side and look along the cut toward the lake. **Story landing — Before June 13.** Read the court date at the edge of the public grounds while following the line through the two pier heads. In bad weather, use the models or the engineering drawing from indoors. [1,3–4]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- **The canal in three days.** The tradition preserved by HAER places the last manual work after word of a granted injunction had arrived. HAER dates the injunction June 13, after the May opening. MNHS describes real hand labor in late April following months of work and places the first reported passage on May 6. The whole canal story and the last breach describe different scopes. Cooley’s Internet Archive plaintext endpoint was inaccessible to the web reader and the 1870 *Duluth Minnesotian* issue was located bibliographically through MNHS. No verbatim newspaper quote is used. [3–4,9]
- **Current hours.** The operator’s Army Corps page returned HTTP 403 to the web reader; the museum’s supporting association currently posts the ordinary hours. Holiday exceptions and condition of the exterior grounds need day-of confirmation. [1–2]
- **Historic scope.** The North Pier Light’s registration concerns that structure, and the locator below uses the engineering survey for the broader canal. [3,8]
