## 2. The story
A food-history visit can begin with a picking bag or apple box, but it should not end with the product. Yakima Valley Museum lists picking bags, packing stands and box-making/lidding equipment in its collection. These objects point toward harvesting, carrying, sorting and preparing fruit for shipment: several jobs hidden inside the apparently simple act of buying an apple. An object alone cannot establish what its operator earned or how a family lived. [3]

**Guadalupe Gamboa's oral history supplies a named family rather than an anonymous workforce.** In Anne O'Neill's interview transcript, headed **April 9, 2004, Olympia**, he names his parents **Arcadio Gamboa and Martina Molina Gamboa**. He recalls the family's move from Texas, early asparagus work near Sunnyside, seasonal travel among crops and the consequences for children's schooling. He distinguishes his family's work for a small grower from recruitment by larger companies. [4]

His concise retrospective observation is: **“So as a child I grew up going from school to school.”** [4] It is not a visitor slogan about picturesque migration. It connects a harvest economy to education, family decisions and unequal opportunities. Read it as one participant's memory, not a statistical description of every worker. His later organizing alongside **Tomás Villanueva**, documented by UW's oral-history project, makes workers historical actors as well as laborers. The project's profile connects their work to the UFW cooperative and hop-worker organizing. [5]

The photography record offers a second kind of primary evidence. **Irwin Nash** documented farmworker communities and organizing, and WSU Libraries preserves the collection. Images can record collective action and ordinary life, but captions, dates and later identifications need scrutiny. They are not permission to locate the same families or enter the same properties. [6]

The valley was not an empty agricultural canvas. The **Yakama Nation's own publication of the 1855 treaty**, signed June 9, 1855 and ratified March 8, 1859, provides an essential framework of land and reserved rights. [7] This dossier does not reduce Indigenous food relationships to a prelude to commercial farming, assign a modern parcel's legal status, or offer access to Tribal land. The museum stop and the Nation's own account are distinct voices, not interchangeable authorities.

## 3. Then / now
Then, food production joined skilled manual work, seasonal movement, family labor and contested bargaining power. Gamboa's remembered experience makes the school calendar and the crop calendar part of the same household story. [4]

Now, the public museum gathers objects away from their original workplaces, while oral histories and archive photographs let a visitor consider the people behind them. A tool in a case is not an operating demonstration, and a historical photograph is not proof that its location remains accessible. The museum is an interpretation site, **not the documented site of the family's childhood, a particular strike or a labor camp**.

The comparison should remain specific: what a preserved object reveals about work, and what only testimony or records can reveal about the worker. Do not imply that historical employment conditions remain unchanged today or that one reform ended all inequity.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Prepare one page of the named history before arrival.** Read the transcript's opening family account, with its date and source. It gives a human scale without pretending to know every worker's life.
2. **Start in the museum's currently offered agricultural interpretation.** Identify one labeled tool or container that connects a crop to a task. If no relevant display is available, ask about public interpretation rather than requesting entry to storage.
3. **Bring the work back to the family.** Consider what an object's label can establish about use, then compare it with Gamboa's account of schooling and movement. Keep testimony and material evidence separate.
4. **Finish with the strongest story moment: workers could organize as well as harvest.** The history is not merely a sequence of machines getting faster. People built institutions and asserted a voice in the conditions under which food was produced. [5]

Optional onsite question: **Which displayed object has a documented connection to a named worker or family, and which parts of its labor history are still unknown?** Do not approach current farmworkers, seek immigration information or photograph people as historical stand-ins. The progression stays inside authorized public interpretation.
