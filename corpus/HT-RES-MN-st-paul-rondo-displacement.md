## 2 · Story — “We didn’t have that choice”

Rondo was a neighborhood with its own reasons to exist. Rondo Center of Diverse Expressions describes families buying homes, opening businesses, founding churches and organizing civic and social groups. That community history matters when looking at a freeway cut: the taking of a house could also separate people from the places where they found work, care and company. The published resistance history documents residents organizing after they learned in 1953 that a school would be lost. Advocates pressed for a below-grade freeway. The resulting cut still split the neighborhood. [2–3]

Nathaniel Khaliq recalled in a 2025 Minnesota Historical Society interview that his grandfather resisted removal and proposed moving his home back to make room. Khaliq remembered returning to the house on the day his grandfather was taken out. He spoke about the housing choices Black families faced under racial covenants: “We didn’t have that choice.” This is Khaliq’s recollection as printed in the archive’s transcript. He was uncertain which agency employed the workers he remembered in the house, and the draft leaves that identification unresolved. [4]

Gloria Ellen Gilbreath Wilson’s 2003 interview gives the loss another context. MNHS’s finding-aid summary records that her father worked as a Union Depot redcap for 41 years, that Wilson became a matron at the depot, and that the family home was taken for a bridge over the freeway. The summary connects a downtown workplace with a family address that no longer survived the highway project. It does not establish the place of that home within today’s plaza. A separate Union Depot dossier takes up the work from public station space. [5]

Marvin Anderson and Floyd Smaller grew up in Rondo, then organized to preserve its memory. The plaza occupies the former site of a building that served several neighborhood uses and burned in 2013. The loss of that building led to the memorial effort. The community account identifies Seitu Jones’s chimes as honoring streets that once ran through Rondo. It also describes a lighted tower higher than the freeway in Anderson’s own account. [1–2,6–7]

## 3 · Then / now

Rondo Avenue held homes, businesses and institutions. Part of the corridor was replaced by the interstate. Today the plaza is a community-built place of memory on a distinct parcel, with benches, engraved brick and interpretive displays. It is a public reading anchor. Personal homes and other parcels remain historical subjects rather than suggested destinations. [1–5]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- RCODE’s About page states the plaza opened in July 2016. The governor’s proclamation names July 14, 2018 as opening day; contemporary reporting describes the ceremony, and Marvin Anderson told the City Planning Commission in 2019 that it opened the previous July. The 2016 groundbreaking notice provides another dated milestone. Treat July 14, 2018 as the documented opening ceremony and retain the operator’s different wording. [2,6–8,11]
- The operator’s home and About pages describe 23 and 26 panels respectively. An installation inventory and revision history have yet to reconcile the totals. No count is used for visiting. [1–2]
- The legislature’s retrospective account says about 700 homes were destroyed. A legislative presentation calls 700 African American-owned homes. The denominators are reported as printed and not combined. A contemporaneous demolition ledger was not obtained. The Wilson interview’s legacy item link returned HTTP 404. Its finding aid was read; the original transcript or audio was not verified. [5,9–10,12]
