# Davis Bayou and the second life of a work camp

*On Old Ground · Mississippi · Jackson County · Research draft for Dash · Hunts DISABLED*

## 2. Story

In May 1938 about two hundred young men arrived in Ocean Springs from Quitman. Company 1437 of the Civilian Conservation Corps had to build a place to live before anybody could take a vacation here. Work began in June. The Park Service identifies ten buildings, including four barracks, a mess hall, a bathhouse and a recreation building. A later federal architectural survey describes the company as all white. In a federally segregated program, these particular jobs and training places went to white enrollees. [5, 6]

The crew had two different kinds of buildings to make. The men slept in wooden barracks. The vacation cabins planned for a wooded peninsula were poured concrete. Herbert B. Campbell’s September 1938 layout shows ten cabins around a loop, leaving the trees between them. H. C. Dietzer’s construction records and the federal survey document the trade the men were learning. The surviving plans draw a fuller park than the later progress reports can establish. [5]

In the summer of 1946, Richard L. Caylor of Delta State Teachers College brought twenty students to the old park. The following summer the Gulf Coast Research Laboratory held its first official field classes there. The former barracks housed students. The classroom could be a wooden table under a live oak. By 1949 local schoolchildren were supplying empty jars for specimens, and a former Seafood Commission boat could take a field class out to the islands. The laboratory later acquired a separate site at Halstead Road and East Beach. The early park camp and the permanent laboratory are two different places. [5, 7]

A photograph in the laboratory’s history preserves one class at work. Five people face the camera around an outdoor table. There are papers in front of them, a small microscope on the table and a long wooden building behind them. The caption identifies a botany class at Magnolia State Park around 1947. It does not name the five people. That table is the clearest record of how a Depression-era work camp became a place to study the Gulf. [7]

## 3. Then and now

How the park was built still shapes what can be seen. The crew worked on roads, drainage and public recreation facilities as well as its own camp. Hurricane Camille damaged the park in 1969. A 1973 archaeological survey found the former camp overgrown. Today the Park Service interprets road material on the signed CCC Spur Trail and describes nearby low remains as possible building framework. It assigns no numbered barracks to those stones. [5, 8, 9]

## 5. Public progression

Begin inside the Colmer center. Compare the wooden quarters in the class photograph with the concrete vacation-cabin plan in the federal survey. For the outdoor evidence, ask staff about the signed CCC spur. NPS describes it as a 0.2-mile hiking trail and identifies a connection from the Davis Bayou Trail at the visitor-center parking area. At stop 4 the interpreters identify a road. At stop 5 the attribution is tentative. The indoor visit stands on its own if conditions or mobility make the spur unsuitable. [1–6, 8, 9, 11]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Campbell’s ten-cabin design, the July 1941 revision showing three incomplete cabins, and later accounts suggesting all ten were built describe different stages. HABS leaves the final build count open. The survey also notes a disagreement between the general management plan’s description of housing and records of the cabins’ intended use for vacationers. [5]
- The historic survey and laboratory history separate cabins, barracks and cafeteria. The cafeteria burned during the 1949 session. Neither source establishes that the students slept in the concrete vacation cabins. [5, 7]
- The only verified coordinate pair would need to identify the same visitor entrance in independent sources within about ten meters. No such pair was established. The spur’s grade, surface continuity and present-day cabin survival were also unverified. [1–6, 9]
- **Tier 1 scheduling signal.** Gulf Islands National Seashore reported 7,576,923 recreation visits in 2025 across Mississippi and Florida. A separate Davis Bayou visitor count was not established in this review. [10]
