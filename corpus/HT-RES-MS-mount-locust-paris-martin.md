# Mount Locust, the travelers’ stand where Paris Martin was enslaved

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP05 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Use the National Park Service information center or its exterior interpretive panels during posted opening hours. The house has a separate structural closure notice. Check the current Mount Locust listing before traveling. No graves or private family ground are stops. [1,2]

## 2. Story

For boatmen who had floated goods down the Mississippi, the hard part was getting home. Many sold their cargo and boats at Natchez or New Orleans and walked north. William and Paulina Ferguson acquired the Mount Locust property in 1784. A meal and lodging at their farm met a real need on that return journey. After William died, Paulina married James Chamberlain. The property grew into a cotton plantation dependent on enslaved labor. The inn was only one part of a much larger workplace. [2,3]

## 3. Then and now

A letter dated February 24, 1822, from Louis Bonaparte Chamberlain to his mother Paulina congratulates her on accommodating Andrew Jackson and William Carroll. The letter, catalogued with the family’s papers, preserves whom a son thought worth mentioning. Plantation estate records preserve different names, often beside prices. Read together, these are records of the same household produced for very different purposes. [3,4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Paris Martin was enslaved as a field hand here. In July 1863 he escaped and enlisted at Natchez in the 50th United States Colored Troops. Discharged in 1866, he returned to Jefferson County and lived there until his death in February 1917. His brother Anthony also served. Researchers used Paris’s death certificate and a surviving government headstone to confirm his burial. The date and the identification belong to the account of his life, with no directions to the grave. [4,5]

## 5. Public progression

At the public interpretation area, compare the traveler's short stop for food with the length of a working life spent here. The surviving house and interpretive panels give the inn a visible form. Much of the housing for the people who produced the cotton no longer stands. [2,4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The NPS property history describes public grounds; an older news notice discusses the house closure. Ordinary operating days can change. No reopened house interior was verified. Counts taken from one census year and totals across the plantation's lifetime describe different groups. [1,2,4]
