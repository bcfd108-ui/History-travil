# HT-RES-NY-west-point — The plain above the river bend, held since 1778 and never taken
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A high plain on the west bank of the Hudson where the river makes its sharpest double turn — fortified in 1778, chained across that same year, betrayed on paper in 1780 and never actually taken, and converted in 1802 into the United States Military Academy, which has occupied the ground every day since and still runs it as an active post that visitors enter on the Army's terms, not their own.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is an active military installation, not an open historic site: West Point, the United States Military Academy, on the west bank of the Hudson River in Orange County, New York, about 50 miles north of New York City. The operator is the United States Army — the Academy and U.S. Army Garrison West Point. Public entry begins outside the gate, at the Frederic V. Malek West Point Visitors Center, 2107 New South Post Road, West Point, NY 10996, which the Academy's own visitors page describes as the gateway to West Point, nestled at Pershing Center right outside Thayer Gate, the main gate; the same page states the Visitors Center is open from 9 a.m. to 4:45 p.m., 7 days a week, closed for all federal holidays, houses the West Point Experience exhibit (open 9 a.m. to 4:30 p.m.), the Visitor Control Center, and the Army West Point Gift Shop, and stands adjacent to the West Point Museum. Access to the post itself is controlled, and the terms printed here are the Academy's own. Under the Academy's July 17, 2019 public-affairs release, non-Department of Defense visitors seeking unescorted access must check in at the Visitor Control Center to be issued local access credentials, must undergo on-the-spot background checks in compliance with the National Crime Information Center–Interstate Identification Index before credentials are issued, and should bring a state-issued driver's license compliant with the Real ID Act of 2005 — or, without one, one of the alternatives the release lists, including a U.S. passport or passport card, a permanent resident card (Form I-551), a foreign passport with a temporary I-551 stamp, or an employment authorization document with a photograph; the release places the Visitor Control Center at 2107 New South Post Road and gives its hours then as Monday through Sunday, 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. General visitors who are not seeking unescorted access see the Academy grounds by guided public tour: the Academy's admissions pages direct general visitors to public tours through West Point Tours at (845) 446-4724, separate from admissions tours for prospective cadets. The West Point Museum, next door at 2110 New South Post Road in Olmsted Hall, is free — the Army Museum Enterprise page states "Free Admission" — and carries its own hours, Tuesday through Saturday, 10:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m., closed Sunday and Monday, Thanksgiving, Christmas, and New Year's Day, and the first Tuesday of every month. No visitor should plan on wandering the post unescorted on a historic-site model; that is not the access this ground offers, and this draft prints none. West Point was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000562, per the National Register record for the United States Military Academy), and its nomination form classifies public access as "yes: restricted" — the register's own word for exactly the boundary above.

## 2. The story
The river made the post. The U.S. Army Garrison West Point's own history explains the choice in navigational terms: vessels passing West Point had to make a sharp ninety-degree turn to the west, sail a quarter of a mile, then make another right-angle turn to the north; the bends were sharper than at any other point on the Hudson, the river was very narrow, the tidal effects were the greatest and the current the swiftest there, and the winds were treacherous. General George Washington considered West Point, in the Garrison history's words, the most important strategic position in America — because a British fleet that passed it could split the colonies along the Hudson.

The Americans first built on the wrong side. Construction began in the summer of **1775**, but on Constitution Island — then called Martelaer's Rock — across the river. The flaw showed at once: the island was vulnerable by land and dominated by higher ground on all sides, especially from West Point. Other Hudson works followed, including Forts Montgomery and Clinton at Popolopen Creek, many destroyed by the British in October 1777.

West Point itself was occupied late, and then permanently. Until January 1778 it was not occupied by the military at all. On **January 27, 1778**, Brigadier General Samuel Holden Parsons and his brigade crossed the ice on the Hudson and climbed to the plain — and, in the Garrison history's sentence, from that day to the present West Point has been occupied by the United States Army. It is, on the Army's own telling in source after source, the oldest continuously occupied military post in America.

Washington then gave the ground an engineer. To reinforce the defenses, he personally selected Thaddeus Kosciuszko, a Polish army general and engineer, to design West Point's fortifications in **1778**, and Washington transferred his own headquarters to West Point in **1779**. Continental soldiers built the system Kosciuszko laid out — forts, batteries, and redoubts on the plain and the hills above it, Fort Putnam among them on the high ground — and fashioned its most famous work: an iron chain across the Hudson, meant to stop British ships from separating the colonies. The Garrison history dates the placing precisely: the chain, known as the Great Chain, was placed in the river on **April 30, 1778**, measured approximately 1,600 feet, and weighed between 180 and 186 tons. That weight is one of three different weights the Army's own publications give for the same chain, and the disagreement is printed in §6 rather than resolved here; the chain's seasonal life — taken up for the winter ice and relaid each spring — is the part the accounts share. Links of the chain survive as trophies on the post (see §4).

The fortress was never taken — but it was once sold, on paper. In **1780** the post's commander, Major General Benedict Arnold, plotted to turn West Point over to the British; the plot collapsed, Arnold escaped to the British, and, in the Installation Overview's flat sentence, Fortress West Point was never captured by the British, despite Benedict Arnold's treason. At the end of the war in **1783** the materials used to construct West Point were sold to recoup funds, though the post remained occupied, and its strategic importance returned to focus in 1794 as tensions between France and England rose again in Europe.

The second founding turned a fortress into a school. Washington, Knox, Hamilton, and John Adams — soldiers and legislators who had felt America's wartime reliance on foreign engineers and artillerists, Kosciuszko's brilliance notwithstanding — urged an institution devoted to the arts and sciences of warfare. President Thomas Jefferson signed the legislation establishing the United States Military Academy at West Point in **1802**; the HABS documentation for the Academy (HABS NY-5708) dates the establishing act to March 16, 1802. The first cadets were assigned to the Corps of Artillerists and Engineers, instructed in the practical use of artillery, and Joseph Gardner Swift was the Academy's first graduate. The Academy remained under the Corps of Engineers until June 1866.

The school's maker was its fifth superintendent. Sylvanus Thayer, superintendent from **1817 to 1833** and remembered as the Father of the Military Academy, upgraded academic standards, instilled military discipline, and emphasized honorable conduct — and, aware of the young nation's need for engineers, made engineering the foundation of the curriculum. For the first half of the nineteenth century, the Garrison history records, USMA graduates were largely responsible for the construction of the bulk of the nation's infrastructure. Today the Corps of Cadets is over 4,000 strong; graduates receive a Bachelor of Science degree and a commission as a second lieutenant, serving a minimum of five years on active duty, under the enduring commitment the Garrison history prints as Duty, Honor, Country.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a Continental fortress of earth, stone, and timber on a high plain — batteries, redoubts, and forts designed by Kosciuszko from 1778, a great iron chain running from the shore to Constitution Island, and a commander in 1780 willing to hand the whole position to the enemy for a price. **Now:** a federal service academy of granite academic buildings, barracks, and chapels on the same plain, still an active Army post, its Revolutionary works surviving as remains within a National Historic Landmark district whose own nomination describes Revolutionary War fortifications alongside Academy structures from its earliest years to the immediate past.

**Then:** the Plain as the whole post — in the Garrison's own description of its early years, the entire academy was located on the Plain, used for drill, mounted cavalry maneuvers, and summer encampment. **Now:** the Plain still at the center of the post, the parade ground the HABS aerial photographers framed in 1982 with the Cadet Chapel rising in the foreground (see §7), and Trophy Point beside it — designed by Stanford White and dedicated in 1897, in the Garrison's description, and formerly the site of graduation ceremonies before class sizes outgrew it in the mid-twentieth century.

**Then:** a chain of iron links floated on log rafts across 1,600 feet of tidal river and hauled out before the ice each winter. **Now:** thirteen surviving links of that chain displayed at Trophy Point, one for each of the original colonies on the standard interpretation, kept there among the post's war trophies by the West Point Museum, which maintains part of its collection on display on post, including the cannon collection on Trophy Point.

## 4. What's still there
- The Plain — the parade ground and original garrison ground, occupied since January 27, 1778, which the Garrison calls the site of the longest continually occupied U.S. Army garrison in America
- Trophy Point, designed by Stanford White and dedicated in 1897 (Garrison), with the Battle Monument, the cannon collection the West Point Museum maintains on post, and the surviving links of the Great Chain displayed there
- The Cadet Chapel, the Gothic stone chapel that dominates the HABS aerial views of the parade ground, and the Old Cadet Chapel of the Academy's early years
- Remains of the Revolutionary War fortifications — the forts, batteries, and redoubts of Kosciuszko's system, including Fort Putnam above the plain, which the West Point Museum administers and operates seasonally (Army Museum Enterprise)
- The academic core and barracks around the Plain, documented building by building in the HABS survey of the Academy (HABS NY-5708, Library of Congress), including Professors' Row and Cullum Memorial Hall
- The West Point Museum in Olmsted Hall at 2110 New South Post Road — free, per the Army Museum Enterprise — collecting and interpreting artifacts of the Academy, the Army, and the profession of arms, with a collecting mission the museum dates from 1843; and, outside Thayer Gate beside it, the Frederic V. Malek West Point Visitors Center, with the West Point Experience exhibit on the cadet's 47-month development (USMA visitors page)
- Constitution Island, across the river — the first, flawed 1775 position, inside the same historic ground the National Register nomination describes, its batteries built in two phases between 1775 and 1777

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Visitors Center, 2107 New South Post Road:** Start outside the gate, as the Academy directs — the West Point Experience exhibit, the Visitor Control Center for anyone seeking credentials, and the tour departure point, in the center the USMA page keeps open 9 a.m. to 4:45 p.m.
2. **In the West Point Museum, 2110 New South Post Road:** The free museum next door before any gate crossing — the Academy's and the Army's material history, including the institution that keeps the Trophy Point cannon collection, on the Army Museum Enterprise's Tuesday–Saturday hours.
3. **On the guided public tour:** The post as the Army shows it, through West Point Tours — the Plain, Trophy Point, and the Cadet Chapel as the tour is constituted on the day; there is no self-guided version of this step.
4. **At Trophy Point:** Stand with the chain links and the cannon at the point above the bend — the ninety-degree turn, the quarter mile, the second turn — and read the river the way the 1778 engineers read it.
5. **On the Plain:** End on the parade ground Parsons's brigade climbed to on January 27, 1778 — the fortress become a school, still occupied, still working.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The Great Chain's weight does not agree across the Army's own publications, and the disagreement is printed rather than resolved. The U.S. Army Garrison West Point history page states that the chain placed in the river on April 30, 1778 measured approximately 1,600 feet and weighed between 180 and 186 tons. The Garrison's own Installation Overview page states that Continental soldiers extended a 150-ton iron chain across the Hudson. The Department of Defense's Military OneSource installation history states that American Continental Line soldiers installed a 65-ton iron chain across the Hudson. Three official Army or Defense publications, three weights — 180–186 tons, 150 tons, and 65 tons — for the same 1778 chain. The date the chain was placed, April 30, 1778, and its West Point–to–Constitution Island span are carried consistently by the Garrison history and are printed as settled; the weight is not.

A related placement caution is printed rather than smoothed: the Garrison history is explicit that construction in 1775 began across the river on Constitution Island, that the Popolopen Creek forts were a separate work destroyed in October 1777, and that West Point itself was not occupied until January 1778 — any account putting the Great Chain, or a garrison, on the plain in 1775 or 1776 conflicts with the post's own history.

The visitor-access record is dated, and its date is printed with it. The unescorted-access terms in §1 come from the Academy's public-affairs release of July 17, 2019, effective July 22, 2019; the Visitors Center hours come from the Academy's current visitors page as consulted. Access rules at an active installation change, and the 2019 release is printed as a 2019 release — a visitor must confirm current requirements with the Visitor Control Center before traveling, and nothing in this draft should be read as a current credential guarantee. The West Point Museum's hours are printed from the Army Museum Enterprise page as consulted; the museum's own page also notes closure on the first Tuesday of every month, a detail a Tuesday traveler needs.

No coordinates are printed in the locator below. The National Register nomination for the Academy marks its street-and-number line "not for publication," the nomination text consulted carries no latitude or longitude, and the Library of Congress HABS frame record for this entry's photo carries empty latitude/longitude values in its place field.
