# Nacogdoches Stone Fort — the house whose stones moved twice
Research draft · Texas OP03 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

### A merchant’s house at the road

The early house stood along the old San Antonio road near today’s Fredonia Street. Antonio Gil Y’Barbo used it in his trading business. The Handbook describes native ironstone walls, interior adobe partitions, walnut trim and a building whose long wall faced the main road. Its public reputation grew over years of changing owners and uses. Even SFA’s present museum page describes the original as a Spanish colonial house before it explains how the nickname stuck [1, 3].

### The preservation effort began with a loss

“Preserve the Old Fort” is the title recorded for a March 11, 1902 Post item in the SFA Stone Fort Collection finding aid. The finding aid cites the article’s argument linking historical people to the building. That is evidence of the preservation appeal, and the newspaper page itself was not independently retrieved. The collection describes deeds tracing the house and lot from 1841 to 1901. The building came down in 1902. The Cum Concilio Club then raised money for a memorial on Washington Square and laid its cornerstone on July 4, 1907. Some fabric from the original went into that memorial [4].

### The footprint and the public memory separated

The memorial came down when the campus museum was built in 1936. SFA records reused material in the university building. The surviving museum and the lost downtown house therefore tell related stories from different periods. The public therefore has a rare chance to read both a colonial business and a twentieth-century effort to build public memory [1, 3–5].

## 3 · Then / now

**Historic place.** The long downtown front formerly followed the Old San Antonio Road and the side stood along the lateral now known as Fredonia Street. Treat the old frontage as contextual reading on today’s Main Street and avoid assuming any surviving section of the original building stands there [3, 6].

**Lawful physical anchor.** SFA Stone Fort Museum on the circle at Griffith and Clark Boulevards. Drive up from Starr Avenue using SFA’s visitor directions, park legally and take the signed short walk to the museum door. Downtown orientation uses the public Charles Bright Visitor Center frontage, 200 E. Main Street. Washington Square and historical archive rooms are outside this itinerary [1, 6–7].

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. If starting downtown, locate the Royal Road interpretation from the Charles Bright visitor frontage or through its public room when open [6–7].
2. Enter the campus museum from its signed public approach on a verified open day. Distinguish visible replica construction from claims about particular pieces of reused stone [1, 4].
3. **Strongest story moment — The stones survived two demolitions.** At the campus entry, read the fall of the original house, the 1907 memorial and the 1936 reopening in sequence. The preservation campaign itself left a physical record [1, 4].

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

SFA’s museum gives an original building period of approximately 1788–1791 [1]. The Handbook’s narrative places the building after the laying out of the town in 1779, while the historic photograph’s verso explicitly claims 1779 for the house [3, 5]. The verso dates its depicted scene to 1885, calls that sixteen years before destruction, and thus implies 1901; SFA records 1902 demolition [1, 4–5]. The photograph metadata also contains a Fort Worth place entry even though the caption and description identify the subject as Nacogdoches [5]. Deed originals and the 1902 Post image remain open. Exact original stones have not been isolated in the campus façade.
