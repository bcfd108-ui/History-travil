# The burial record, three memorials and Robert Johnson’s working life

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP21*

## 2. Story

Robert Johnson made records that circulated far beyond the rooms where he first played. Mississippi’s state encyclopedia describes recording sessions in San Antonio and Dallas, a changing network of musicians and years traveling after returning to the Delta. It also traces the later attention that came when Johnson’s records reached the promoter John Hammond, who wanted him for a December 1938 New York concert. Johnson had already died near Greenwood. A performance that did not happen and the records that outlived him frame two kinds of evidence about a working musician. [1][2]

The University of Mississippi catalogs a certified copy of a Mississippi death certificate for Robert L. Johnson. The online description carries a July 1938 date, while the state encyclopedia and NPS use August 16, 1938. Greenwood’s tourism office reports that the certificate names “Zion Church.” Its story names several churches that later acquired memorial associations. The full archival certified-copy image returned HTTP 403. The accessible preview did not resolve the burial or date handwriting. The register’s brief reported wording is treated as a reported transcription and can identify a lead, not an exact plot. [1–4]

A later generation did the work of recovery and commemoration. Greenwood’s account distinguishes the Columbia Records memorial at Mount Zion, the memorial at Payne Chapel and the later Little Zion gravestone. Writer Ace Atkins describes meeting Rosie Eskridge, who was associated with the Little Zion account through her husband. Atkins writes that “only the grave diggers were present and no words spoken.” This is the author’s published account of his reporting, not a direct transcript of Eskridge or a burial record. It illustrates why later accounts cannot all use the same meaning of “eyewitness.” [3][5]

Stories about a bargain at a crossroads are lore. The state encyclopedia discusses how the crossroads and devil stories became attached to Johnson’s death and recorded identity. This draft records that process of attribution. It leaves any origin near a particular intersection unlocated and supplies no recreation of the story. [1]
