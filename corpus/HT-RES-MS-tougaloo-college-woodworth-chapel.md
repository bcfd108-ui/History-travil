# Tougaloo College and Woodworth Chapel — the students who built a church and the students who asked for books

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP11 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Woodworth Chapel is on the Tougaloo College campus at 500 West County Line Road, Tougaloo, Madison County. The college invites the community to a Sunday service that its Religious Life office lists at 9:30 a.m. Confirm the service with the college for the intended date, especially during breaks. Attend as a guest of the congregation. The admissions office separately arranges campus visits. No self-guided campus or interior opening hours have been established. [1–4]

## 2. Story

Walk into Woodworth for an invited service and you enter a room built by students. In 2016, Claflin University president Henry N. Tisdale submitted a campus history to Congress that identifies Walker Frazier as head carpenter and the students as the crew who built the chapel in 1901. The craft was part of what students learned at a school founded in 1869 to educate people after emancipation. The Queen Anne chapel, an active church and meeting space, gave successive generations a room of their own. The college records visits by Fannie Lou Hamer, Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert F. Kennedy. [1,4–5]

One episode in the college’s civil-rights history began with a book request, away from this room. On March 27, 1961, nine Tougaloo students, all members of the North Jackson NAACP Youth Council, went to the downtown Jackson public library reserved for white readers. Edwards Hollis later recalled going to the desk and asking for a specific book. The students stayed when directed to leave. Police arrested them. They spent about 32 hours in jail, and each received a $100 fine and a suspended thirty-day sentence. Fellow students and townspeople came out in support. [6–7]

College president Adam Daniel Beittel declined to discipline the students when people outside the college pressed him to act. Chaplain John Mangram publicly backed them. Beittel’s surviving letters and notes include an April meeting with church board members who wanted the students to drop their appeals. A private college could offer the organizers some institutional protection. Keeping it took decisions by people with names. [4,6]

## 3. Then and now

The college restored Woodworth from 1999 to 2002 and rededicated it in 2003. Its 1902 Murray Harris pipe organ remains part of its musical life. Union United Church of Christ worships here. The students’ 1961 library protest, the demonstrations that followed it, and the 1963 Woolworth lunch-counter action all have their own places and dates. The campus supplies their educational and organizing context. [1–2,6]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Woodworth is the surviving structure for this entry. Public access follows the invitation to the announced service or the itinerary the college confirms for a hosted visit. Follow campus staff for arrival and visitor parking. Classrooms, residence halls and other campus buildings fall outside that invitation. No additional stops or photography prompts. [1–4]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

**Hold the request and the response together.** One student remembered asking for a book. President Beittel’s correspondence documents the pressure that reached back to her college after the arrests. At Woodworth the connection is the institution that educated and stood behind the students. It would change the story to place the library desk or the arrests inside the chapel. [6–7]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The National Park Service marks the campus as closed to general public visits. The college expressly invites worship and offers scheduled admissions visits. Those statements describe different purposes and leave weekday walk-in access unverified. The archives spell the participant’s given name Jeraldine. *American Libraries* uses Geraldine in reporting her own account. No location within the chapel has been verified for planning the read-in. [1–4,6–7]

## 7. Historical photograph

The college archives identify Jerry Keahey as photographer of a group portrait of the Nine. The collection requires permission for publication. An image of the group would document the people, and would still need independent identification of its setting. No image adopted. [6]
