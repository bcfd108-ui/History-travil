## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** winning the 1893 campaign was not the end of political work. It created new questions about organizing, informed voting and whose contribution would be remembered.

The documentary edition identifies **Minnie J. Reynolds's “The Chance in Colorado,” September 2, 1893, Woman's Journal, page 276**, reprinted from the Colorado W.C.T.U. Bulletin. The indexed primary text announces that the question would go before voters on November 7 and distinguishes the measure from a constitutional amendment. Its editorial introduction describes Reynolds's press work and coalition-building. [3] This is direct campaign-era writing accessed through an indexed scholarly transcription, not an original newspaper image; only the beginning of the primary passage was recovered.

**Elizabeth Piper Ensley** supplies a fuller aftermath voice in *Election Day* (1894). The published excerpt states: **“Women will study politics.”** She discusses political clubs, organization and Black women's campaign work, while criticizing uneven support for women's enfranchisement among Black male colleagues. [4] Her argument does not reduce Black women to supporters of white leaders: they were political organizers with their own expectations and criticism.

The National Park Service's historical account identifies Ensley as the association's **1893 treasurer**, connecting Black activists to the campaign. It describes collaboration among suffrage organizations, labor and Populist networks. [5] That interpretive account supports context; it is not a substitute for the women's documents.

**Original interpretation:** read the two moments together. Before the vote, a journalist explains the opportunity. Afterward, a Black organizer asks what women will do with political power. The story becomes work before and after a victory, rather than a single congratulatory date.

## 3. Then / now

NPS distinguishes Colorado's successful **1893 statewide referendum** from earlier defeats, including 1877. [5] This is not the claim that Colorado was the first jurisdiction to enfranchise women, nor that all barriers to voting disappeared.

The archive today preserves records that can complicate a celebratory narrative. A collection listing is evidence of holdings, not evidence that a letter is on exhibition or will be available without advance retrieval. No physical suffrage gallery was verified for this dossier.

Ensley's own retrospective argument also has limits: it is advocacy about political behavior, not a comprehensive turnout dataset. “Women” in a campaign text does not automatically mean every resident woman was eligible or could exercise the right equally. Citizenship, other eligibility rules and racial discrimination need separate attention rather than being erased by the headline success.
