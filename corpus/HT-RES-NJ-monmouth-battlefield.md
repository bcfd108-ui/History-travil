# HT-RES-NJ-monmouth-battlefield — Seven Farms Under Fire

Division NJ seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Monmouth Battlefield State Park preserves the ground where the Continental Army and the British Army fought through the heat of June 28, 1778, northwest of Freehold in Monmouth County, New Jersey. The National Historic Landmark nomination describes a battle "waged over the fields and swamps to the north and west of the small village of Monmouth Courthouse," in two scenes of conflict about three miles apart, and records that the western ground — the part a visitor walks today — has survived in its historic setting of orchards and fields [2]. The New Jersey State Park Service, which operates the park, puts the human fact first: "Seven families farmed the fields that became the battleground in 1778. As the armies came together on June 28, 1778, the farmers were caught in the crossfire" [1]. This is reflection ground. The battle is told here plainly, the dead are part of the record, and nothing about the day is a game.

## 1. Why this place

Monmouth was the combat debut of the American army after the winter at Valley Forge. The nomination's statement of significance opens with exactly that: "the battle of Monmouth of June 28, 1778, marked the combat debut of the American Army after the hard winter's training at Valley Forge" [2]. What the day proved — that drilled Continental troops could stand in open ground against British regulars and hold — is why the ground was kept. The nomination calls Monmouth "the longest sustained engagement of the war" [2], and the State Park Service calls it "one of the longest battles of the American Revolutionary War," fought through "a three-hour cannonade, the largest artillery battle of the war" [1]. The battlefield that survives is not a monument plot. It is the western battleground itself — fields, ravines, brooks, a hedgerow line, a church, and a farmhouse that stood in the middle of it — held largely as it was, in the nomination's words, "preserved largely in its historic appearance" [2].

## 2. What happened here

In June 1778 the British evacuated Philadelphia. The nomination lays out the movement: on June 18, General Clinton left Philadelphia with 10,000 British troops, heading for the New Jersey shore, where they were to be taken by boat to New York; Washington, at the head of 14,000 Continentals, pursued, and by June 27 the British rear guard and supply train were at Monmouth Courthouse [2]. Washington decided to attack the next morning, against the advice of most of his lieutenants. The striking force was offered to General Charles Lee, the army's second in command; Lee first refused it, then accepted [2].

On the morning of June 28, Lee attacked without adequate reconnaissance. The nomination's account is blunt: his force fell into confusion, Lee ordered a retreat, and the untried troops fell back over hills and swamps toward the main army [2]. The State Park Service's longer telling gives the weight of the two bodies that met first: Lee's advanced corps numbered nearly 5,000 men, and the British counterattack that struck it numbered around 9,000 [1]. Washington met Lee on the high ground near the west ravine, relieved him of command on the field, and took the battle over himself in the face of the counterattack [2].

Washington re-formed the army and ordered General Anthony Wayne to make a holding action. Wayne placed his men behind a hedgerow near an old parsonage, and there, in the nomination's words, "raged the heart of the battle, with the 100 degree temperature taking almost as heavy a toll as the gunfire" [2]. Wayne's men also sprang an ambush from a position the park's telling calls the "Point of Woods" [1]. On Wayne's right, an American artillery battery gained Combs Hill and, in the nomination's phrase, "with deadly accuracy enfiladed the attacking British" [2]. The State Park Service records the artillery duel at three hours, the largest of the war, and records the cost plainly: "By the end of the long day, over 600 men were dead, dying or wounded on both sides" [1].

The battle ended shortly after five o'clock, with the British withdrawn to the far side of the middle ravine and the Americans holding the ground [2]. In the night, Clinton slipped away to Sandy Hook, where his army and its train were taken by water to New York [2]. The result, as both the nomination and the park read it, was a draw on the field and something larger underneath: Washington "had received unmistakeable evidence that the training and discipline imparted to the army by von Steuben at Valley Forge had created a new Continental Army" — troops who "had grappled with the finest troops of the British army… and they had held their own" [2].

The ground itself was a working farmscape, and the park keeps that fact in front of the visitor. Seven families farmed these fields in 1778 [1]. The Craig family was one of them. The park's account: "As the British approached, the Craig family loaded children, household goods and food into two wagons and fled to safety" [1]. The Historic American Buildings Survey's written record for the Craig House, quoting an account published in 1904, adds what the family left behind: before leaving, Mrs. Craig put the family's silver in a kettle and sank it in the open well; British soldiers were buried behind the house; Lord Stirling's artillery stood on the high ground on the westerly side of the farm; and a battle map of the period marks the house "John Craig. British hospital" [3]. Those details are carried here as the record carries them — a tradition quoted in a federal survey's data pages, not a verified inventory.

The Old Tennent Church, on the high ground northwest of the main American position, survived the battle and everything since; the nomination calls it "a precise reference point in determining the battle" [2]. The Craig House and its barn, 700 yards north of the middle ravine, were completely restored, and the nomination describes the house as it still stands: "a two-story, square, shingled frame house," with two additions on its east end — a one-and-a-half-story frame wing and a single-story frame shed [2]. In the 1990s the park restored the exteriors of the Craig House and the Rhea-Applegate House, reconstructed historic fences, lanes and woodlot, and restored the apple orchard, bringing the landscape back toward its 1778 appearance [1]. The Visitor Center stands on Combs Hill, the artillery ground [1].

### The Molly Pitcher telling — lore, labeled as such

The name visitors bring to this field is Molly Pitcher. The National Park Service's telling, on its women's-history pages, runs this way: "Molly Pitcher" was a nickname given to women who carried water to soldiers in battle; at Monmouth, the story goes, Mary Ludwig Hays carried water through the fight, and when her husband was wounded she took his place at his cannon and worked it for the rest of the day [4]. The Park Service itself keeps the telling and the record apart: it says Hays is "rumored to be the real Molly Pitcher of legend," and that the legend likely grew from more than one woman's service [4].

What is documented is narrower, and it stands on its own. Mary Ludwig Hays followed the army as a camp follower with her husband, an artilleryman; he was wounded at Monmouth; and in 1822 the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania granted her a pension — not as a widow's benefit, but, in the Park Service's words, for her own services in the war [4]. The park's Visitor Center takes up the same ground honestly: its exhibits invite visitors to "discover the real 'Molly Pitcher' and learn how battlefield archaeologists found evidence of her" [1]. This dossier prints the cannon story as lore, sourced to its telling, and the pension as record. The two are not merged.

## 3. What's gone

The eastern scene of the battle is gone as ground. The nomination records that the first clash — Lee's attack east of the village, around Lake Topanemus and the east ravine — lies where "the present town of Freehold… has over grown the historic crossroads settlement of Monmouth Courthouse" [2]. The landmark boundary deliberately excludes the eastern ground because it has been, in the nomination's words, "severely intruded upon" [2]. The historic bridge over Weamaconk Creek is gone too; the nomination notes a railroad right-of-way crossing the creek near the original bridge site, "of which no evidence remains" [2]. The hedgerow the Americans held was a farm feature, not a monument; it survives as a restored landscape element in the park's ongoing return of the ground to its 1778 appearance, not as untouched 1778 growth [1][2]. The seven farms are gone as working farms, though the Craig House, its barn, and the Rhea-Applegate House remain. Nothing in the sources checked counts the dead by name or by side beyond the park's figure of over 600 dead, dying, or wounded; no burial ground within the park is described in them [1][2].

## 4. What's still there

The western battleground itself: fields, orchards, woods, and the ravines and brooks — Wemrock Brook, Weamaconk Creek, the middle ravine — that shaped the fight [2]. The Craig House and barn, restored, standing 700 yards north of the middle ravine [2]. The Rhea-Applegate House, its exterior restored in the 1990s [1]. The Old Tennent Church on its high ground northwest of the field [2]. Combs Hill, with the Visitor Center on it, where the American battery stood [1]. Wayside exhibits along a walking trail lay out the battle on the ground where it happened, and living-history programming runs in season — military reenactments, an annual reenactment of the battle, lectures, and a sutler area [1]. The park also carries 25 miles of trails, picnic areas, and a playground, but the core of the place is the field itself [1].

## 5. Standing there

Visitor order, conditional and plain. This is reflection ground: walk it slowly, read the waysides, and leave the field as found.

1. **Visitor Center, Combs Hill.** Orientation first — exhibits, the "real Molly Pitcher" display, and the view from the artillery position the battle turned on [1].
2. **The hedgerow and parsonage ground.** The heart of the second fight, read against the nomination's description of Wayne's line [2].
3. **Craig House.** Open for tours on Sunday afternoons in season; the farm the armies passed through, and the house the battle map marked as a British hospital [1][3].
4. **Old Tennent Church ground.** The reference point on the high ground; church ground is the congregation's — view from the public road and treat it accordingly [2].
5. **The ravines and brooks.** Wemrock Brook and Weamaconk Creek from the trails; the terrain is the argument of the whole battle [2].

## 6. Unresolved / gaps

- **Nominated acreage vs. the modern park.** The 1975 nomination describes a landmark of 1,450 acres and says the park was "not yet open" as it was written [2]. Larger acreage figures for the present-day park circulate in secondary sources, but no primary source checked for this dossier states the park's current acreage. The nomination figure stands, dated; the modern figure is left blank rather than borrowed.
- **Craig House building dates.** The State Park Service dates the farmhouse's Dutch-framed kitchen to 1746 [1]. The HABS written record dates the house in two campaigns — 1704–1717 for the minor unit, 1747 for the major unit — and names John Craig (d. 1724) and Samuel Craig (1708–1747) as builders [3]. The index record for the survey gives the span 1717/1747. The conflict prints unreconciled.
- **The nomination's UTM references are not legible** in the digitized copy at NPGallery — the table is garbled in the scan. No coordinate is transcribed from it; see §10.
- **The Molly Pitcher cannon story is lore.** It is printed as the telling, sourced to the National Park Service's own framing of it [4]. The documented record is the 1822 Pennsylvania pension granted for Mary Ludwig Hays's own services [4]. Nothing in the sources checked documents any individual woman working a gun at Monmouth.
- **Casualty figures.** The park's "over 600 men… dead, dying or wounded on both sides" is the only figure carried [1]. The nomination gives none. No other number is printed.
- **Craig House tradition details** — the silver in the well, the burials behind the house — reach this dossier as a 1904 account quoted in the HABS data pages [3]. They are printed as tradition, not as findings.
