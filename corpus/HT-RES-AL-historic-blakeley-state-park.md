## 2. Story
**Historic Blakeley State Park / Fort Blakely · Research draft · Quiet reflection · Fred**

The park preserves two histories that overlap in the ground. Blakeley was chartered by the Mississippi Territory in 1814. The park describes a commercial town with a newspaper, builders and steamboat work and a contraction after the 1820s that it attributes to economic factors and repeated yellow fever. The old Baldwin County courthouse and jail foundations date from around 1820 in the operator's guide. By 1865 the town was still the county seat but had lost much of its earlier population. The park identifies colonial and Indigenous evidence in the wider landscape. No burial or cultural-site position is published here. [2–3]

In March 1865 Confederate Captain George Shortridge Jr. described Blakely to his family as a “wild romantic looking spot,” in the edited transcription printed by his former university chapter. He wrote before he and other men of the 62nd Alabama Infantry were captured. T. Semmes Favrot identifies the source manuscript in the Shortridge family papers at the Briscoe Center and discloses minor spelling and punctuation changes. This is a transcript quotation. [4]

At the same place, men of the United States Colored Troops did the work of a siege and the final attack. A. Watson Webber's April 11 report places the 51st U.S. Colored Infantry at the front from April 2, except for a relief before the final fighting. His report praises “the officers and men of my command” and records unnamed suffering alongside that praise. The printed footnote acknowledges that the detailed list of two enlisted men killed and other casualties was omitted from the transcription of the official record. On April 9 Alexander S. Millard wrote of the assault and surrender. Auburn's diary transcription is separate from the federal command report. Both can be read without translating the ground into an itinerary. [5–7]

## 3. Then and now
The surviving resources include earthworks and foundations. The operator distinguishes the early courthouse site from the later Civil War works, and calls the visible building outlines at Washington Square interpretive “ghost structures.” They represent earlier construction. The Alabama Legislature established Historic Blakeley Authority in 1981. [2–3]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
- The park uses Blakeley for the town and sometimes the fort. The Army publication and the NPS battle entry use Blakely for the fort. Both spellings are kept. [2, 5, 7]
- Millard's diary gives 1,500 men at the surrender he describes. The NPS summary gives a Confederate force of about 4,000 and an estimated Confederate casualties total of 3,700. The diary and summary do not count a clearly identical unit or category. No aggregate prisoner figure is adopted here. [6–7]
- The operator calls it the last major battle of the war; the NPS describes it as essentially the last combined-force battle. The precise dated local claim is the fall of Fort Blakely on April 9, 1865. [2, 7]
- The original federal nomination, complete casualty list and a digital facsimile of Shortridge's letter were not retrieved. The free chapter transcript identifies editorial changes. No modern coordinates or visitor boundary independent of the battlefield were established. [2–6]
