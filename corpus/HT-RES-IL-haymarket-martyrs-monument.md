# Haymarket Martyrs' Monument, Forest Home Cemetery — the Haymarket defendants' graves and labor's memorial ground
HT-RES-IL-haymarket-martyrs-monument · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The movement that ended here began with a date. In 1884 the Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions called for May 1, 1886 to open a nationwide campaign for the eight-hour day. When the day came, the NPS summary records some 80,000 workers packing Michigan Avenue in Chicago, the center of the movement, where the anarchist International Working People's Association and organizers such as Albert Parsons — printer, Knights of Labor member, and editor of the labor paper *The Alarm* — had built the campaign. On May 3, Chicago police fired on workers confronting strikebreakers at the McCormick works, killing two. A protest meeting was called for the evening of May 4 at Haymarket Square. [1]

The meeting was nearly over — Parsons had already left, and about 200 people remained — when 176 police carrying repeater rifles advanced to disperse it. An unknown person threw a bomb. One policeman was killed by the blast itself; in the panic and crossfire that followed, the eventual toll in the NPS account reached seven policemen and four workers. The bomb thrower has never been identified. Martial law, warrantless searches, and the shutdown of union newspapers followed across the country. Eight men were prosecuted not for throwing the bomb — the state never proved who did — but, in Governor John Peter Altgeld's later summary of the case, on the theory that their past speeches and printed words had incited the unknown thrower. Louis Lingg was accused of throwing it despite a witness placing him more than a mile away. [1, 6]

The two-month trial ended with seven men sentenced to hang and Oscar Neebe sentenced to fifteen years. On November 11, 1887, August Spies, Albert Parsons, Adolph Fischer, and George Engel were hanged. Lingg was found dead in his cell the day before. Governor Richard Oglesby commuted the sentences of Samuel Fielden and Michael Schwab to life imprisonment. In June 1893 Altgeld pardoned Fielden, Schwab, and Neebe outright, condemning the trial in a long public statement as a miscarriage of justice — a judgment on the packed jury, the judge's conduct, and the absence of evidence connecting the defendants to the bomb. The five men who died in 1887 were buried at German Waldheim Cemetery, in the NPS nomination's account the only Chicago-area cemetery that would accept their remains. Schwab was buried beside the monument in 1898 and Neebe in 1916. The Pioneer Aid and Support Association, organized to support the defendants' families, raised the monument by subscription: a sixteen-foot granite shaft on a two-stepped base, sculpted by Albert Weinert, carrying bronze figures of Justice standing over a fallen worker, the names of the men, Spies's attributed last words, and a bronze plaque of Altgeld's pardon. It was dedicated in June 1893, days around the pardons, after a march from Chicago. The American Federation of Labor's recommendation that May 1 be kept as International Labor Day carried the Chicago story around the world; the monument became, and remains, the physical place that memory returns to. It was designated a National Historic Landmark on February 18, 1997. [1, 2, 5, 6, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Two grounds carry this story. The meeting and bombing ground is Haymarket Square in Chicago, at Des Plaines and Randolph Streets; the NHL nomination record states that the square lost its physical integrity, in part to Kennedy Expressway construction in the 1950s, which is why the landmark is the burial ground and not the square. The burial ground is the former German Waldheim Cemetery (established 1873), merged with Forest Home Cemetery (established 1876) in 1969, in Forest Park. The Haymarket Square site itself is a separate lead on the sweep list, flagged NO-ANCHOR?, and is not the anchor for this dossier. [2, 3, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Forest Home Cemetery, 863 S. Des Plaines Ave., Forest Park — public cemetery grounds during posted visiting hours, monument and graves read from the cemetery's roads and paths. The Illinois Labor History Society holds the deed to the monument and is legally responsible for its care, and runs guided tours of the cemetery in season. Give funerals, burials, and grieving families the ground without exception; this is a working cemetery first and a historic site second. [3, 7, 8]

## 5. Public progression

Enter from Des Plaines Avenue during visiting hours and walk to the monument section, keeping to cemetery roads and paths. Read the monument front first — Justice over the fallen worker, the date 1887 on the steps — then walk around it: the names, and Altgeld's pardon in bronze, are the argument the monument makes in its own defense. Then read outward to the graves around it, the defendants first and Radical Row after, in quiet. **Strongest story moment · A pardon cast in bronze.** The state that hanged four men in 1887 printed its own governor's judgment that the trial was unjust onto the back of their monument in 1893 — the verdict and its reversal stand on the same stone, and the visitor has to hold both. This is burial ground: no scoring, no spectacle, and no lingering past the 4 p.m. gate. The ILHS's seasonal guided tours are the deeper version of this walk when their schedule allows. [1–3, 7, 8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Dedication-date conflict printed in full: the NPS place page gives June 23, 1893 for the monument's dedication; nomination-based and other accounts give June 25, 1893. Both dates fall in the same week as Altgeld's pardon (June 26, 1893 in the pardon text as published by UMKC's trial archive; "June 1893" in the NPS summary). This dossier prints both dedication dates and treats neither as settled. [1, 2, 6]
- Visiting-hours conflict printed in full: the cemetery's current pages give 8:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m. daily, while older dawn-to-dusk wording still appears on the cemetery's own site. A September 2026 Forest Park Review report states the 8–4 hours were newly imposed after theft and vandalism and were being petitioned against. Follow 8–4 and confirm by phone. [3, 4]
- Phone-number conflict on the operator's own site: the cemetery's history page body gives 708.366.1900 while its contact footer prints 708.336.1900 — a transposed digit in one of the two. The 366 number appears in the site's body text in more than one place; verify before relying on either. [3]
- Operator timeline error, printed plainly: the cemetery's own history timeline states the monument "becomes a National Historic Landmark" in 1897. The landmark designation was February 18, 1997 (NRIS 97000343). The cemetery page is wrong by a century; this dossier follows the NPS record. [2, 3]
- Death-toll framing differs by source and by moment counted: the NPS summary gives one policeman killed by the bomb itself and an eventual seven policemen and four workers dead; Northwestern's Homicide in Chicago project records Officer Matthias J. Degan killed by the bomb with other officers wounded, some by fellow officers' fire. Counts that name a single "killed at Haymarket" figure without saying which moment they count should not be repeated. [1, 5]
- The bomb thrower is unidentified in every source checked this run. Any account naming a thrower as fact exceeds the record.
- No cemetery photography or group-visit policy was published on the pages checked this run; ask at the office before any organized visit.

## 7. Historical photo records

No historical photograph is adopted here. **Inspected and held, not adopted:** the Illinois Labor History Society's restoration pages, which record that the monument's bronze floral decoration was stolen in the early 1980s and was recreated by conservator Andrzej Dajnowski from historic photographs, with rubber molds of the floral piece and the Altgeld plaque made against future damage — the society's pages document the historic-photograph basis of the restoration but do not publish the underlying photographs with collection, credit, or rights information this run could verify. The NPS nomination (NRIS 97000343) is the controlling record for the monument's fabric; its photo plates were not individually inspected frame-by-frame this run, and no image from it is reproduced or cleared here. A present-day aligned view from any historic stance remains unverified. No Photo Point. [2, 8]

## 8. Optional photo-tag observations

Quiet witness only. Reading and planning are free and available without an account, location sharing or contribution. This is an active cemetery and a place of continuing labor pilgrimage; reflection posture throughout, and family burials nearby are not part of the visit.
