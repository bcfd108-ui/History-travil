## 2. The story
**A neighborhood a child knew as home.** In her May 19, 2021 testimony, Viola Ford Fletcher recalled parents and siblings, neighbors and friends, and a Greenwood household in which she felt safe. She told Congress that after her family fled the white mob, she never finished school past fourth grade. She described shipyard work during the war and a longer working life in domestic service. Listen to the continuity she refused to let the country forget: “I live through the Massacre every day.” [4]

**A family could survive and still lose its prospects.** Her brother Hughes Van Ellis testified, “We were made refugees in our own country.” He described having to start from nothing and contrasted his wartime service with the segregation he returned to. These are different accounts from members of the same family; this draft does not attribute his sister's recalled childhood observations to him. Lessie Benningfield Randle separately testified about the home she shared with her grandmother in Greenwood. Their statements describe home, work and the demand to be heard. [4,5]

## 3. Then / now
The Library of Congress places a 1915 Sanborn record alongside aftermath documentation and notes that Greenwood residents began rebuilding soon after the May 31–June 1, 1921 massacre. Rebuilding a business district did not replace lost relatives, schooling, savings or household security. Today's Greenwood Rising is an interpretive institution in the district, not a verified witness's dwelling or parcel. [3–6]
