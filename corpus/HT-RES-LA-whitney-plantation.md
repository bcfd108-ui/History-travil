# Whitney Plantation — Anna and Victor Haydel

Labeled research draft · Regional March · Louisiana OP03 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

November 20 operator check · Whitney’s visitor-center exhibits are free from 9:30 a.m. to 4:15 p.m. on open days. Tuesday and Thanksgiving Day are closed. The paid outdoor tour uses posted gravel paths. Only the guided tour gives upper Big House access. Recheck the operator for holiday and severe-weather changes before leaving. [1]

## 2. Story

In November 1860, an estate inventory set prices beside the names of Anna and Victor, an enslaved mother and her son. In the cropped manuscript image reproduced by researcher Michael N. Henderson, their names stand on consecutive lines. Figures of 800 and 100 are visible down its right edge. Henderson’s presentation translates the valuations as 800 piastres for Victor and 100 for Anna. The inventory’s author counted people as assets. It did not set down what mother and son wanted for themselves. [2–3]

A church certificate reproduced alongside the inventory names Victor Haydel, Antoine Haydel and Anna. Its reproduction is captioned with the December 13, 1835 baptism date. It is a later certificate drawn from the register. The museum says Marcelin Haydel bought Anna in New Orleans, that she worked in the Big House, and that Antoine sexually assaulted her. The certificate records the relationship of the parents to the child. The strength and nature of the coercion belong to the museum’s historical interpretation, with Anna held in bondage and Antoine part of the owning family. Antoine was the brother of Marie Azélie Haydel, whose estate inventory includes both Anna and her son. [2–5]

Henderson also prints a family account from Curtis M. Graves, who heard it from his grandfather Elphége Haydel and whose account historian Ibrahima Seck published. In that telling, Anna “was bought from the slave market in New Orleans.” This is a transmitted descendant account, printed in a modern source; it is neither Anna’s own voice nor an original sale record. In a separate dated response to Henderson, Catherine M. Haydel identifies herself as a descendant through Victor’s son. The museum traces Victor and his wife Celeste after emancipation into paid domestic work, farming and land ownership nearby. The inventory and the family story stop being two pieces of evidence about property once a reader follows Victor and Celeste beyond the estate. [2, 5]

## 3. Then and now

Then — Sugar and other crops depended on enslaved labor. The house where the museum places Anna’s domestic work and the inventory written after the owner’s death record different sides of the same household. Later twentieth-century preservation documentation identified features still associated with the historic plantation district. [2–5, 7–8]

Now — The nonprofit museum uses the standing Big House, memorials and a collection of buildings with different origins. The operator identifies two displayed cabins as coming from the neighboring Mialaret Plantation. The 1938 Johnston frame and the 2021 Highsmith frame show the same elevation at different distances. The open colonnade and right stair remain, with changed vegetation and shutters. Only the 2021 frame is adopted. No exact repeat point. [1, 5–13]

## 5. Public progression

Start at the museum’s signed entrance and see the visitor-center exhibits. Continue on the posted inland tour only if it is open and conditions permit. The operator distinguishes the upper house guided section from the audio visit. Save the outdoors for another open day if weather or staff instructions keep the paths closed. [1]

**Strongest story moment — Two names on the same page.** At the end of the signed museum visit, read Anna and Victor together in the inventory and beside the baptism certificate. A piece of paperwork that prices a person also preserves a name a descendant can follow. End in quiet reflection. [2–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The original 1860 complete inventory, the original 1835 parish register and the purchase act for Anna were not retrieved. The annotated manuscript detail and later baptism certificate were inspected as pixels at the researcher’s reproductions. Several certificate entries are faint. Names, evidentiary scope and supplemental transcription are reported separately. [2–4]
- The descendant’s reported age for Anna at Victor’s birth is consistent with the estate appraiser’s approximate age in 1860. Henderson’s display narrative identifies a child-sized figure with Anna. That display raises a question about the represented age, which the original baptism and purchase records would help settle. The museum uses the year 1835 without asserting Anna’s age in its history. [2–5]
- The operator’s tour page repeats text for 14 and 15 stops. Use the operator’s current map onsite. The 1992 nomination records a historic district at the time of survey. The museum’s current collection and relocation statements describe a later arrangement. [1, 7–8]
- The web reader returned HTTP 403 on the LOC item. Direct HTTPS returned the full item HTML and the LOC image service supplied the inspected scan. [6, 9]

## 7. Historical photo records

**Historical frame held.** The inspected 1938 Frances Benjamin Johnston frame, LOC item 2017887332 and reproduction LC-DIG-csas-01616, shows Whitney’s raised shuttered gallery, the right stair, roof chimneys, and a foreground gate and fence. LOC reports no known restrictions, but the item supplies no affirmative open license or publication history sufficient for a public-domain conclusion here. Credit this as an archive reference only. [6, 9–10]

**Contemporary photo adoption.** Carol M. Highsmith Archive, LOC 2021755920, LC-DIG-highsm-67699, February 26, 2021. The actual frame shows the same shuttered elevation, right stair, column line and chimneys beyond a lawn. Highsmith dedicated her photographs to the public domain, and LOC identifies this item as her original digital file. **Printed credit:** Library of Congress, Prints & Photographs Division, photograph by Carol M. Highsmith, February 26, 2021, LC-DIG-highsm-67699. The difference in distance and foreground prevents assigning a repeat stance. This is a dated contemporary comparison, with no 2026 access inference. Henderson’s document images are sources only. [2–4, 11–13]
