## 2. The story
This entry fills an agricultural-industry gap rather than adding another railway-town overview. Its central question is how a familiar food changed the organization of work. The depot supplies a place to encounter that question; the museum’s public exhibits supply an invitation, not evidence that every historical process described here is displayed.

**Named primary technology voices:** University of Idaho agricultural engineers **J. W. Martin and Elmer N. Humphrey**, authors of *The Idaho potato harvester*, Agricultural Experiment Station Bulletin 283, June 1951. Their contemporary report describes machines developed with farmers and local mechanics, backed by engineering assistance. Its stated motivations include labor availability and production costs. It records a research program sponsored in 1947, not the invention of all potato mechanization in that year. On printed page 3 they wrote: **“Farmers were willing to give the plan a try.”** The sentence, authors and institutional footnotes were checked in the original scan. [2,3]

Their account pairs practical construction with attention to damage: padding and gentler handling mattered because digging a crop successfully did not mean delivering it intact. This is engineers’ testimony about collaborative trials, not a named farmer’s diary, an anonymous worker’s consent to mechanization, or proof that this exact machine operated in Blackfoot. [3]

**A separate regional family voice:** in a 1991 interview with Rosa Rodríguez, **Rita Padilla Pérez** recalled her family’s life in eastern Idaho. The published transcript around 00:06:55–00:06:59 adds potato harvesting to her father’s work history. This is a family recollection recorded in Idaho Falls, not a Blackfoot employment record and not evidence that her father worked on the machines in Bulletin 283. Only the relevant published transcript passage was used; the audio was not independently heard. [4]

Original interpretive voice: *A machine can lift a potato without lifting the whole story. Behind the harvest are the people who designed equipment, the families who worked the seasons, and the problem of moving food without destroying its value.* This is our prose, not a historical quotation. The two records are deliberately held beside each other, not fused into an invented encounter.

This draft does not narrate Tribal agricultural history. Adding it requires relevant Tribal-published material first; the absence here must not be converted into a claim that regional history began with commercial settler agriculture.

## 3. Then / now
- **Then:** the bulletin documents farmer-built and mechanic-built equipment and an institutional effort to improve it. **Now:** a museum can interpret innovation, but it is not a working experimental farm. [3]
- **Then:** the research record describes a production problem from an engineering perspective. **Now:** its priorities can be compared with a family’s recollection of seasonal work without pretending either source represents all workers. [3,4]
- **Then:** this was a railway depot. **Now:** its established public use is a museum. The operator dates the building to 1913; that date is not an opening date for the museum or a claim that historical passenger access survives unchanged. [1]

The useful contrast is not quaint tools versus modern efficiency. It is the change in who performed which tasks, what remained difficult, and whose account survived.

## 4. What's still there
The museum publicly interprets potato history, growing and harvesting, and industry development inside the former Oregon Short Line depot. Its current visitor page establishes the institution and subject matter; it does not inventory individual historical machines or guarantee that the bulletin’s equipment appears in the galleries. [1]

**Surviving evidence independent of exhibits:** the university preserves the 1951 paper, drawings and photographic figures, and the Hispanic Oral History Project preserves Pérez’s interview and transcript. Those records can support a reading companion even when a particular object is absent from display. Their preservation does not grant unrestricted reproduction rights. [2–4]

**Excluded from visitor access:** railway property, historical loading approaches, nearby commercial agriculture and processing sites. An industrial landscape can be historically significant without being a public destination. No crop-field, factory-floor, trackside or private worker-housing stop is included.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Named farmer/worker gap:** Martin and Humphrey are named technical participants; the farmers on page 3 are unnamed. Pérez supplies a named family narrator, not a recovered Blackfoot harvest payroll. A specific local farmer’s or picker’s first-person record remains a worthwhile next unit, not something to fabricate.
- **Geographic scope:** statewide engineering research and an Idaho Falls interview provide regional context. Neither proves a particular Blackfoot company’s use of a machine or employment of a family.
- **Exhibit gap:** exact machine identities, accession numbers, current display positions and comprehensive labor interpretation are not confirmed.
- **Dates:** the catalog’s June 1, 1951 value and the cover’s June 1951 agree at month/year precision; do not turn the catalog’s day into a verified issue-day claim. The 1947 sponsorship predates publication. [2,3]
- **Scan limitation:** the PDF has 22 digital pages but extends to printed page 24, with missing/skipped printed pagination and severe distortion on some sheets. Printed and digital page references must remain separate. OCR is not reliable enough for unchecked quotation.
- **Accessibility/operations:** doorway geometry, room-by-room access, temporary closures and present exhibit inventory need independent verification. No contact was made.
