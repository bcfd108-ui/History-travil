## 2. The story
**Start with the living community, not an empty wilderness.** The Burns Paiute Tribe's own published history identifies its people primarily as descendants of the Wadatika Band of Northern Paiutes. It recounts forced removal, return to a landless situation, survival and continuing cultural preservation. In its own words: **“Our elders are our most precious ‘cultural resource’”**. The quotation preserves the wording while using single quotation marks inside the excerpt. This is a living Tribal account, not a vanished-culture preface to federal management. [4]

**Original interpretive proposition:** conservation has a human history because places are protected through work, memory, institutions and continuing obligations. A refuge's founding date cannot be treated as the beginning of people's relationship with its landscape.

The Friends of Malheur historical profile by Peter Pearsall identifies **George M. Benson**, beginning federal refuge service in 1918, as a game warden who preferred the title refuge protector. It describes bird banding with local children, his taxidermy work and the museum dedicated to him in 1953. This is a community organization's historical synthesis, not Benson's own diary. His wife **Ethel** appears in the household account; her work and personal perspective are not supplied in sufficient detail to reconstruct. [5]

A contemporary administrative record adds a different kind of human evidence. An indexed 1949 narrative signed by Superintendent **J. C. Scharff**, dated May 20, reports Benson's January 31 retirement and identifies incoming clerk **Gladys Howe**. Direct download failed, so the available indexed passage is a primary-record lead with limited inspection, not a fully checked facsimile. No doubtful OCR wording is quoted. [6]

**A fully read named primary voice:** in a December 29, 2020 reflection published by Friends of Malheur, Refuge Manager **Jeff Mackay** wrote, **“During the past year Refuge personnel have stepped-up taking on additional duties”**. His account specifically credits **Suzanne McConnell** with performing administrative duties during a vacancy. These are dated observations about 2020, not current staffing facts or information for visiting work areas. [7]

This makes a useful interpretive contrast: the popular image is birds and open space; the human record also contains patient observation, clerical work and responsibilities passed between people. The worker does not disappear merely because the visitor came for wildlife.

**Original visitor narration:** Ask what it takes to keep caring for a place after the founding proclamation has been signed. Someone watches, records, maintains and teaches; someone keeps the paperwork moving. And the place already belongs to histories that a federal institution cannot replace. A good visit should make those relationships more visible without demanding access to every part of the landscape.

The entry is not a guide to the 2016 occupation. No tactical chronology, occupied-building route, operational infrastructure or access to sensitive locations is included.

## 3. Then / now
- **Tribal continuity:** use the Tribe's own account of endurance and current preservation work, not a story ending with removal. [4]
- **Early refuge labor:** Benson's public historical profile supplies a named conservation worker, but it cannot stand in for an entire staff, household or community. [5]
- **Administrative continuity:** the 1949 personnel note and Mackay's 2020 reflection show that conservation institutions depend on people doing everyday jobs. The first remains an indexed lead; the second was read directly. [6][7]
- **Current public interpretation:** the Visitor Center is a seasonal facility. Its closure is itself a boundary to respect, not an invitation to substitute offices or field stations. [1]

Do not equate the earlier Malheur Indian Reservation with the later wildlife refuge merely because both use the same place name. Different institutions, histories and boundaries require separate treatment.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Now:** read remotely. This dossier does not send a visitor to a seasonally closed interpretive center.

**When the Visitor Center's opening is freshly confirmed:** proposed 30–45 minutes, an editorial allowance rather than an operator tour length.

1. Read the Tribe's short self-history before arrival. Carry the distinction between living homeland and federal refuge into the visit, without making a Tribal community or cultural site an uninvited stop.
2. Use only the designated public Visitor Center arrival and open visitor doorway. If closed, stop the itinerary rather than trying administrative buildings.
3. Read available interpretation on the refuge's history. Ask whether it identifies the people doing the work. Do not claim that an unnamed display is Benson's or that every mounted specimen is his work.
4. Compare the dated Benson account with Mackay's administrative-work testimony. Both can be considered in the public visitor room; no access to former homes or staff workplaces is necessary.
5. **End at the strongest story moment:** protecting a place requires people, but visitors do not gain a right to go everywhere in order to understand them. Put the Tribal statement about elders beside the institution's account of work. Ask whose knowledge the interpretation makes visible and whose voice still needs to be heard.

The ending is reflective, not a hunt. No wildlife encounter, photograph, purchase or interaction with staff is required. Bad weather, a closed entrance or new restriction converts the plan back to the archive version without loss of the historical question.
