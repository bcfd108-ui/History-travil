## 2. The story
**Linda Eben Jones**, a Northern Paiute member of the Reno-Sparks Indian Colony and Stewart student in 1963–66, is a coauthor of a 2021 public-history essay. Her first-person passage recalls seeing her graduation photograph in the new museum and feeling again her relief at leaving school. She also values friendships and adults who helped her. Her concise statement is **“We all had different memories of Stewart”**: her family included multiple generations of students whose experiences were not interchangeable. This is her published first-person writing within a coauthored essay, not a newly conducted interview or a claim to have heard an original recording. [3]

Her contribution changes the visitor's task. Do not decide whether a warm memory cancels a painful one. Listen to who is speaking, when they attended, and what they chose to share. A building can be restored without resolving the experience of every person who lived inside an institution.

The current Nevada Department of Native American Affairs describes the school within federal forced-assimilation policy and emphasizes Native resilience and continuing presence. Its museum relationship supports public interpretation, but the department is a state body, not a substitute for every Tribal Nation's authority or a family's own account. [4]

The museum describes itself as community-created and developed with alumni, including contemporary Native art rather than treating Native people as figures who exist only in the past. [1] This draft's landing is therefore not the architecture: it is the difference between an institution keeping records about children and former students choosing the words through which visitors meet them.
