# Bethany Deaconess Hospital, American Falls — the hospital the reservoir moved

## 1. Trip gate

**Time-sensitive.** The 1926–27 hospital building at **500 Pocatello Ave.** (later the Power County courthouse annex and museum) was **condemned in 2024** after storm water damage, mold, and foundation failure, and Power County is preparing to **demolish** it. County minutes record a finalized SHPO/EPA memorandum of agreement and demolition bid specifications (Oct. 13, 2026), bidding expected to open in late November with **construction beginning in January** (Oct. 19, 2026), and a Nov. 9, 2026 agenda item for a further RFP update. As of this run the building is **standing but unsafe and closed**. [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]

- **Exterior from the public street (until demolition):** the building fronts Pocatello Avenue beside the town square and can be seen from the public sidewalk. Do not approach a condemned structure or enter any work zone; once abatement/demolition begins, any posted closure governs. [1, 4]
- **Museum anchor (primary, durable):** the **Power County Museum**, which operated inside this building from 2007 until the 2024 condemnation, reopened at **592 Gifford Ave., American Falls**, with a grand reopening on **April 25, 2026**. The county's museum page confirms the move ("We are now located at 592 Gifford Ave.") and lists a hospital display among its exhibits; regular hours were not posted in the sources found. [8, 9]

## 2. The story

The hospital's roots run to Mennonite farm families. In 1905 General Conference Mennonites near Newton, Kansas, answered Carey Act advertising for irrigated land, met rancher-businessman **W. H. Philbrick**, and from 1906 settled at Aberdeen in Bingham County. The **Bethany Deaconess Hospital Association of Goessel, Kansas** sent Mennonite deaconess nurses to the Aberdeen–American Falls area, and in **1913** a three-story stone hospital was built in the original American Falls townsite, run by those nurses. The association later helped found hospitals in Soda Springs and Burley. [1]

The American Falls Dam ended that first hospital. After the 1919–20 drought, irrigators and the federal government combined to build a reservoir whose pool covered most of the old townsite. The **Bureau of Reclamation** bought out more than 1,200 property holders, purchased higher land, and paid for a new planned town; acquiring the reservoir lands took five years and cost nearly twice the dam itself. The 1913 stone hospital could not be moved; it was dismantled, and patients were quartered temporarily in an old bakery in the Hessler Block. [1]

By deed of **November 9, 1926**, Reclamation conveyed Lots 7–8, Block 49, of the Reclamation Addition, on condition that a hospital building worth at least $10,000 be built and maintained. Power County levied a special tax, added **$17,500** in Reclamation reimbursements, and built a new one-story Craftsman-influenced brick hospital on a modified-H plan (107 × 57 ft, raised basement). **John Hayward** of Idaho Falls drew the plans with local physician **Dr. C. F. Schiltz**; Pocatello contractor **S. H. Anderson** supervised; patients moved in by **January 1927**. It had 21 rooms and 42 beds, large and small operating rooms, a nursery, diet kitchen, and nurses' dormitory. The deaconess nurses ran it, with Aberdeen Mennonite church members as directors; county ward patients paid $2.50 a day, out-of-county $3.00, and Aberdeen's Mennonites — though from Bingham County — the in-county rate in thanks for their help. [1]

After Dr. Schiltz died in **1931**, the commissioners renamed it **Schiltz Memorial Hospital**, by then wholly county-run. Ordered by the State Board of Health in 1958 to renovate or replace it, the county built a new **Power County Hospital** (opened April 1961); the old building was the only hospital for Power County and outlying areas from 1927 until then, and many longtime residents were born there. [1]

In July 1961 the county auctioned the building. The new owners ran it as the **"Great West Apartments" for former patients of State Hospital South**, the state psychiatric hospital at Blackfoot — a chapter the nomination records in one sentence. It was sold in 1969, bought by **American Legion Roland Evans Post No. 3** in 1973 as a meeting hall and lounge, and listed in the National Register in **1995** (ref. 95000507; nomination by Madeline Buckendorf, July 28, 1994). Power County bought it back in the early 2000s, and it became the county annex — home to county departments, youth groups, and, from spring **2007**, the Power County Museum — until the 2024 condemnation. [1, 3, 8]

**Reflection (Family 2 cross-reference):** the people who lived here in 1961–69 after discharge from State Hospital South are not named in any source reviewed, and should not be. Their presence is part of the building's history and is recorded here with the same weight as the births.

## 4. What's still there

- **Visible from the public street (time-limited):** the north-facing brick hospital with its twelve concrete steps to a full-story Craftsman portico, low front gables with stucco ends and exposed rafters, and brick-infilled basement windows (1970s). A 2022 photograph shows "MUSEUM" and "Power County Museum / Gerald Fehringer Youth Center" signs, since superseded. Per the 2024 KIFI report the outer wall was visibly leaning. [1, 3, 10]
- **Planned salvage:** the Museum Board asked for about 100 bricks and a 3-D model plaque; a resident asked for salvaged bricks for a church pathway with a historical marker (Oct. 26, 2026 minutes). Whether these were adopted into the final bid was not confirmed. [7]
- **Museum (592 Gifford Ave.):** Power County history collections including a hospital display, research room, digitized photographs, archaeology section. [8, 9]
- **Gone:** the 1913 stone hospital (dismantled, c. 1925–26). [1]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Start at the Power County Museum, 592 Gifford Ave.** Confirm hours first (none posted in sources found). Ask for the hospital display and the town-relocation story. [8, 9]
2. **If the building still stands, view 500 Pocatello Ave. from the public sidewalk** on Pocatello Avenue by the town square. Do not approach the condemned building or any demolition work zone. [1, 4]
3. **Read the 1926 deed condition on site:** a Reclamation lot granted only for hospital use, a structural reminder that the whole town was rebuilt by agreement. [1]
4. **Close with the reservoir:** the first hospital, like most of the old town, gave way to the American Falls Reservoir. [1]

- **Demolition status:** as of the latest primary record found (Nov. 9, 2026 agenda), demolition had not started; January construction start is a plan from Oct. 19, 2026 minutes. Re-verify before publication. A third-party summary site (citizenportal.ai) was used only as a finder; all statements here rest on county minutes/agendas. [5, 6, 7]
- **SHPO/EPA MOA terms:** finalized per Oct. 13, 2026 minutes; the MOA text (mitigation, e.g., documentation) was not found. Printed as a gap.
- **Museum hours and Bethany content:** the county page lists a hospital display; whether it concerns Bethany specifically was not confirmed. Regular hours not posted. [8]
- **County re-acquisition:** "early 2000s" per KIFI; the deed date was not located. The nomination (1994) records Legion ownership. [1, 3]
- **Building age wording:** KIFI says the annex was built "almost a decade after the county was established" (Power County created 1913; building 1926–27) — imprecise; the nomination's 1926–27 dates are used. [1, 3]
- **Hayward's role:** the nomination calls John Hayward an Idaho Falls "builder" in Section 7 and "contractor" in Section 8, while the form's Architect/Builder field lists "Hayward, John (architect)".
- **Address wording:** the nomination's street line reads "500 Pocatello Highway Avenue" (OCR/typed correction); 500 Pocatello Ave. is used here. [1]
