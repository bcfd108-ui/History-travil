# Lemhi Pass and Sacajawea country · Read from Salmon

History Travel · Idaho · Draft for Dash QC · Desk review 18 November 2026

## 2. The story

On August 12, 1805, Meriwether Lewis wrote that he was looking for a road. He found “a large and plain Indian road.” Those words put the expedition in its proper place on the ground. People already knew the crossing and had made a route clear enough for a stranger to follow. Lewis ate the last of the party’s venison that morning. At the divide he saw more mountain ranges, partly snow-covered. The ridge answered one question about drainage and left open the pressing question of how to carry the baggage onward. [5]

Five days later, at the forks east of the divide, the expedition’s journals bring Sacajawea into focus doing identifiable work. Lewis names her, Charbonneau and Labiche as the chain through whom the discussion took place. He records the captains asking for horses to move their goods and a pilot if travel by water proved impossible. Clark identifies Cameahwait as her brother. These are the captains’ written recollections. The text preserves the purpose of the conversation and her work as an interpreter. It does not give a transcript of her words. The August 17 meeting is distinct in place and time from Lewis’s August 12 sightline. [5, 6]

The valley’s history did not end with the expedition getting horses. The Indian Claims Commission records the movement from the Lemhi reservation to Fort Hall in 1907. Salmon’s interpretive center describes the departure as forced and identifies the Agaidika Shoshone relationship to this homeland today. The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes speak publicly about what is at stake in Idaho’s rivers. In a 2021 statement on salmon recovery, Chairman Devon Boyer said, “Our traditional culture is based on the fish and wildlife that provide subsistence for our people.” Read that statement with its date and purpose. It comes from a living government speaking about its future. [7–9]

## 3. Then / now

An established Indigenous crossing became a landmark in the written expedition record. Today public agencies interpret the divide and a city-run center in Salmon presents the Agaidika perspective. The mountains can be seen in the landmark file. The trading conversation can be read in the journals. The history of removal and the Tribes’ current words belong alongside both. [1, 3, 5–10]

## 4. What’s still there

- **View from public ground, seasonal** — pass landscape from designated public day-use areas when the managing forests confirm access. No overnight use. [1, 2]
- **Public interior, seasonal/by appointment** — the Sacajawea Interpretive Center’s exhibition. In the off-season the operator invites callers to request an appointment at 208-756-1188. [3]
- **View from public ground** — the city park’s regular visitor area during published hours. This itinerary provides no stop at a gathering, traditional activity area or community site. [3, 4, 8]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Start in Salmon with the August 12 journal. Follow the change in Lewis’s account from looking for a road to following one. Use the photographs in the landmark file if the pass is snowbound. [5, 10]
2. Turn to August 17 and read the list of interpreters before the requests for horses and a pilot. In an open season the center is a public place to deepen this reading. A pass visit is optional and needs current agency confirmation. [1–4, 6]
3. **Strongest story moment** — finish quietly with the center’s account of 1907 and Chairman Boyer’s statement about the fish. Leave the last words to people with a continuing relationship to the rivers. This can be done from a seat in Salmon without approaching any tribal activity or closed door. [7–9]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The center’s name uses **Sacajawea**. The Nebraska edition indexes **Sacagawea** and prints Lewis’s phonetic forms in the diary. Keep the spelling attached to the speaker or institution. Neither an exact birthplace nor the words she used in August 1805 are established by these sources. A Lemhi Shoshone website linked by NPS did not load during review. No account has been reconstructed from it. [3, 5, 6, 12]

BLM describes early June through October as the ordinarily snow-free byway season. The Forest Service gives late November to early June for one road segment and notes that the two sides can have different conditions. They describe different extents. Neither establishes a November 18 summit drive. [2, 13] No reservation, home, treaty activity area or place of removal is a proposed route waypoint. [1, 2, 7]

## 7. Photo record

**Ray H. Mattison, June 1958.** Two photographs in the landmark file. The first looks along the road approaching the pass from the west. The second is captioned as an east view on the Pacific side and paired with Lewis’s description of mountains to the west. That juxtaposition does not document the direction of Lewis’s gaze in the image. The road trough and the ridge suggest broad orientations, but the captions do not establish a unique bend, safe public stance or second fixed control for a repeat frame. No Photo Point in either case. The first caption records the negative’s then-location at the Region Two Office Library in Omaha. [5, 10]
