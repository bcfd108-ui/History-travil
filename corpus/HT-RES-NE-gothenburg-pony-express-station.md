# Gothenburg Pony Express Station at Ehmen Park

Nebraska OP04 · Research draft for Dash · November 19, 2026

## 2. The story

### A building moved to town

Mrs. C. A. Williams donated the log building, which was taken apart, moved and reassembled at Ehmen Park in 1931. Merrill J. Mattes and Paul Henderson describe a squared-log building that had stood two stories high on the ranch and was rebuilt as one story in town. They distinguish it from a nearby building known as the blacksmith shop. The association credits two Gothenburg teenagers with seeing a use for the cabin when passing tourists stopped at the windows. Their proposal led to an interior open to visitors in the mid-1950s. [1–3]

### What a tablet can tell you

The 1931 monument at the ranch, as transcribed by Mattes and Henderson, claimed, “This is one of the regular stations of the Pony Express.” The park cabin has its own tablet, which the 1960 study describes as giving an 1854 trading-post origin and Pony Express service in 1860–61. These are historical statements about how people remembered the building in the twentieth century. The authors searched the period accounts of Richard Burton and others and found no contemporary identification for a Machette station. They also found two documented stations only sixteen miles apart around the proposed ranch location. They considered several explanations and left the question open. [3]

### Two accounts inside the same agency

The current National Park Service place page calls the cabin the actual station. The NPS historic resource study treats the identification as disputed and reviews the road-ranch histories from which the building might have come. The distinction matters on the ground. This is a real log building with a documented twentieth-century move, a public place to learn how the relay worked and an open question about exactly where these timbers fit into it. Midway, a separate station south of town, stands on private land. [1,4,5]

## 3. Then / now

The building was part of the Upper 96 Ranch before its move. Today it is a city-owned museum building at Ehmen Park managed by the Gothenburg Pony Express Association. The height and location changed. The logs, the rebuilt form and the public interpretation can each be read as evidence with a distinct date. [1,3,5]

## 5. Public trip-shaped progression

1. From the public park path take in the one-story structure. Give the move date before the relay date. [1,3]
2. Read the two tablets as transcribed by the historians and distinguish the ranch monument from the tablet on the park building. During the open season add the staff interpretation. [1,3,5]
3. **Strongest story moment — The missing upper story.** Keep the one-story cabin in view and read the survey’s description of the two-story predecessor. Then compare the place page’s confident identification with the resource study’s evidence. The two readings travel well if the paths are icy. [3–5]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The operator and current NPS place page identify the relocated cabin as a Pony Express station. The 1960 survey found no contemporary Machette entry and offered alternatives. The NPS resource study preserves the uncertainty and the alternate ranch identifications. The 1931 tablet is commemoration, and its claims need a contemporary match. [1,3–5]
- The survey gives the move as 1931 and transcribes the attached tablet’s dates for later ranch use as 1862–1932. The one-year overlap is unresolved. No donation instrument or contemporary move photograph was retrieved to refine the sequence. [1,3,5]
- A photo caption in the same 1960 survey calls the building in Gothenburg Park Gilman’s Ranch Station while the text treats Gilman’s and Machette’s separately. The caption’s building attribution remains open. [3,5]

## 7. Historical photo record

The Nebraska State Historical Society’s digitized 1960 study has a black-and-white view captioned as Gilman’s Ranch Station and says it is now in Gothenburg Park. The modern city-park museum can be compared visually with recent NPS images. The historic caption and text disagree on the identification, and exact facing or a repeatable public vantage was unverified. No Photo Point. [3,4]
