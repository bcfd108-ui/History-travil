## 2. The story

The named voice is **Echo Klaproth**, a ranchwoman and writer whose family landscape is near Douglas. In a **2004 interview with folklorist Christina Barr**, published in the Nevada Arts Council education guide *The Lingo of Our Calling*, she said: **“We learned responsibility at a really young age, because we had chores.”** The guide ties her recollections to the Moore family's Nine Mile ranch and describes her writing as grounded in what she experienced there. [3]

This is firsthand retrospective testimony in an edited publication. The indexed passage was read; the complete original interview and its audio were not examined. A ranch name establishes the story's geographic association, **not a visitor destination**.

The interpretive opportunity is to change the question asked of an old ranch object. Not only “What is it?” but “Who learned to use it, who did the recurring work, and who later told that story?” Klaproth supplies a woman's own account of responsibility rather than an anonymous figure added to a cowboy scene.

## 3. Then / now

The **2014 Wyoming House Digest** records an introduction of Echo Roy Klaproth as a fourth-generation rancher, writer, retired teacher and then-poet laureate. It specifically notes her recording devoted to ranchwomen. This establishes recognition of ranchwomen's storytelling beyond one interview; it does not prove the recording is playing in the Douglas museum. [4]

The **2014 WyoPoets workshop publication** identifies her later teaching/farming context near **Shoshoni**. Keep that distinct from the Douglas-area family landscape recalled in 2004. Neither is a current residential locator, and no claim is made about where she now lives or works. [5]

The historical task is therefore not to freeze a woman at one ranch gate. It is to follow how lived work became recollection, writing and public cultural interpretation. The museum is an independent place to consider that history, not the site where her interview necessarily took place.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Resolve the door before the journey.** Use the public museum only within a confirmed open window. In December–April the printed opening-time error is a stop-planning blocker, not an invitation to guess.
2. **Look for named work.** Within open public galleries, use an actual label to identify a worker, maker or owner. Do not attach Klaproth's name to an object without evidence.
3. **Read her recollection alongside the object.** Compare what an object label explains with what a participant's memory supplies. A tool can show a task; testimony can show how responsibility was learned.
4. **End with authorship.** In a permitted quiet space, ask whose work becomes visible when the worker also writes the account. The strongest moment is recognizing a ranchwoman as a source of history, not merely a supporting character in someone else's story.

If the museum is closed, use the published text elsewhere. No replacement drive, private overlook, work-yard viewing or ranch-door approach is part of this dossier. The sequence is interpretive, not an operational tour or timed route.
