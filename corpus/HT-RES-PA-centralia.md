# HT-RES-PA-centralia — An empty street grid, a hilltop church, and a fire still burning underneath
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A town of about 1,100 people in 1962 was bought, condemned, and mostly demolished because a trash fire got into the coal underneath it and never went out — and the Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection's own warning, printed first in this entry, is that the ground is still dangerous. What remains is a street grid, cemeteries, one church on a hill, and a fire the Commonwealth still monitors.

## 1. Trip gate
This trip gate leads with the operator-level warning, because for Centralia the warning is the visit's defining fact. The Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection (DEP), on its Centralia Mine Fire Resources page, states: "The area encompassed by the Centralia Mine Fire is extremely dangerous. Walking and/or driving in the immediate area could result in serious injury or death. There are dangerous gases present, and the ground is prone to sudden and unexpected collapse. DEP strongly discourages anyone from visiting the immediate area." The DEP's FAQ is equally direct: "Visitation is not recommended due to the many hazards associated with abandoned mine sites and coal mine fires in particular," under the State's "Stay Out – Stay Alive" program, and: "The State does not provide directions to or recommend the touring of hazardous abandoned mine sites including the mine fire in Centralia Borough."

Within that warning, the lawful-standing boundary exactly as the sources carry it is narrow. Most of the borough's property was acquired by the Commonwealth through the relocation and the 1992 condemnation (see §2); the DEP FAQ records that "the State, County and surrounding township authorities maintain the traveled roadways as per their jurisdiction" — the traveled public roadways are what a visitor can lawfully be on. The cemeteries stand on ground the DEP describes as safe from the fire itself: asked whether the fire will burn beneath the cemeteries in or near the borough, the DEP FAQ answers "No. Most of the ground underlying the cemeteries in Centralia is barren (not underlain by coal) or physically isolated from the coal by excavation projects." The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary Church stands on the hill north of the borough, outside the burn zone in the community record (see §4 and the dating caveat in §6); its ground is the congregation's, and this entry directs no one onto it. The abandoned section of Route 61 known as Graffiti Highway is not a lawful or available stop: it is private land, and its owners buried it under dirt in April 2020 (see §2). Nothing in this entry directs a visitor into the fire area, onto condemned or posted ground, onto the buried highway, or onto any remaining resident's property. There is no operator, no visitor center, no hours, and no fee — none is printed because no source establishes one.

## 2. The story
A town called Bull's Head stood on this ground by 1811, later called Centreville. Coal was first mined in the Centralia area in 1842, and the Centralia Colliery opened in 1862. Centralia Borough was incorporated in February 1866, per the DEP's resources page (the DEP's own FAQ gives 1862 — the discrepancy is printed in §6). It was an anthracite town of, by the DEP's figures, approximately 1,100 to 1,200 people in 1962, occupying about 155 acres over the Buck Mountain Coal Bed.

The fire was first reported on May 27, 1962, just before Memorial Day, in a surface mine pit just southeast of the borough boundary, near the eastern border of the Independent Order of Odd Fellows Cemetery. The DEP's account is careful, and this entry keeps its care: the fire "reportedly originated as an intentional controlled burning of residential trash in an abandoned strip pit" — a pit excavated around 1935, about 75 feet wide and 50 feet deep — and "it is believed that the lack of a properly constructed non-combustible shale barrier in the strip pit enabled the trash fire to spread to adjacent carbonaceous refuse material and then to nearby coal mine workings." The DEP FAQ adds that this ignition scenario — borough personnel deliberately burning trash to reduce its volume, health vectors, and odors ahead of Memorial Day cemetery visitors — is the most widely accepted of several plausible theories; the others it names are a mine explosion in the 1930s, spontaneous combustion of trash, and vandalism. The origin is therefore printed here as reported and believed, not as settled. In August 1962, per Bucknell University's archive description of the DeKok collection, deadly gases seeping from the fire forced the closure of all remaining mining operations in the Centralia area.

What followed was twenty years of failed control and rising danger. From 1962 to 1978, state and federal governments spent $3.3 million trying to control the fire, "with limited results" (DEP chronology). The critical turning point, in the words of Bucknell University's finding aid for the papers of David DeKok — the reporter who covered the fire from 1976 and wrote its history, *Unseen Danger* (1986) — came on February 14, 1981, when twelve-year-old Todd Domboski sank into an opening in his grandmother's yard as he investigated smoke rising from the ground; the archive records that the event made national and international headlines and brought the fire's hazards to the public's attention. (The fuller detail carried by later secondary accounts is addressed in §6; the Commonwealth records checked for this entry do not name him.)

The end of the town as a populated place came by purchase and then by law. In 1983 a federal Office of Surface Mining study estimated $663 million to extinguish the fire. In 1984 Congress appropriated $42 million for voluntary acquisition and relocation. Between 1985 and 1991, 545 residences and businesses were acquired and their residents moved. In January 1992 the Centralia Task Force, authorized by the Office of Surface Mining, used condemnation procedures against what remained — the DEP chronology gives the reason as the threat from noxious gases and subsidence — and condemnation was initiated against the remaining 53 properties in 1992–93. The borough and the property owners sued; the county court ruled against the borough in November 1993, and the Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruled against the property owners in September 1995 and the borough in December 1995. The DEP FAQ records that after September 18, 2003, Centralia's ZIP code ceased to be active and residents received mail through Ashland.

The fire broke a highway as well as a town. The DEP FAQ records that the fire consumed or weakened the coal pillars beneath Route 61, causing subsidence; after repairs, in 1993 the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation closed the damaged portion — approximately 4,000 feet — and routed traffic onto a smaller local road as a bypass. The abandoned stretch, later covered in visitors' graffiti and known as Graffiti Highway, passed out of PennDOT's hands in 2018 and, as local press reported in April 2020, its private owner had it buried under dirt that month (see §6 for the sourcing of this step). The painted road in the photographs no longer exists in that form.

The last legal chapter in the records checked is a settlement, not an eviction. Bucknell's archive description records that as of 2013, state and local officials reached an agreement with the seven remaining residents of Centralia that they could live in the borough for the rest of their lives. The DEP's chronology, current to the same period, counts five remaining property owners as of January 2013 and gives the 2013 population as five owners. No later official resident count was verified from a primary source in this pass, and no present-tense resident number is printed in this entry (see §6). What the current DEP record does carry, as checked November 22, 2026, is this: the fire "has been burning in the abandoned deep mine workings of the Buck Mountain Coal Bed since May 1962," the DEP chronology's standing entry reads "Fire continues to spread," and the DEP's current involvement is "Yearly On-Site Monitoring of Fire." The DEP FAQ, whose figures run to January 2012, estimated the fire could burn for over 100 more years if left uncontrolled, and put its extent by January 2012 at approximately 400 surface acres — with the caution that not all of that area is burning at once.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a borough of approximately 1,100 to 1,200 people (DEP, for 1962) — 545 families and businesses over about 155 acres of anthracite ground. **Now:** the DEP chronology's 2013 comparison line reads "Population of five owners," most buildings demolished after Commonwealth acquisition, and the borough's ZIP code inactive since 2003. Later population figures circulate widely; none verified from a primary source in this pass prints here.

**Then:** Pennsylvania Route 61 running south through town on coal pillars, patched against subsidence at a cost of about $0.5 million in 1983 alone. **Now:** the damaged 4,000-foot section closed since 1993, traffic on the bypass, and the abandoned stretch — the Graffiti Highway of the photographs — buried under dirt by its private owner in April 2020, as the first photograph in §7 shows it in 2016, before the burial.

**Then:** a municipal dump in an old strip pit beside the Odd Fellows Cemetery, burned off before Memorial Day 1962. **Now:** that cemetery still stands on ground the DEP describes as barren of coal — and the fire first detected beside it is, per the DEP record checked in November 2026, still burning in the Buck Mountain workings and still monitored yearly.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The traveled public roadways through the borough, maintained by the State, County, and surrounding township authorities per their jurisdiction (DEP FAQ) — the street grid's surviving frame
- The cemeteries in and near the borough, on ground the DEP states is mostly barren of coal or physically isolated from it, which the DEP says the fire will not burn beneath (DEP FAQ)
- The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary Church on the hill to the north of the borough — recorded by the community site centraliapa.org as still standing because it lies outside the mine-fire burn zone; that record's content dates from the mid-2010s and no current-dated primary source for the church was verified in this pass (see §6)
- The buried section of old Route 61 — the former Graffiti Highway — under the dirt placed on it in April 2020; the painted surface is gone from view
- The fire itself, underground: burning since May 1962 in the Buck Mountain Coal Bed workings, under yearly on-site monitoring by the DEP (DEP resources page and chronology, checked November 22, 2026)
- A small number of remaining homes under the 2013 life-tenancy settlement — private residences, not sights; the count prints only as the dated figures in §2 and §6 give it

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — and for this entry the conditions are the DEP's: the State does not provide directions to or recommend touring this site, and the progression below stays on traveled public roadways and cemetery ground as §1 bounds them:

1. **From the traveled public roadway, at the borough's edge:** Read the grid first — streets laid out for 545 families and businesses, over ground the DEP warns is prone to sudden collapse. Do not leave the traveled roadway for the fire area.
2. **At the Odd Fellows Cemetery ground:** The fire's first reported location was the pit near this cemetery's eastern border. The cemetery ground itself is the ground the DEP describes as barren of coal. Stand there, and nowhere the ground is posted or condemned.
3. **From the public roadway below the northern hill:** The Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary Church, viewed from public ground — the congregation's ground is not a stop.
4. **On the Route 61 bypass:** The living road tells the dead one's story — traffic runs on the smaller local road PennDOT substituted in 1993. The buried Graffiti Highway section is private, covered, and not a stop; the 2016 photograph in §7 is the visit to it.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The incorporation date conflicts inside the Commonwealth's own record: the DEP resources page says Centralia Borough was incorporated in February 1866; the DEP FAQ says it was "officially incorporated in 1862" — the year the Centralia Colliery opened. Bucknell's archive description also gives 1866. Both DEP forms print here; 1866 is used in §2 with its source named.

The 2013 settlement headcount does not reconcile across sources: Bucknell's archive description says seven remaining residents; the DEP chronology counts five remaining property owners (January 2012–2013). Contemporary Associated Press reporting counted eight residents. These are different units — residents, property owners, people covered by a settlement — and this entry prints each with its source rather than choosing one. No official resident count later than the DEP's 2013 figure was verified from a primary source in this pass; the frequently repeated statement that five people remained as of the 2020 census is therefore not printed as fact here, only flagged as unverified against a primary source in this pass.

The Domboski incident is carried in this entry by Bucknell University's finding aid for the David DeKok Collection, which names the date, the boy, his age, his grandmother's yard, and the turning-point judgment. The fuller details in wide circulation — that he clung to a tree root, that his cousin Eric Wolfgang pulled him out, that the steam from the hole carried a lethal level of carbon monoxide, and figures for the hole's depth (commonly 150 feet) — come from contemporary press and later secondary accounts, not from the Commonwealth or university-archive records checked here, and they print in this section as reported detail, not as §2 fact.

The Graffiti Highway's burial is likewise a press-carried step: the DEP record establishes the 1993 closure and the fire damage under Route 61, and the Wikimedia Commons file page for the §7 photograph records that "in 2020, the property owners buried the entire highway with 10,000 tons of dirt," but the April 2020 burial itself — its start on April 6, 2020, the owner named as Pagnotti Enterprises, and the trucking of dirt by its crews — rests on local press reporting from that month, not on a Commonwealth record found in this pass. The ZIP code's end also prints two ways in secondary accounts (revoked 2002 vs. the DEP FAQ's "after September 18, 2003, the Centralia Zip Code ceased to be active"); the DEP form is used in §2.

Finally, the church: the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary Church's survival is carried here by the community record at centraliapa.org, whose content dates from the mid-2010s. No Commonwealth or diocesan source with a current date was verified for it in this pass, so every church statement in this entry is dated to that record rather than asserted as of 2026. The same discipline applies to the fire: "still burning" prints because the DEP's own current resources page, checked November 22, 2026, carries it in the present tense — not because older articles said so.
