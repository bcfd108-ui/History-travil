## 2. The story

Begin with a kitchen, not a cattle king. **Ham Sam**, a Chinese cook, fed the bunkhouse crew; **Wilhemenia** cooked in the ranch house. [6]

Named primary witness **J. H. Gehrmann** wrote to the Montana Historical Society on **November 19, 1974**: **“Sam always had a pie for us.”** Recalling childhood visits beginning in 1904, he remembered boys seeking food at the ranch-hands' kitchen. This is an owner's relative remembering workers, not Ham Sam speaking. The historical study reproduces the letter excerpt; original manuscript and ledger images were not inspected. [6]

The small domestic detail changes the question: not merely who owned the cattle, but who made daily life possible? Cooking, fuel, washing, repairs, and feeding people were work, even when the landscape was quiet. That is the interpretive thesis of this dossier, not a claim that one remembered pie represents every worker's experience.

The observer's explanation of Ham Sam's assigned name does not establish his preferred identity. Do not invent his dialogue or perform an accent. [6] The strongest story includes the silence left when records preserve employer-family memories more readily than workers' own accounts.

Charles Morrow Wilson saw the ranch in 1937. He described “a crew of cowboys who have learned to double as farm hands.” Warren’s tractor, mechanical thresher and irrigation improvements changed the skills the hands needed. Read Wilson alongside the cooks’ letters. His article, quoted by NPS, names the changing occupation. The correspondence locates people within the household that fed it. The original magazine page remains unexamined. [11]

## 3. Then / now

Then: ranch hands ate, slept, repaired gear, and socialized in Bunkhouse Row. The NPS brochure dates that assemblage to 1907–1930s and identifies an added room at the ice-house/tack-room building as Ham Sam's summer home. It also explains the ice storage's conversion after refrigeration arrived in 1935. These are layered buildings, not a single frozen year. [7]

Now: the visitor can encounter a working ranch preserved and interpreted by NPS, including worker accommodation and barns. The operator explicitly invites self-guided bunkhouse visits, including in winter. [8] That does not prove today's displayed furniture belonged to the named cook, that his room is open, or that a particular kitchen arrangement dates to his employment.

The comparison is between documented functions and surviving public interpretation, not a verified matched photographic pair. An optional house tour can provide a household contrast without allowing the owner's rooms to consume the whole visit.

## 4. What's still there

- **Public visitor approach:** NPS describes an asphalt trail of about a quarter mile between the visitor center and historic area, with gradual inclines and closer accessible parking available. Surface description is not a winter snow-clearance or individual-accessibility guarantee. [9]
- **Self-guided interpretation:** bunkhouse, Draft Horse Barn, and Thoroughbred Barn are specifically promoted by the operator. Follow doors, barriers, and staff directions on the day; operating livestock work takes precedence. [8]
- **Guided interior only:** the Ranch House. Do not enter independently if a door happens to be open. [3]
- **Documented feature, entry not established:** Ham Sam's summer room. Mention the association; do not supply a door-level route or presume interior access. [7]
- **Excluded:** railway right-of-way, wastewater facilities and other Special Use Zone areas; museum storage without authorized arrangements; corrals, work spaces, or animal areas not designated for visitors. [3]

This dossier stays in the home-ranch visitor circuit. The operator describes separate west-side trail hours and access arrangements, but those are not an extension automatically activated by this stop. [10]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Suggested compact visit, not a surveyed route or timed reservation:**

1. At the visitor center, check winter footing, which worker-life rooms are open, and whether a house tour is practical. Begin with Ham Sam and Wilhemenia as the people to keep in mind.
2. Follow the designated visitor approach into the historic area. Notice the distinction between visitor circulation and work areas; do not approach an animal for a photograph. [8][9]
3. Visit an open barn only where invited. Treat animal care and equipment as labor context rather than a demonstration promised for today.
4. If available, take the house tour as a contrast in domestic space. If unavailable, omit it without improvising access.
5. **End at the publicly accessible bunkhouse interpretation**, indoors only if open. Read Gehrmann's brief recollection and ask who is absent from the written record. The strongest moment is not the biggest room: it is imagining the meal that made another day's work possible, while admitting we have not recovered the cook's own words.

If the bunkhouse is unavailable, finish the reflection at an open public interpretive location identified by staff. No substitute incursion into the summer room or working kitchen.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- Primary evidence is **a published transcription/excerpt of a retrospective letter**, plus the historian's description of account records. No worker-authored diary, interview, or Chinese-language name has been verified for Ham Sam.
- The historian explicitly cautions against overreading sparse labor accounts. Do not convert an absent monthly entry into unemployment, a precise wage, or a definitive headcount. [6]
- Closure prose/compendium and generated holiday hours disagree; explicit closure governs this draft. [2][3]
