# Quindaro — Caroline, a river town and the record of refuge

## 2. The story

**Land and leadership.** The Wyandot Nation of Kansas Tribal Council’s own resolution recounts the Nation’s forced removal from Ohio, members’ abolitionist role, and refuge offered to people seeking freedom from slavery. It identifies Nancy Quindaro Brown as the person for whom the town was named and prints the meaning “Strength in Unity.” The state account identifies Nancy Quindaro Brown Guthrie and describes the town company’s free-state port project. The period’s venture and the Nation’s account belong in the same reading. One should not substitute the company's promotional story for the history of the people who made the town possible. [2,3,4]

**What a resident could do.** Clarina Howard Nichols worked on the Quindaro Chindowan. In an 1882 letter printed in the Wyandotte Gazette, she recalled young settlers who put “hand and brain into the struggle for ‘Free Kansas.’” The public library’s version is a transcription prepared for readability. It describes a gathering place for discussion and an aid point in a network with branches inland. It is a participant’s account written long after the events. A University of Chicago finding aid separately inventories John Morgan Walden’s May 1857 notice and surviving newspaper materials. The finding aid establishes records to consult; it does not supply the complete originals. [5,6,9]

**Caroline’s name.** The state historical society reproduces Nichols’s account of an evening in late October 1861. Nichols says neighbor Fielding Johnson warned her that Caroline needed to be hidden while men seeking to return her to slavery were nearby. Nichols recounts arranging refuge in a cistern. The story reaches us from the person who gave shelter. Caroline’s own account, later whereabouts and exact location that evening remain unverified here. The landscape gives this account a place in history without turning anyone’s danger into an activity. [4,6]
