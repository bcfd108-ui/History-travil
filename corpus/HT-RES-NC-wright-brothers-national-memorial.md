# Wright Brothers National Memorial — twelve seconds, four flights
*On Old Ground research draft · North Carolina OP01 · November 21, 2026*

## 2. Story
“Success four flights thursday morning” was the beginning of the message the Wrights sent home on December 17, 1903. They had already spent seasons trying to control gliders around Kill Devil Hills. Their newer problem was how to get a pilot, an engine and a machine into the air together and bring them down in one piece. Charles E. Taylor, the mechanic at their Dayton bicycle shop, built the engine with the brothers after they could not find a suitable commercial one. At the camp a broken propeller shaft interrupted the work. A trial on December 14 left Wilbur with a brief rise and a hard landing. Three mornings later, Orville had the controls for the first run. [5–7]

Look closely at the photograph. The rail continues across the sand, and the wings seem improbably long beside Wilbur, still on foot. Orville had prepared the camera and asked John T. Daniels, a member of the lifesaving crew who had come to help, to make the exposure. Daniels caught the machine just after it cleared the rail. The brothers traded turns three more times. In the fourth flight Wilbur traveled 852 feet according to NPS, far beyond the first run’s approximately 120 feet. Orville’s telegram compresses the morning into a sentence with a request to tell the press and word that the brothers would be home for Christmas. The quoted phrase above follows the received telegram’s capitalization in the Library of Congress facsimile. [5, 6, 8, 9, 19]

The Flyer demanded physical work from its pilot. NPS describes a hand control for the forward elevator and a hip cradle for wing warping and coordinated tail movement. The Flyer pitched repeatedly during the first flight. Under the museum’s reproduction, the pulleys, wire and open frame make the engineering problem easier to see than a silhouette in a photograph can. The original rail carried the machine into a headwind, which helps explain the short distance covered over the ground. [3, 6]

## 3. Then and now
The first flight took place on the historic field at Kill Devil Hills. The archive caption uses Kitty Hawk, where the brothers sent the telegram. The flight line and boulder mark the takeoff area and landing distances in today’s park. The camp buildings are reconstructions. The aircraft inside the Visitor Center is a reproduction of the 1903 Flyer. The museum and memorial are later layers of interpretation at the historic ground. The register documentation inventories the commemorative fabric and the First Flight Marker. [3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 17, 18]

## 4. Surviving features and access
- **Public interior, during operation.** The NPS Visitor Center’s Flight Room has a reproduction Flyer and interpretation. The anchor is the interior and signed pedestrian approach from its public parking. [1–4]
- **Historic ground, described from indoors.** The historic takeoff and landing area lies on the memorial grounds. Boulder, flight markers and reconstructed camp appear in the operator’s site interpretation. This entry supplies no onward dune or shoreline route. [3, 4, 10]
- **Commemorative fabric.** The later granite memorial on Kill Devil Hill is a separate feature from the lower first-flight field. The historic photograph shows the 1903 field before the memorial complex existed. [8, 10]

## 5. Public progression
Drive to the NPS Visitor Center from US 158 and use the designated lot and signed entrance. Start in the museum, learn how the control system worked, and compare the 1903 frame with the reproduction’s shape. In poor weather, the museum can stand alone during operating hours. If the operator closes the center, the archive photograph and telegram carry the story remotely. [1–4, 8, 9]

**Strongest story moment — Twelve Seconds, Four Flights.** In the Flight Room compare the size of the wings to the machine’s exposed pilot position. Then read the archive frame as a scene of several people working: Orville at the controls, Wilbur alongside and Daniels behind the lens. Picture Wilbur’s last flight going several times as far as the first before the brothers worded their telegram home. [3, 5–9]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- **Flight timings.** The inspected Western Union receiving copy reads “longest 57 seconds.” Orville’s pocket diary instead gives 852 feet in 59 seconds for Wilbur’s fourth flight, the figures NPS reports. Orville’s January 22, 1937 letter to Sam H. Johnson also gives 59 in its retrospective transcription of the telegram. The receiving copy establishes what reached Dayton. It does not establish where the two-second difference arose. Orville called the first flight about 12 seconds in the diary because the watch was not stopped promptly. [5, 6, 19–23]
- **Wind.** The telegram says “twenty one mile wind.” On diary p. 53, Orville distinguishes a little over 20 miles an hour, corrected, on the brothers’ instrument at the first trial from 27 on the government instrument at Kitty Hawk. NPS reports 27 mph and the September 1908 caption gives 24. The diary identifies two different instruments and places. The basis for the caption’s 24 remains open. [6, 14, 19, 20]
- **Place names and attendance.** LOC’s item says Kitty Hawk whereas the operator locates the flight and modern anchor at Kill Devil Hills. The NPS 2025 figure of 341,576 counts recreation visits to the whole unit, with no Visitor Center door figure verified here. [2, 4, 8, 11]
- The original PDF endpoint [16] failed in the first pass. The separately cataloged Library of Congress facsimile of the received Western Union form and the original diary folios were subsequently inspected. The send-side copy and the origin of the number discrepancy remain unverified. The modern museum entrance coordinate pair still lacks independent agreement. [4, 16, 19–23]

## 7. Historical photo record
**Adopted archive record — First flight.** Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division, Wright Brothers Negatives, item 00652085, LC-DIG-ppprs-00626, December 17, 1903. The inspected image shows a biplane in flight over open sand, the rail extending from the foreground left and a person to the right identified as Wilbur by the archive. A white area at lower left is the missing corner of the original negative. LOC attributes the preset camera to Orville Wright and the shutter release to John T. Daniels. LOC's item rights advisory is “No known restrictions on publication.” The inspected September 1908 *Century Magazine* scan, printed p. 644, reproduces the same aircraft, rail and runner. Its U.S. publication term has expired. The modern comparison is to the reproduced craft and NPS interpretation, with no field-verified camera point. [6, 8, 12–14]

**Printed credit:** Orville Wright, camera setup, and John T. Daniels, exposure, *[First flight, 120 feet in 12 seconds, 10:35 a.m.; Kitty Hawk, North Carolina]*, December 17, 1903. Library of Congress Prints and Photographs Division, Wright Brothers Negatives, item 00652085, LC-DIG-ppprs-00626.
