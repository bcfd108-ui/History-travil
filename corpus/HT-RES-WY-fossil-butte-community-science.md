## 2. The story
The story here is not “find your own fossil.” It is the less visible work that turns an object into evidence others can examine.

The monument's **Museum Management Plan**, developed through a staff team visit in August 2001, names **Arvid Aase**, museum specialist, **Kent Bush**, lead curator, and **Lynn Marie Mitchell**, archivist, among its contributors. Its executive summary pairs museum collections with **“associated data.”** That short phrase is the interpretive hinge: a specimen's scientific usefulness depends on records and care, not just its visual appeal. This is a primary institutional planning voice, not a personal diary or proof that every recommendation was implemented. [5]

The plan's historical account places **David Haddenham** in fossil collecting from 1918, describes the work extending across his family for more than fifty years, and distinguishes summer field work from winter preparation. These are the planning team's retrospective statements about a named local worker, not Haddenham's own testimony. The account also identifies **Carl and Shirley Ulrich** with a late-1940s enterprise and a 1958 request to the Interior Department for resource protection. No commercial access or collecting activity is inferred from that history. [5]

The public connection was explicit in NPS's **July 6, 2017** announcement of a visitor-center exhibit about **Lee Craig and David C. Haddenham**. It linked local fossil work to scientific publication and interpretation. The release establishes an exhibit's announced opening, not its exact present condition or a continuing public field program. [6]

**Interpretive conclusion:** a fossil can travel from workbench to museum and publication, but the important journey is also from privately known object to evidence that can be checked. This distinguishes the stop from Ekalaka's community-built museum story: here the focus is the public responsibilities of scientific collecting, documentation and interpretation inside a national monument.

The historical account is not an instruction to repeat historical extraction. The visitor's task is to understand why preservation and records matter without removing anything.

## 3. Then / now
- **1918 onward:** Haddenham's family work is described retrospectively in the museum plan. Exact working years should not be replaced with the broader 1920s–1950s shorthand used in some interpretation. [5]
- **2001 planning process:** named museum professionals assessed collection stewardship. A planning document is evidence of decisions and concerns at that time, not current facility conditions. [5]
- **2017:** an archived NPS release announced interpretation of early workers and modern science. Its archive warning matters; it is not an invitation to a current event. [6]
- **Present:** prepared specimens are publicly interpreted in the visitor center; no collecting is allowed in the monument. [7]

## 4. What's still there
**Public interpretation:** the current NPS exhibits page describes original fossils and casts, including a crocodilian cast identified as **FMNH PR1674**, whose original is at Chicago's Field Museum. The identifier belongs to that specimen, not to a visitor location. A cast can support interpretation without being misrepresented as the original. [8]

**Museum documentation:** the downloaded management plan preserves the names and concerns of the staff team. It does not open storage, labs or collections to unscheduled visitors.

**Protected resources:** NPS states that monument fossils are protected and describes documentation, cataloging and scientific use. Its FAQ cites **36 CFR 2.1** for collecting restrictions. This dossier supplies no excavation, preparation, collecting or locality-finding advice. [7]

**Excluded:** every fossil locality coordinate, private quarry, historical cabin approach, field research area, off-path investigation, storage room and operational work area. No specimen handling is assumed. No sensitive cultural ground or private land is treated as part of the stop.

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Before departure:** confirm visitor-center opening and the entrance-road condition. Do not add the scenic drive as a winter fallback.
2. **Inside the admitted visitor area:** begin with an available prepared specimen or cast label. Establish which it is, rather than assuming authenticity from appearance.
3. **Read the human layer:** use the published account of Haddenham and the named museum team. Do not promise that a particular historic photograph, diorama or original notebook is currently displayed.
4. **End on evidence:** consider what a scientist would lose if an object survived but its supporting records did not.

The strongest moment is the difference between seeing a fossil and being able to explain what is known about it. This progression needs no field excursion or operational demonstration. A closed display can be replaced by the cited public text, not by unauthorized access.
