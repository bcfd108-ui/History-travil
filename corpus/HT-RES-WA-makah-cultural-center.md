## 2. The story
The museum's own Ozette account begins with a **winter 1969–1970 storm** that exposed wooden objects. A hiker informed the Tribe, which contacted Washington State University; excavation began in April 1970. The account preserves Makah oral history of an earlier slide and explains that the Tribe kept the recovered collection on the reservation and sought an institution to care for and interpret it. [6] The story is not outsiders discovering a people who had forgotten themselves. Archaeological exposure, remembered history and modern institutional decisions are different kinds of evidence.

A named Makah participant's published perspective makes this distinction concrete. **Janine Bowechop**, with **Patricia Pierce Erikson**, wrote *Forging Indigenous Methodologies on Cape Flattery: The Makah Museum as a Center of Collaborative Research* in 2005. The essay identifies Bowechop's work replicating Ozette objects and excavating at Hoko River, and describes collaboration as **“joint intellectual work or coalition building.”** It explains how Makah youth, elders and researchers participated in knowledge-making rather than merely supplying objects to outside specialists. [7]

Their example of collections management is especially revealing: household associations and Makah-language relationships shaped storage and classification, challenging an arrangement based only on standard archaeological object categories. The museum could preserve relationships and meanings as well as materials. [7] This is a published participant account of institutional practice at that time, not permission to inspect storage rooms or interview community members now.

MCRC opened in **1979**. [1] Its present self-description includes a language program, education, archives and historic preservation alongside the museum. [4] The historical endpoint is therefore not the excavation. It is the continuing choice of what to preserve, how to name it, who controls it and what to share. No recorded voice was played or independently transcribed in this pass; the named voice above is written publication.

## 3. Then / now
Then, Ozette was a Makah village with households, tools and relationships that cannot be reduced to a collection of picturesque objects. The storm exposure and subsequent excavation were twentieth-century events; the slide that preserved objects was earlier. The museum describes the recovered material broadly as 300–500 years old. [1,6]

Now, the public museum in Neah Bay combines selected ancestral objects with photographs, dioramas and replicas. A replica longhouse is interpretation, not a surviving Ozette house relocated intact. A modern visitor sees what the museum chooses to present, not unrestricted access to all cultural knowledge. [8]

The meaningful then/now comparison is continuity of Makah care and interpretation, not a staged comparison at an archaeological trench. No route to the source village is needed or supplied.

## 4. What's still there
The museum's exhibits page lists **18 showcases, three dioramas, full-sized canoe and longhouse replicas**, changing temporary exhibits and an outdoor ethnobotanical garden. These are operator-described facilities, not a verified inventory of today's displays. [8]

The Library and Archives Department preserves photographs, documents and recordings. It lists separate weekday hours and appointments; museum admission must not be treated as a research appointment or access to restricted holdings. [9]

The website's “Accessibility” page discusses **website accessibility**, not physical grades, doorway dimensions or visitor assistance. Do not promote it into a verified building-access survey. [10] Public museum access, if confirmed, remains distinct from offices, research storage, cultural properties and reservation land generally.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Resolve the gate before departure.** Confirm current museum admission under Makah authority, including weather closure. A long peninsula drive should not rely on contradictory web banners.
2. **Begin with museum orientation.** At the offered visitor entrance, follow current staff directions and signs. Establish photography and recording rules before taking out a camera.
3. **Follow the museum's interpretive sequence.** Consider one ordinary household object or replica through its label: material, maker if named, use and family relationship. Do not infer ownership or sacred meaning where the label does not supply it.
4. **End with the strongest story moment: who holds the authority to tell the story?** The public galleries themselves embody the decision to keep the collection and interpretation under Makah care. The point is not that the visitor has now learned everything; it is that the community chooses what this visit may teach.

Optional question for an available museum interpreter: **How does the museum distinguish an ancestral object, a teaching replica and knowledge that is not offered for public interpretation?** Accept boundaries without pressing for examples of restricted material. This is a proposed museum conversation, not outreach already undertaken.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Public hours and closure notices conflict within the museum's own website and with the NPS listing. No dated reopening notice was verified here. The visitor gate remains unresolved, not declared closed or cleared. [3–5]
- The museum gives a radiocarbon expression of **500 ± 50 years BP** for the slide, while public summaries use broader age ranges. BP is a dating convention, not an instruction to subtract from 2026. No single calendar-year catastrophe is assigned. [6]
- Bowechop and Erikson describe remains from at least four houses; the museum's Ozette account describes six. Differences in scope or classification may matter, but no reconciliation was established. [6,7]
- The word “discovered” in visitor summaries concerns archaeological exposure, not the origin of Makah knowledge of Ozette. [1,6]
- No Makah-issued museum-specific photography rule was located in the pages read. Third-party reports say no interior photography, but they cannot authorize exceptions. Default here is **no interior photography or recording without current museum permission**.
