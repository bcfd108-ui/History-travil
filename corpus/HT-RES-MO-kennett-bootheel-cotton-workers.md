# Kennett — What was left after the cotton was picked

Missouri OP05 · Draft for independent Dash review · Quiet witness for displacement and racial violence

## 2. Story

A July 1942 photograph by Arthur Rothstein catches a slice of Kennett Main Street with the words COTTON EXCHANGE BANK above a clock and the county courthouse in the same frame. Cars and people occupy the street. Nothing in the picture can tell a traveler who owned land or paid for seed. It supplies a view of the town in which crop finance, work and public business met. The sign is visible historical evidence of the name on the bank. It does not identify the lender in any particular contract. [1, 3]

In a 1997 oral history, longtime agricultural extension worker William W. Harrison recalled a field season of obligations in Dunklin and Pemiscot counties. He said, “The ginners furnished money for them to live on until the cotton was picked.” He recalled that the money came out of workers’ cotton share, leaving some households with little after settlement. In the same interview he described driving through Kennett and toward Hayti in fall 1934. He remembered observing no improvements in housing on that stretch. The remark reflects a visitor's route and his memory decades later. It is not a survey of every home. Source is the transcript, pp. 24–25, interview by Will Sarvis in Cape Girardeau, June 2, 1997, Oral History Program of the State Historical Society of Missouri and Western Historical Manuscript Collection. [4]

Roy Cooper Jr. and Alex Cooper gave a complementary household account in 1998. Roy recalled the family coming up from Arkansas in 1922 or 1923. His grandfather brought mules, implements and relatives in a subleasing arrangement. The family story includes a threat and a move. Later in the interview, Roy described how families understood crop shares and payments when cotton acreage was taken out of production. This is a named Pemiscot County family’s account, with earlier generations’ experiences conveyed through family memory. The family’s fields and home were outside the Kennett square and are not visitor locations. Source is the transcript, pp. 1–2 and 6, interview by Will Sarvis in Hayti, Aug. 4, 1998, Oral History Program of the State Historical Society of Missouri and Western Historical Manuscript Collection. [5]

Historian Jarod Roll describes a different household on the Kennett Farm Security Administration project in 1941. It traveled north for strawberry then peach work between cotton jobs and returned for picking. Roll cites Helen Beck’s testimony in the 1941 migration hearings. This dossier uses the historian’s account of that household, with the hearing held for independent reading and the family’s name and exact residence open. Walking the square lets a traveler see a surviving town anchor before reading a household’s year as a sequence of work and travel across several states. [6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historical locations.** The courthouse stood on the square while households worked in fields and moved between farms and towns across the Bootheel. Dunklin County dates construction of the present courthouse to 1937–1940. FSA photography shows a courthouse and cotton-bank sign in 1942. No individual contract, field, gin, residence or protest footprint is assigned from these records. [1, 3–7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The open public sidewalks of Kennett Court Square facing Dunklin County Courthouse, 100 Kennett Court Square. Arrive by car, use lawful street parking and take a short public walk on open sidewalks and marked crossings. Stay outside doorways, restricted courthouse grounds, working businesses, roadsides and field access. The neighboring Justice Center is a separate county address. [1–2]

## 5. A short visit

Start on the open public sidewalk facing the courthouse. Use Rothstein's Main Street photograph to put the courthouse and the bank sign into the same working town. Finish with **After the picking came the settlement**. Read Harrison’s sentence about credit, then the narrative of the Kennett project family that returned for another round of picking. The labor history is a quiet reading and asks for no activity at any place of eviction. [1–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

Harrison describes ginner-financed living costs and the Coopers discuss share arrangements plus federal crop-reduction payments. These are distinct obligations and testimony scopes. Neither establishes a universal percentage or a rate for an individual Kennett worker. The photograph’s bank sign establishes no connection to a named account. The original migration-hearing pages Beck pp. 9244–45, local crop rent books and item-level workers’ transcripts from the Bootheel Project remain open. The Library of Congress courthouse and Main Street rich web viewers both returned HTTP 403 in this reader. Main Street raw JSON and image bytes were recovered. The courthouse frame was read through the credited archive derivative. [3–6, 8–10]
