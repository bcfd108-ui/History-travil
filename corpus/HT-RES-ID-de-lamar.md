## 2. The story

The registered Delamar Historic District concerns a settlement in the Jordan Creek valley west of Silver City, not every modern property carrying the DeLamar name. The nomination describes buildings stretched along the road and hillsides; growth from 1889 included a boardinghouse, mercantile business, school and services associated with milling. [3] That organization matters: industrial work required housing, supplies and families as well as machinery.

A contemporary report makes the connection tangible. The **Silver City Nugget**, March 27, 1903, reported that water power at the DeLamar mill relieved its steam boilers and saved coal. It also named miners **Jack Thomas and Charley Goudge** leaving DeLamar for Tonopah. The correspondent wished them well: “They are good men and good miners”. This is a newspaper's voice about workers, not a worker diary or a quotation by either man. [4]

The town cannot be understood as a single overnight boom-and-bust. The Idaho geological survey's 1968 regional synthesis describes efforts to treat lower-grade ore, costly exploration and eventual closure in 1913; it connects the decline to both ore economics and a federal timber dispute. It cites mine-inspector reports, but this dossier has not independently read all those underlying pages. [5]

**Primary-source trail:** Waldemar Lindgren's 1900 USGS study records detailed 1897 field examination around Silver City and De Lamar, assisted by F. C. Schrader and mine personnel including D. B. Huntley. That is contemporary professional observation, not oral testimony from the general workforce. [6] Idaho State Archives **MS 0530**, covering 1891–1906, preserves DeLamar company correspondence, plats, legal papers and inventories. The finding aid is verified; its documents were not read here. [7]

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a mining settlement and associated industrial landscape with changing extraction and milling practices. The nomination's 1970s condition account reports losses and remaining buildings at that time. It is not a current building inventory. [3]

**Now:** the historical town district, legacy mine workings, modern exploration/reclamation responsibilities and proposed new mining footprint must remain separate map layers. BLM's current project is a regulated proposal, not a heritage reconstruction. [1] The geological survey's modern mapping describes renewed exploration after Integra acquired property in 2017; its mine/mill closure chronology concerns the later industrial operation, not the 19th-century town's decline. [8]

Interpretive recommendation: compare the changing economics of metal extraction with the community that supported it, rather than treating a ghost-town label as proof the surrounding country is abandoned or safe.
