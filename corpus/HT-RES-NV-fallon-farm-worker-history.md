## 2. The story
**Earl James Stuart**, interviewed by **Vera Neill on August 1, 1993**, recalled Fallon-area farm work with a distinction more revealing than a machinery list: **“Chores didn't count. They wasn't work.”** He remembered a nominal nine-hour hayfield day, with household farm duties before and afterward. His recollected wage was $1.50 per day in the Depression-era passage. That is one man's retrospective account, not a verified prevailing wage or legal measure of a working day. The transcript body uses Stuart; the catalog title uses Stewart. Both spellings must remain visible. [3]

**Vina M. Harrigan Woods**, speaking with **Eleanor Ahern**, remembered childhood participation in the household's honey business, including scraping wax from sections. She recalled local sales and pooled shipments beyond Fallon. The named household member is describing labor she performed, not just repeating a business's publicity. Her account also describes loss of the bees, but no diagnostic or husbandry advice is drawn from it. The visible transcript heading leaves its interview date blank. [4]

Both public transcripts were read in their relevant passages; no original audio was listened to. Their work connects a farm's outward product with effort that a visitor might otherwise overlook. The interpretive point is not that hardship was wholesome or that children should reenact it. It is that a count of output can conceal who did the necessary tasks and when their day actually ended.

The two narrators do not constitute a complete social history of Fallon labor. No claim is made that their household arrangements represented hired migrant workers, all women, or Tribal families. Adding those experiences requires their own named, appropriately published evidence, not borrowed authority from these interviews.
