## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** The revealing historical change here is not a newly accessible old photograph. It is the power to decide whether a photograph should be accessible at all.

**Named first-person, Tribe-published voice:** Managing Editor **Romalita Laban**, writing in the Hopi Tribe's online *Hopi Tutuveni* page on July 28, 2022, explains why she continues newspaper work during difficult circumstances. She calls it **“my way of giving back to those who came before me.”** This is her public first-person statement, not a quotation invented for a museum label. Her editorial introduces republication of **Stewart B. Koyiyumptewa's** 2018 guest editorial about an attempted church apology. It is not a report that Hopi religious leaders accepted that apology, nor an invitation to observe religious life. [2]

**A dated act of custodianship:** Louella Nahsonhoya's report in *Hopi Tutuveni*, April 7, 2015, page 4, describes the March 23 presentation of digitized H. R. Voth photographs to Cultural Preservation Office Director **Leigh Kuwanwisiwma**. It names archivist **Stewart Koiyayumptewa** in coordinating the delivery. These are the spellings in that report. The article makes the photographs' contentious history and restricted knowledge explicit; the transfer is not a public-release notice. [3]

**Institutional context:** The Hopi Cultural Preservation Office describes a role involving Tribal government as well as villages, clans and religious societies. Its public explanation of intellectual property describes damage when outsiders reproduce knowledge or images without permission. An official website is therefore evidence of the boundaries of sharing, not a blanket waiver of them. [4]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Read these accounts as evidence of continuing authorship and stewardship. The newspaper editor, archivist and cultural-preservation office are not merely intermediaries who deliver material to tourists. They help determine what the record means and who it serves. The visitor's best contribution may be to refrain from turning every accessible reference into a photograph, map pin or task.

### Who defines the public record?

In December 2018 the Hopi Tribe’s Office of the Chairman issued its own account of testimony in a water adjudication. It identifies Chairman Timothy Nuvangyaoma and former Chairman Herman Honanie as witnesses. The release reports that Nuvangyaoma spoke about his childhood and explained village sovereignty and the responsibilities of tribal government to the court. It provides the Tribe’s public account of what he presented. Read it as a summary by his office. [6]

The archived Senate hearing of September 25, 2024 adds the words of the speaker himself. Nuvangyaoma’s prepared statement says, “Our oral histories go even further back.” He was contrasting what archaeological records could date with the history carried in Hopi accounts. He also explained to the committee why the Tribe considered a reliable water supply essential to exercising its sovereignty. Set the hearing beside the newspaper’s report of photographic stewardship. Both records show Hopi institutions deciding how public audiences encounter evidence and what institutional purpose it serves. [3,6–8]
