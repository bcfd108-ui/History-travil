## 2. The story

**Mitch Smallsalmon**, identified as a Pend d’Oreille elder, recalled in 1978: **“Their home life was good, they were growing up in a good way”**. Travelers’ Rest Connection republishes the passage from *The Salish People and the Lewis and Clark Expedition* (University of Nebraska Press, 2005). The published excerpt was read; the original interview, recording and full book were not examined. [4]

That recollection places children and home life at the center of the landscape. The cooperating organization connects seasonal family life with continuing homeland ties despite the 1891 removal. Removal is not the end of the story or evidence that the land lacked people before the expedition. [4]

A concrete public interpretive anchor exists: **Seasons of the Salish**, described by the Connection as explaining the seasonal round of Séliš and Ql̓ispé people, with traditionally made objects including a cedar-bark basket and bitterroot digging stick. The organization records a 2023 update. This is published exhibit evidence, not a same-day inventory check. [5]

**Original interpretive approach:** ask how one place can be a brief campsite in an expedition journal and part of a continuing homeland in community memory. Those accounts have different timescales and purposes; neither should be flattened into a single “discovery” narrative.

## 3. Then / now

The park operator reports archaeological evidence identified in 2002 supporting the expedition's presence. Its claim concerns the Corps of Discovery campsite, not the beginning of human history here and not independent verification of every story told about the broader Bitterroot. [1]

The public exhibits distinguish archaeological finds from replica expedition equipment and also include modern and contemporary Indigenous work. A replica is an interpretive tool, not an original artifact. Contemporary work must not be treated as evidence of a vanished people. [5]

The Connection's winter programming page describes sharing history through storytellers. A tradition of winter programming does not establish an available performance on a particular date. No storyteller attendance or future event is promised. [2]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Suggested sequence, not a surveyed route or guaranteed program:

1. **Start indoors during winter hours.** Check closures and available public exhibits before assuming an outdoor component.
2. **Begin with the homeland.** Read Seasons of the Salish before making the expedition the starting point. Look for the distinction between contemporary interpretation and original evidence.
3. **Compare kinds of evidence.** Ask what the archaeological interpretation can establish, and what a journal or community recollection contributes differently. Do not turn that question into a search in the ground.
4. **Optional outdoor pause:** only on an open signed public path, without leaving the visitor route or searching for artifacts. If icy, omit it.
5. **End with home life.** Return to Smallsalmon's words. The strongest story moment is recognizing that the place was part of families' lives, not merely a stop along somebody else's journey.

Where interpretation addresses removal or loss, use quiet reflection, not scoring or reenactment.
