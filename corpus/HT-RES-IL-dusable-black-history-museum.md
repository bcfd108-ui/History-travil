# DuSable Black History Museum and Education Center — the museum that began in a Bronzeville living room
HT-RES-IL-dusable-black-history-museum · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The museum was chartered on February 16, 1961, as the Ebony Museum of Negro History and Art. Its founders — artist and teacher Margaret Taylor-Burroughs, her husband Charles Burroughs, and fellow Chicago citizens including Gerard Lew, Eugene Feldman, Bernard Goss, and Marian M. Hadley — built it to correct an omission: Black history and culture were, in the museum's own account, overlooked by most museums and academic establishments of the time. The first museum occupied the ground floor of the Burroughses' own home at 3806 S. Michigan Avenue in Bronzeville. Burroughs recalled clearing the furniture out of the first-floor parlor for the museum, collecting objects as neighbors heard what they were doing and brought things in, and teaching classes in how to teach Black history; school students began visiting. [1, 2, 3, 5]

In 1968 the museum took the name it still carries, for Jean Baptiste Point du Sable, the Afro-French fur trader the Encyclopedia of Chicago identifies as Chicago's first non-Indigenous permanent settler. The renamed DuSable Museum of African American History outgrew the Michigan Avenue house. In 1973 the Chicago Park District granted the use of a park administration building in Washington Park — a building that had also served as a Chicago police lockup — and the museum moved to its present ground at 740 E. 56th Place. Across the street from its first home, the South Side Community Art Center, which Burroughs had also helped found, the young museum had formed part of a Bronzeville cultural corridor; in Washington Park it became a larger civic institution, hosting political fundraisers, community festivals, and social and civic events for Chicago's Black community, and serving as a model emulated by Black museums founded in other American cities. [2, 3, 5]

The building grew with the institution. In 1993 a new wing named for Harold Washington, Chicago's first African American mayor (1983–87), added exhibition space, an auditorium, and a research library, including a permanent exhibition on Washington's life and public service. The museum's permanent presentation also includes "Freedom: Origin and Journey," tracing the African American experience through successive periods. In 2016 the museum became a Smithsonian Institution affiliate. In 2022 it adopted its present name, DuSable Black History Museum and Education Center. The museum describes itself as the nation's first museum devoted entirely to the history and culture of the African American community and as its longest-standing independent keeper; Britannica describes it as the oldest independently owned museum in the United States dedicated to African American history and culture. Both phrasings are printed in the Gaps section rather than merged. [1, 3, 4]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Two grounds carry this story. The founding ground is the Burroughs home at 3806 S. Michigan Avenue in Bronzeville, where the museum began in 1961; that house is a separate historic property (see Gaps). The museum's working home since 1973 is the former South Park administration building in Washington Park at 740 E. 56th Place. [2, 3, 4, 5]

**Lawful physical anchor.** DuSable Black History Museum and Education Center, 740 East 56th Place, Chicago. Enter through the museum's public entrance during staffed hours; the surrounding Washington Park grounds are public park ground. The Michigan Avenue founding house is not this dossier's anchor and no interior access to it is promised here. The museum's campus also includes a second Burnham-designed structure south of 57th Street (the former stables/roundhouse), leased from the Park District in the early 2000s for rehabilitation as event space; its public visitor terms were not verified this run and it is not a promised stop. [1, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Begin inside the museum: the founding story first — a parlor cleared of furniture in 1961 — then the permanent exhibitions, with the Harold Washington Wing as the second half of the visit, since Washington's mayoralty is part of the same civic story the museum was built to tell. Step out to the terrace and sculpture garden afterward and read the building itself: a park administration headquarters, briefly a police lockup, turned by a community-founded museum into its permanent home. **Strongest story moment · The parlor.** In 1961 the museum was one cleared room in the founders' own house, stocked with what neighbors carried in. Everything at 740 E. 56th Place — the Burnham building, the Washington Wing, the Smithsonian affiliation — grew out of that room. Keep the visit unhurried; this is a working community institution, not a roadside stop. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Building date variance: secondary accounts give the administration building's design as c. 1915 by D.H. Burnham and Company; the Cultural Landscape Foundation dates the former South Park Commission headquarters to 1910. Both dates are printed; the dossier does not choose between them. [4]
- Wing date variance: Britannica and the museum's standard accounts date the Harold Washington Wing to 1993; TCLF records architect Wendall Campbell designing the 25,000-square-foot wing in 1992, dedicated to Washington. The 1992 design / 1993 completion reading is plausible but is not asserted here as resolved. [3, 4]
- Collection size variance: Britannica gives more than 15,000 pieces of art and memorabilia; BlackPast gives more than 13,000 artifacts, works of art, and books; a 2010 obituary account gave more than 100,000 pieces including manuscripts and papers. No single figure is treated as exact here. [3, 5]
- "First / oldest" phrasing: the museum's own visitor page calls it the nation's first museum devoted entirely to African American history and culture and the longest-standing independent keeper of Black America's history; Britannica calls it the oldest independently owned museum of its kind in the United States; Smithsonian Magazine calls it the oldest independently owned museum of Black culture in the United States. The claims agree in substance and differ in wording; all are attributed, none is restated as this dossier's own finding. [1, 2, 3]
- Register-status confusion in secondary listings: some travel listings state flatly that the museum was designated a Chicago Landmark and added to the National Register in 2004. TCLF's more precise record is that both campus buildings are contributing features of the Washington Park nomination listed in 2004 and of the Chicago Park Boulevard System Historic District listed in 2018. This dossier follows the contributing-feature record and prints the flatter claim as unverified. [4]
- Hours: the operator page checked this run gives Tuesday–Sunday 10 a.m.–5 p.m., last admission 4:30 p.m. Third-party listings retrieved in the same search gave Wednesday–Sunday 11 a.m.–4 p.m. and other variants. Follow the operator page and confirm before a time-critical visit. [1]
- The founding house at 3806 S. Michigan Ave. is described in secondary accounts as a Chicago Landmark and National Register listing in its own right (as a former railroad workers' social club and boarding house); its nomination record was not retrieved this run, and no access terms are stated for it here.
