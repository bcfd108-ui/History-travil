## 2. The story

**A statement from Fargo, not a later recollection.** On **May 24, 1918**, the North Dakota Woman’s Christian Temperance Union sent the Senate a communication signed by president **Elizabeth Preston Anderson** and corresponding secretary **Barbara H. Wylie**. Senator Gronna presented it for printing in the *Congressional Record*. The statement appears on printed **page 7153**, in the May 28, 1918 record. Both PDF text and the scanned page were directly examined. [3]

**Their words.** The signers protested delay on the federal suffrage amendment and claimed to represent 3,000 women. They challenged the inconsistency of fighting for democracy abroad while withholding it at home, calling enfranchisement **“this act of simple justice.”** That is a contemporary signed organizational statement, not a recording of Anderson speaking. The membership figure is the petitioners’ claim, not an independently audited count. [3]

**Original interpretation.** Their argument converts public service into a demand for equal political standing. The striking moment is not a parade photograph: it is two women insisting that national democratic language should apply to them. Preserve Wylie’s signature beside Anderson’s rather than letting the more familiar name absorb the collective work.

**A movement, not one biography.** The state’s research guide lists **Linda Slaughter**, **Nina Farley Wishek**, Anderson and several organizations as documentary avenues. It identifies Anderson’s papers as **10108**, including a scrapbook, temperance-lobbying essays and autobiography. Those manuscripts were not retrieved for this draft. [4]

## 3. Then / now

**Partial rights were genuinely partial.** The state education account records defeat of the 1914 suffrage referendum and a more limited 1917 law. Women could vote for presidential electors and specified local offices but not legislators or members of Congress. Anderson and Wylie were therefore writing after a real gain that had not delivered full electoral equality. [5]

**State ratification was another stage.** NPS dates North Dakota’s ratification of the Nineteenth Amendment to **December 1, 1919**. This is not the date of the Fargo communication, nor the national completion date. Keeping those events separate shows why a successful local law did not end the campaign. [6]

**The ending must stay honest.** Historian **Liette Gidlow**, writing for NPS, explains that the amendment removed sex as a voting barrier but left racial, citizenship and other exclusions intact. Many Native women lacked U.S. citizenship before the 1924 act, and citizenship itself did not erase all voting obstacles. This national context is not a substitute for a North Dakota-specific Tribal voting history or a named Tribal community’s account. [7]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Prepare one page.** Read the 1918 communication before considering an archive visit. Retain its place, date, signatories and printed page number so the institutional setting does not swallow the actual evidence.

**Enter only the public research process.** If a visit is independently arranged, use the institution’s registration and requests. There is no self-directed access to stacks, storage or staff areas. Keep materials in their supplied order and follow staff instructions. [2]

**Read across the change.** Pair the communication with the contextual distinction between limited 1917 suffrage and 1919 ratification. This is a documentary sequence, not an assumption that the originals are exhibited next to one another.

**End with the unfinished promise.** The strongest story moment is the gap between the signers’ demand for justice and the women still excluded after 1920. Let the visit end in reflection rather than an all-women-won celebration. No reenactment, test or political action is requested.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**What was directly checked:** the scanned federal page, its extracted text, current archive visitor rules and the cited interpretive pages. No archival manuscript, audio interview or historical voting roll was physically examined.

**Document versus signature:** the record prints the women’s names and offices. It does not prove which signer drafted each sentence. The text is organizational advocacy, not a private diary or an eyewitness account of every campaign event.

**Do not repeat universal claims.** The state guide uses broad language about universal enfranchisement; that shorthand is qualified here using the NPS analysis of continuing exclusions. A future North Dakota-specific treatment needs additional primary and Tribal-published evidence, not a borrowed national timeline. [4][7]

**Travel gaps:** date-specific closures, retrieval availability, entrance accessibility and any reproduction charges have not been confirmed. No gallery display, object placement or appointment is represented as verified.
