# HT-RES-OH-john-rankin-house — A brick house on a hill that a river could read
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

From the crest of Liberty Hill above Ripley, the Ohio River and the Kentucky shore lie in plain sight — and for nearly forty years before the Civil War, that sightline ran both ways. The small brick house John and Jean Rankin built on the hill was home to thirteen children and, on the estimate its operator still carries, to more than two thousand people escaping slavery, sheltered on their way north in a town that made conducting a communal trade. The house survives, restored, still looking down the slope they climbed.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a guided tour of a one-and-a-half-storey brick house museum on a bluff above the Ohio River at Ripley, in Brown County, Ohio, with its visitor centre, grounds, and the steep hillside steps below it. The operator is the Ohio History Connection; the house is a National Historic Landmark. The operator's own site page states the terms, printed here as that page carries them: open April 7 through October, Wednesday to Saturday 10:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. and Sunday noon to 5:00 p.m., closed Monday and Tuesday and on Easter Sunday; tours are on the hour, the last beginning at 4 p.m.; allow one hour or more. Admission as the operator's page carries it: Adult $10.00; Children/Students (K-12) $5.00; Ohio History Connection Member free; Ripley Heritage Members free; Children 5 & Under free; Golden Buckeye Card Holders $7.00. The operator's page gives the address as 6152 Rankin Hill Road, Ripley, OH, and the contact number as 800.752.2705. Group-tour arrangements are handled by phone, per the operator's group-visits page, which repeats the same hours.

## 2. The story
John Rankin was a Presbyterian minister formed by the revival country of Tennessee. He was born in Jefferson County, Tennessee — **February 4, 1793** in the date most biographical accounts carry, though a conflicting February 5 date prints in §6 — educated at Washington College Academy under the abolitionist Samuel Doak, and graduated in **1816**. He helped found the Tennessee Manumission Society in **1815**, was ordained in **1817**, and spent roughly four years preaching at Carlisle, Kentucky, where he started a school for enslaved people that mobs drove from a schoolhouse, to an empty house, to a friend's kitchen, until the students stopped coming. The landmark nomination summarises the arc plainly: Rankin worked for the antislavery cause from 1817 until the close of the Civil War in **1865**.

The move north was made in winter, by water. On the night of **December 31, 1821 – January 1, 1822**, Rankin rowed his family across the icy Ohio River to Ripley, Ohio — a free-state river town described as rough, saloon-heavy, and already settled by anti-slavery Virginians. He became minister of Ripley Presbyterian Church and founded a boys' academy. The family's first permanent Ripley home was a brick dwelling on Front Street, yards from the river — built about 1823, in the marker record's dating — and it was from that house, not yet the hill house, that Rankin wrote the work that made his name. Provoked by his brother Thomas, a Virginia slaveholder, he wrote letters against slavery that the local newspaper, *The Castigator*, published; gathered in book form in **1826** as *Letters on American Slavery*, they became, in the Ohio historic-sites summary's words, among the first clearly articulated antislavery views printed west of the Appalachians. The letters worked on their first reader: Thomas freed the people he held and moved to Ohio. They also travelled — Garrison, Theodore Weld, Henry Ward Beecher, and Harriet Beecher Stowe all fell under Rankin's influence, and the line attributed to Beecher after the war, asked who abolished slavery — "Reverend John Rankin and his sons did" — measures the reputation those letters built.

The hill house came next, and its date is genuinely unsettled; §6 prints the conflict in full. The landmark nomination — the fullest primary description this draft rests on — states that the house was **constructed in 1828**: an unadorned one-and-a-half-storey, three-bay brick building in a Federal vernacular manner, about thirty-five feet square around a centre hallway, laid in Flemish bond on its south facade and common bond elsewhere, on an uncoursed limestone foundation, with flush chimneys at both gable ends. It was built in two sections: the main block, and a two-bay brick wing along the east elevation added as bedroom space for a family that would reach thirteen children; a one-storey kitchen wing at the northwest corner appears in historic photographs and was likely built about the same time, though the 1936 HABS plans do not include it. Several accounts date the family's move up the hill to **1829**, with nine children then living — a move date, not necessarily a build date, and this draft keeps the two separate.

What the house was for is the reason it is landmarked. Set on the crest of Liberty Hill, with a commanding view over Ripley, the river, and the Kentucky shore, it became one of the best-known and best-documented stations of the Underground Railroad. From **1822 to 1865**, in the operator's framing, Rankin, his wife Jean, and their children sheltered people who had crossed from Kentucky, a slave state, into Ohio — and Ohio was not safety itself: slavery was illegal there, but under the Fugitive Slave Law of **1850** a freedom seeker could still be apprehended, and had to leave the United States, for Canada, to be beyond reach. The Rankins fed, hid, and moved people onward, sometimes as many as twelve at a time under the roof; when Rankin was away, Jean and the children protected the house against bounty hunters and escorted people to neighbouring stations. The house was watched by slave catchers, and slave owners put a price on Rankin's head. The operator's current estimate, printed as an estimate, is that over 2,000 enslaved people seeking freedom stayed with the Rankins; §6 sets that figure against smaller counts elsewhere and leaves the disagreement standing. Ripley was not one man's work: the operator's pages stress the neighbours, the church community, and conductors such as John P. Parker, the African American inventor and formerly enslaved man whose Front Street house is Ripley's other Underground Railroad landmark.

One Ripley story outgrew the town. In the winter of **1838**, a woman escaped across the frozen Ohio River carrying her child in her arms, and was sheltered by the Rankins. Rankin later told the account to Professor Calvin Stowe at Lane Theological Seminary in Cincinnati; Stowe's wife, Harriet Beecher Stowe, heard it, and the escape became the basis for Eliza's crossing in *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, published in **1852**. The operator and the landmark tradition both carry the connection, and the 1997 landmark designation rests in part on it. A second tradition — the lantern in the hill-house window as a signal to people on the Kentucky shore — is carried here as tradition, not documented fact: no primary record consulted documents the signal as practice. The hillside steps below the house, by contrast, are physical: a staircase descends the steep slope toward the town and river, counted at about 150 steps in the operator's visiting account — a figure that conflicts with the "100 steps" of local naming, printed in §6.

After the Civil War the Rankins sold the house. The State of Ohio purchased it in **1938**, two years after the Historic American Buildings Survey had recorded it — the survey whose photograph is this entry's first photo (§7) — renovated it, and dedicated it as a state memorial and museum on **June 13, 1948**. The nomination records the property at about twenty acres, owned by the state and operated as a historic site; today the Ohio History Connection operates it, describing a house restored to look as though the Rankins had just stepped away. The house was listed on the National Register on **November 10, 1970**, and designated a National Historic Landmark on **February 18, 1997** — nationally significant, in the nomination's terms, for its association with the Underground Railroad in Ohio and with Rankin, reputed one of Ohio's first and most active conductors.

Rankin himself died on **March 18, 1886**, in Kansas — a man who had argued against slavery in print before Garrison's *Liberator* existed, and who lived to see the institution ended by constitutional amendment. What the house sheltered people toward — the far shore, the next station, Canada — is the geography a visitor still reads from its windows.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a new brick house on a bare hill crest, built in sections as a family of fifteen outgrew it, its windows commanding the river crossing below, its household organised around the illegal work of receiving people at night and moving them on. **Now:** the same house, restored and furnished as a museum, toured on the hour in season, its hilltop prospect over Ripley, the river, and the Kentucky hills intact.

**Then:** in November 1936, when the Historic American Buildings Survey photographed it, a weathered, deteriorating house — chairs on the porch, a wire fence across the yard, its gable end patched in sheet material — two years before the state bought it. **Now:** the restored landmark of the operator's photographs; the 1936 view in §7 is printed precisely because it shows the house before rescue.

**Then:** a river town where conducting was dangerous, collective work — Rankins on the hill, Parker on Front Street, neighbours and church people throughout — conducted under surveillance. **Now:** a 55-acre National Register historic district along Front Street, with both houses open as museums on the Ohio Historical Underground Railroad Trail.

## 4. What's still there
- The Rankin House itself — the one-and-a-half-storey, three-bay brick house, about thirty-five feet square, Flemish bond facade, limestone foundation, east bedroom wing and northwest kitchen wing, restored and operated as a house museum (NHL nomination; Ohio History Connection)
- The hilltop setting on Liberty Hill, with the commanding view of Ripley, the Ohio River, and northern Kentucky that the nomination makes central to the site's significance (NHL nomination)
- The hillside steps descending from the house toward the town — about 150 steps in the Ohio History Connection's own visiting account (Ohio History Connection)
- The visitor centre and gift shop at the site, off Rankin Hill Road (Ohio History Connection)
- The 1936 HABS record of the house — two photographs and ten measured drawings, held by the Library of Congress as HABS OH-630 — documenting the house before the 1948 renovation (Library of Congress; NHL nomination)
- The John P. Parker House on Front Street, a separate site and record, named here as the operator names it — Ripley's other Underground Railroad landmark (Ohio History Connection)
- Ripley's Front Street historic district, 55 acres on the National Register, per the operator's site page (Ohio History Connection)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the visitor centre:** Begin with the operator's orientation to the Rankin family, the Ripley community, and the Underground Railroad, before the hourly house tour.
2. **Inside the house, on the guided tour:** The restored rooms as the operator presents them — the household of fifteen that was also a station — with the river prospect the nomination frames.
3. **On the hilltop ground outside:** Stand where the house reads the river — the Kentucky shore across the Ohio — and reckon with the sightline that made the hill a station and a target.
4. **On the hillside steps:** The descent toward the town and the river, at the operator's count of about 150 steps — and the climb back, which the operator's own visiting account warns takes considerably longer.
5. **On Front Street, as a separate ground:** The first Rankin home's street and the John P. Parker House — distinct sites with their own records, not part of this house's tour.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The house's construction date is a three-way disagreement, and all three statements print here unresolved. The National Historic Landmark nomination states, in its description, that the house was "Constructed in 1828." The Ohio History Connection's own site page — the operator's current public statement — states "Built in 1825." The Library of Congress HABS survey record for the same house carries, in its notes, "Building/structure dates: 1815 Initial Construction." These cannot all describe the same event in the same sense; whether they reflect different datings of the main block, the east wing, an earlier structure on the hill, or simple error in one or more records is not established by any source consulted, and this draft adopts none of them. Separately, several accounts date the family's move into the hill house to 1829; a move date does not settle a build date, and the two are not merged here.

The number of people the Rankins sheltered is likewise printed as a range of claims, not a count. The Ohio History Connection's site page estimates "over 2,000 slaves seeking freedom stayed with the Rankins, sometimes as many as 12 at a time," and its visiting writing repeats the 2,000 figure as an estimate. The Ohio historic-sites summary carried in reference form credits Rankin, his wife, and his children with assisting "hundreds of escaped slaves" — a materially smaller claim. Both print; the surviving record does not permit this draft to choose between an estimate in the thousands and a summary in the hundreds, and any single number stated without its source would misrepresent the evidence.

John Rankin's birth date conflicts by one day in the biographical record: February 4, 1793 in the standard biographical account, February 5, 1793 in the America's Black Holocaust Museum's biographical article. Both print; neither is adopted, and no primary vital record was consulted for this draft.

The hillside steps carry two counts. The Ohio History Connection's own visiting account counts 150 steps and warns visitors accordingly; local naming calls the stair the "100 Steps to Freedom." Both print unresolved — no step-by-step count from a survey record was consulted.

The house's address is stated two ways in primary-adjacent records: the landmark nomination's location block reads "6152 Rankin Road," while the operator's current site page reads "6152 Rankin Hill Road, Ripley, OH." The operator's current form is used in §1 and the locator, with the nomination's form printed here as the variant it is.

The lantern-signal tradition is flagged as a gap of a different kind: it is universally retold and repeated in the operator's descriptive material, but no primary document consulted for this draft — not the nomination, not the HABS record — documents a window signal as the Rankins' practice. It is carried in §2 as tradition only.

No coordinates are printed for this entry. The Library of Congress HABS record carries blank latitude and longitude fields for this house, and the landmark nomination consulted states a street address and boundary description but no coordinate point verified as the house. Lead-list material offers at least two differing coordinate pairs for this house; per this package's locator discipline, none was used, and latitude and longitude below stay blank.

## 7. Photo record
**First photograph:** *The Rankin House from the southeast, 1936* — a black-and-white survey photograph, framed by its film border: a one-and-a-half-storey brick house seen from the southeast, its long porch running along the front under a shed roof on plain posts, two rocking chairs visible on the porch boards, brick chimneys rising from the roof ridge and the far gable, and the near gable end — patched in large sheet panels — pierced by three windows. Bare winter trees stand at both sides of the frame, a wire fence with wooden posts crosses the foreground, and dry grass covers the yard. The house reads as weathered and deteriorating, not restored: this is the building as the federal survey found it in 1936, two years before the state purchased it. No people appear in the frame.
