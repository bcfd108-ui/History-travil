# The Delta childhood behind a television puppet

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP21*

## 2. Story

Jim Henson spent part of his childhood in Leland. The company’s own founder history identifies his parents, Paul and Betty Henson, and places his birth at King’s Daughters Hospital in Greenville in 1936. Paul worked for the U.S. Department of Agriculture. The early Delta setting belongs to the person who later helped make the character, and Leland remembers that part of his experience through a visitor exhibit. [1–3]

The professional record starts in a different place. The company’s original donation announcement says *Sam and Friends* debuted on Washington station WRC-TV in 1955. It featured an earlier, less frog-like Kermit. Jim and Jane Henson worked as partners early on. Much later Jane represented the family when versions of early characters went into the Smithsonian’s collection. The company’s release describes the puppet-face design focus as “the magic triangle.” It is a short quotation from the institution closest to the makers and their design history. It helps a visitor see craft and collaboration inside the finished character. [2][4]

The local operator calls the Leland exhibit a “gift to the people of Leland” from the Jim Henson Company. Families visiting today can see a tableau and family photographs in a former visitor and Chamber building. The display gives Leland a place to connect Henson’s childhood to his later work. It is a local interpretation of an affectionately adopted title. The claim that the broadcast puppet literally debuted on Deer Creek conflicts with the company’s professional broadcast chronology and is left out. [1–4]

## 3. Then and now

Henson’s childhood in Leland and birth in Greenville precede his broadcast work in Washington. The current museum is a former public visitor and Chamber building repurposed for the exhibit. Its artifacts, reproductions and tableau should be read under their own labels. The early Smithsonian puppets are elsewhere. [1–4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Staffed gallery.** The signed exhibit entrance and public displays. The operator posts free ordinary admission. Group tours follow the operator’s instructions. [1]
- **Historical content.** A tableau represents a story. Family photographs link to the biography printed in the company’s materials. No claim is made that a specific puppet is an on-site broadcast original without a label. [1][2][4]
- **Outside scope.** Creek bank, former homes and other properties remain outside this visit. [1–3]
