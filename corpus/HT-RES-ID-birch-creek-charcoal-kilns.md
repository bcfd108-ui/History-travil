# Birch Creek · The charcoal kilns that fed Nicholia

History Travel · Idaho · Draft for Dash QC · Desk review 18 November 2026

## 2. The story

Stand where the slope meets the row of kilns and the odd placement of the doors begins to make sense. Workers packed the chamber from below. Once they could fit no more cordwood through that opening they finished loading through a higher opening reached from the bank behind. An engineering-survey photograph records the rear doors and grade together. That piece of slope was part of the machinery. The domes did not smelt ore. They made the charcoal that other people hauled across the valley to the furnaces at Nicholia. [1, 4, 5]

Sixteen kilns were built about 1885. The slopes west of the works supplied timber. Across the valley, ore descended from the Viola Mine to Smelter Gulch. The kiln workers’ load traveled the other way, by wagon, about thirteen miles to the smelter. Woodland was the settlement at the fuel end, Nicholia at the ore end. Forest archaeologist Steve Armstrong cites a November 8, 1888 Idaho Recorder notice as evidence that Woodland supported a school. The newspaper itself was not retrieved. That small reference is worth following: children and families belong in the story of a place whose surviving equipment looks so solitary. [1, 4, 6]

Four structures survive. Two still have domes. The spaces where others stood are part of the evidence. James A. McDonald photographed the row in 1988 for the Historic American Engineering Record (HAER), returning to the view of an older photograph and identifying the surviving isolated kiln in both. Armstrong’s 2025 project photographs show the next stage in that history: scaffolding on a dome, a mortar cap and limewash. He reported in January 2026 that “the lower arched doorways on all four kilns need some additional restoration work.” No completion of that proposed work is assumed here. [1, 5]

## 3. Then / now

An operating fuel works with a school in its adjoining settlement is now a row of surviving brick shells, incomplete domes and archaeological traces. Opposite it was an ore-producing and smelting district. The main visit stays at the publicly interpreted fuel works. The mine, townsite, buried traces and smelter do not become visitor stops merely because they appear on a historical map. [1–5]

## 4. What’s still there

- **View from public ground** — four surviving kiln exteriors, their position in the bank and the gaps along the former row, from the interpretive area when reachable. [1, 2, 5]
- **Exterior only** — use the paths to study the door positions and vents. No source retrieved authorizes entering surviving shells, and the restorer documented vulnerable arches. [1, 2]
- **View from public ground, separate location** — Idaho highway marker 296 summarizes the connection to Nicholia. Its published position is at the road junction, not at the kilns. Use the online record if the turnoff cannot be used legally or safely. [7]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Begin with the highway panel or its online transcription. Its words “across the valley” place the smelter on the other side of a supply journey. Proceed to the kilns only after the district confirms access. [1–3, 7]
2. On an open visit follow the interpretive path and compare a lower opening with the level of the bank where the upper loading door was reached. From home use HAER frame ID-11-3. [1, 5]
3. **Strongest story moment** — line up the isolated kiln with the surviving group. Look from HAER’s early view of the sixteen to McDonald’s repeat. Keep your attention on the gaps. They show the scale of daily work in a settlement whose original scale the remaining brickwork alone cannot convey. This comparison also works on a phone. [1, 5]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Armstrong gives about 150 people employed. Idaho Heritage Trust says as many as 200. The workforce counted and time period may differ. Armstrong identifies King with Butte, the Trust with Missoula. No business correspondence or payroll was recovered to reconcile either point. The Trust estimates 44,000–50,000 bushels of charcoal produced monthly against a smelter demand of 50,000–60,000. These are different measures, not alternative figures for kiln output. [1, 4]

The highway marker stresses a hoist fire in 1889 and low metal prices. Armstrong’s account emphasizes ore depletion, and the nomination also records a fire. None establishes a single cause. The register says bricks were hauled away for construction, while Armstrong characterizes local reuse as rumored. The road marker places the kilns six miles west, the archaeologist about five miles from the highway. Retain both descriptions. The current forest recreation page gives no site-level road status. [1–4, 6, 7]

The HAER catalogue index dates the old image to about 1895, tentatively, and Armstrong labels an Oberg reproduction about 1898. A common negative for the two reproductions has not been verified. Neither is a firm date. The Library of Congress item viewer returned HTTP 403. Its official photograph caption sheet opened. [1, 5, 8]

## 7. Photo record

**HAER ID-11-1**, photographer unknown, tentatively circa 1895, copy credited to Felicia Nichols. **ID-11-2**, James A. McDonald, September 1988, explicitly repeats the earlier view. **Photo Point for both — APPROXIMATE.** Look north-northwest along the row from its southern side. The isolated surviving kiln must coincide with the leftmost kiln in the old photograph, while the surviving cluster lines up farther along the same bench. The HAER caption identifies the match. High confidence in the relative alignment and direction, low in a precise camera position. Stop at the nearest matching public path. [5]

**ID-11-3**, McDonald, September 1988, rear elevations looking south. **Photo Point — APPROXIMATE**, north of the row looking south, aligning the upper loading openings with the slope behind them. High confidence in side and bearing, low in whether a present public path duplicates it. Use the archive view if paths do not. Armstrong’s Fig. 2 and its red dots are a later annotated reproduction. They are useful for counting, not separate historic markings. [1, 5]
