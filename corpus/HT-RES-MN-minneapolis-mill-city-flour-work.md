# Minneapolis Mill City — The machines were still there
Research draft · Minnesota OP01 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

**A room after the workers had gone.** Thomas Meyer, interviewed by Linda Mack in 2008 for the Minnesota Historical Society, remembered the abandoned Washburn A Mill having “shoes in the lockers and stacks of original drawings.” He recalled belts and wheels still in place and yellow light filtering across wood and stone. His transcript carries what he saw as a visitor and architect, and his inference that the millers had expected to come back. It gives one view from after shutdown. The museum’s Flour Tower incorporates voices of former workers, whose unedited individual recordings were not obtained for quotation here. [2–3]

## 3 · Then / now

**Then — power, grain and people.** The mill and its surrounding Washburn-Crosby complex drew on the falls and on rail shipments of wheat. The first Washburn A Mill exploded in 1878. The later listed complex incorporates the rebuilt mill. The federal photo survey records the later ruined building after the separate 1991 fire, with stripped masonry standing around an interior once filled with levels of machinery. A site visit offers three distinct periods to read: the rebuilt production mill, the fire-damaged structure, and the museum inserted into surviving fabric. [2,4–7]

**Now — surviving walls and a modern route through an old vertical machine.** The museum’s elevator follows eight levels in the Flour Tower program. The Ruin Courtyard retains traces of machinery attachment and the visitor deck looks back across the Mississippi. Neither is a blanket invitation into other industrial remains. [1–2,4–6]

## 4 · What’s still there

- **Public interior by museum admission.** Exhibits, machines on display and Flour Tower with a time-specific slot issued at the ticket desk. [1–2]
- **Public exterior when open and weather allows.** Operator-managed Ruin Courtyard and Koch Rooftop Observation Deck. Areas closed by staff are outside the route. [2]
- **View from public ground.** Museum’s street frontage along the open South 2nd Street approach. Remaining industrial spaces elsewhere in the complex have no proposed access. [1,4–5]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Historic location.** The Washburn A Mill complex at the falls. HAER’s recorded mill address and the operator’s listed visitor entrance differ because they describe different scopes. [1,4–5]
2. **Lawful anchor.** Use the signed Mill City Museum entrance at 704 S. 2nd St. The museum and its marked visitor areas are the only indoor route. [1–2]
3. **Strongest story moment — An emptied wall full of work.** Finish where the masonry and machine traces meet, from the open Ruin Courtyard or the adjacent authorized exhibit if weather closes the courtyard. Read Meyer’s short line and give the work voices in the Flour Tower their own time when a slot is available. Treat the fatal 1878 explosion as quiet witness. [2–3,6–7]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Meyer’s transcript is first-person to his own access and work in preservation. His conjecture about what millers expected is distinct from what he saw. The direct LOC item request through web retrieval returned HTTP 403; its Pictures collection and raw HAER caption were read by separate paths. The selected Daily Globe page from May 3, 1878 was inaccessible through the web retrieval tool, so the blast account here follows MNHS rather than quoting that issue. No period labor roster or individual production worker transcript was recovered. [2–7,9]
