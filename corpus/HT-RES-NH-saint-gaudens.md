# HT-RES-NH-saint-gaudens — The Sculptor's House Above the Connecticut

Division NH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a terraced hillside above the Connecticut River at Cornish, New Hampshire, stands Aspet: the home, studios, and gardens of Augustus Saint-Gaudens (1848–1907), the foremost American sculptor of his generation. He came for one summer in 1885, renting an old brick inn from his friend Charles C. Beaman, converted its hay barn into a studio, and stayed, in season and then for good, for the remaining twenty-two years of his life. Around him gathered the Cornish Colony of artists; his studios here and in New York produced the Shaw Memorial, the Sherman Monument, and the coin designs that closed his career. The estate his widow made a memorial in 1919 is today Saint-Gaudens National Historical Park — the only National Park Service unit in New Hampshire besides a portion of the Appalachian Trail.

## 1. Trip gate

Saint-Gaudens National Historical Park is administered by the National Park Service. The visitor address is 139 Saint Gaudens Road, Cornish, NH 03745, just off New Hampshire Route 12A; phone 603-675-2175. From Interstate 91, the park's directions run from Exit 8 (Ascutney) east to NH 12A north.

The Park Service's operating-hours page gives the season plainly. Park grounds and trails are open year-round during daylight hours. The park buildings — visitor center, Little Studio, Picture Gallery, and New Gallery — are open Thursday through Monday from Memorial Day weekend until October 31, 9:00 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. Aspet, the historic home, is open select hours as staffing allows. An entry fee or proof of a park pass is required on the grounds in season between 9:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m.; the brochure adds that America the Beautiful passes are honored. Between November and May the walkways are not maintained and may be icy or snow-covered. The brochure notes the park has no public telephone, food service, or camping; neighboring communities offer these services.

The park's own brochure counts approximately 30,000 visitors a year. The Saint-Gaudens Memorial, the park's partner organization, advises allowing at least 90 minutes to visit; more than 100 sculptures by Saint-Gaudens are exhibited in the galleries and on the grounds in season.

The visitor boundary is that of a working artist's estate kept as a park: in season the buildings and grounds are taken together on the one admission; out of season the ground stays open in daylight, but the buildings do not.

## 2. The story

The house predates the sculptor by nearly seventy years. The Park Service's landscape history records a 134-acre farm whose house, "Huggins' Folly," was built in 1817 by brothers Samuel and Jonas Huggins and kept as an inn; Walter Mercer, a woolen mill owner, acquired it in 1829, and Mercer sold the farm in 1884 to the New York lawyer Charles Cotesworth Beaman Jr. Beaman had bought the nearby Blow-Me-Down Farm as his own summer place and was drawing his artist friends to Cornish. In the summer of 1885 he rented the old inn to Augustus Saint-Gaudens.

Saint-Gaudens arrived already famous. Born in Dublin on March 1, 1848, to a French shoemaker and his Irish wife, raised in New York City, apprenticed to a cameo cutter at thirteen, trained at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris and in Rome, he had won his first major commission in 1876 — the Farragut monument, unveiled in New York in 1881 to acclaim — in a Paris and New York career that had nothing to do with New Hampshire. What Cornish gave him was a country seat built around the work. He adapted the house to his needs, converted the adjacent hay barn into a studio, and came back every summer with his wife Augusta and their son Homer. He named the estate Aspet, after the town in France where his father was born. The park brochure states that he finally purchased the property in 1892; the Park Service's own landscape history gives 1891, and the conflict is printed unresolved in §6.

The Cornish years were a studio system, not a retreat. As commissions poured in, Saint-Gaudens built a large studio where his assistants worked; in the brochure's phrase, his role became that of an executive producer — developing the concept and the initial models himself, then directing assistants in completing the work. The park's touring text ties the converted barn studio to the *Standing Lincoln* years: the Lincoln for Chicago, unveiled in 1887, was on his boards across his first Cornish summers, though his working base through 1897 remained New York. The *Shaw Memorial* — the bronze relief to Colonel Robert Gould Shaw and the 54th Massachusetts Regiment, unveiled on Boston Common in 1897 — took fourteen years to complete, the brochure's figure, and its casts and studies were worked across his New York and Cornish studios; the park exhibits a bronze cast of it, made in 1997, on the Bowling Green, which the brochure notes is unique and differs slightly from the original. The *Sherman Monument*'s full-size model stood in the Cornish studio — the brochure captions its photograph "Model of the Sherman Monument, Cornish, NH, ca. 1901" — before the gilded group was unveiled in New York in 1903, its winged Victory leading the general.

Two famous works often laid at Cornish's door were not made here, and this entry keeps them separate. The *Adams Memorial* of 1891 — Henry Adams's shrouded figure for his wife Clover in Washington's Rock Creek Cemetery, set by Stanford White — belongs to the years when Saint-Gaudens was based in New York; what stands at Aspet is a recast of 1974 in the gardens, the original's lineage made visible on the ground where he lived. *Diana*, the nude archer of 1891–92, was modeled in New York for Stanford White's Madison Square Garden tower; the park exhibits a cast of the second version, but the figure is a New York work. The last great commission did close at Aspet. In 1905 Theodore Roosevelt engaged him to redesign the gold coinage; working at Cornish with his assistant Henry Hering, who executed the plaster models from the sculptor's designs under his direction, Saint-Gaudens drew the double eagle's striding Liberty from the Sherman Victory. He died at Aspet on August 3, 1907, before the coins were minted.

The studio buildings carry their own losses. In 1904 the large studio burned, destroying the sculptor's correspondence, sketchbooks, and many works in progress; its replacement, the Studio of the Caryatids, burned in 1944. His personal studio survives: the Little Studio, built in 1904 after designs by George Fletcher Babb on the ground of the old barn studio, its red walls set with casts from the Parthenon frieze and a pergola of Doric columns — which the brochure dates to 1889, added after a trip to Italy, carried from the barn studio's arrangement into the new building.

Around the sculptor, Cornish filled with artists. The brochure names the colony: painters Maxfield Parrish, Thomas Dewing, George de Forest Brush, Lucia Fuller, and Kenyon Cox; the dramatist Percy MacKaye; the novelist Winston Churchill; the architect Charles Platt; and the sculptors Paul Manship, Herbert Adams, and Augustus's brother Louis Saint-Gaudens. In 1905 the colony staged "A Masque of Ours: The Gods and the Golden Bowl" at Aspet to honor his twentieth year in Cornish; the Greek temple built as its stage set was later recreated in marble, and that Temple is now the resting place of Saint-Gaudens and his family. Diagnosed with cancer in 1900, he had made Cornish his year-round home; after his death Augusta and Homer continued to summer at Aspet, and in 1919 they established the Saint-Gaudens Memorial to preserve the place. The Memorial ran it as a museum and in 1965 donated the property to the National Park Service. Congress had authorized the national historic site on August 31, 1964; the estate had been designated a National Historic Landmark District on June 13, 1962, and was Register-listed on October 15, 1966. On March 12, 2019, the Dingell Act redesignated it Saint-Gaudens National Historical Park. A Register boundary increase of October 2, 2013 brought in adjoining colony properties, among them Beaman's Blow-Me-Down Farm.

## 3. Then / now

Then, Aspet was a workplace with a famous man at its center. The house filled each summer with family, models, and assistants; the big studio ran at production scale, enlarging his sketches into the monuments that were remaking American public sculpture; the colony's painters, writers, and architects dined, staged masques, and built their own houses along the Cornish roads. The grounds he shaped — terraced gardens, hedged outdoor rooms, a swimming pool, a bowling green, a nine-hole golf course — were the social floor of that world, and after 1900 the establishment ran year-round as his health failed and the work continued anyway.

Now, the same ground is a national park for the arts. The house stands furnished to his occupancy, the Little Studio holds his works where he worked alone, and the galleries the Memorial built from surviving outbuildings after the 1944 fire exhibit the casts. A sculptor-in-residence works in the Ravine Studio where his assistants once carved marble. The colony is gone — its houses are private or, like Blow-Me-Down Farm, folded into the park's boundary — but the estate at its center reads as he left it: house, studios, and gardens above the river, with Mount Ascutney across the water.

## 4. What's still there

Aspet itself: the Federal-style brick house built in 1817 as an inn, "Huggins' Folly," completely remodeled under Saint-Gaudens — the brochure lists a curving stairway with a study added off the main hallway, new bedrooms, a sun room, dormers, and a wide porch with columns. The thornless honey locust before the house was planted in 1886. The first floor is shown as it was in his occupancy; the house opens only at select hours, as staffing allows.

The Little Studio stands where the converted hay barn stood: built in 1904 to George Fletcher Babb's designs, red-walled, its exterior set with casts from the Parthenon frieze, its pergola carried on Doric columns. Saint-Gaudens worked here by himself; it now holds his works and, in the former plaster-casting room, the museum store. The large working studio does not survive — the 1904 original and its replacement, the Studio of the Caryatids, both burned — but the Ravine Studio, built about 1900 for the assistants' marble carving, was restored in 1969 and serves the sculptor-in-residence. The New Gallery and Picture Gallery were made in 1948, when the Memorial remodeled two remaining outbuildings into exhibition galleries four years after the Caryatids fire.

The gardens are exhibits in themselves: terraced garden rooms walled by pine and hemlock hedges, the flower garden, the cutting garden, and the Pan Grove — a birch grove with a green marble pool, a collaborative design of Babb and Saint-Gaudens. On the Bowling Green stands the 1997 bronze cast of the Shaw Memorial; in the gardens, the 1974 recast of the Adams Memorial. At the edge of the grounds, the marble Temple — the 1905 masque's stage set made permanent — holds the ashes of Saint-Gaudens and his family. The stable and icehouse, built before 1885 and remodeled in 1891, survive with exhibits of horse-drawn vehicles. Two trails run into the park's natural areas, the Ravine Trail and the Blow-Me-Down Trail.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Begin at the visitor center for the season's program schedule, then take the Little Studio first: the red walls, the Parthenon casts, the pergola he carried over from the barn studio, and the casting room where the plaster work was done. This was his private working room for the last three years of his life, and it sets the scale of everything else — one man, working small, directing a factory of assistants nearby.

From the studio, take Aspet when its select hours allow: the curving stair and study, the columned porch, the honey locust of 1886 in front. Then the galleries — the Picture Gallery and New Gallery — where the casts and studies carry the public career: the Shaw Memorial's long making, the Sherman model that stood at Cornish, and the coin designs of the last two years, with Diana and the Adams figure present as casts, their originals in New York and Washington.

Go outside for the second half of the visit. Walk the terraced garden rooms to the Bowling Green and stand in front of the 1997 Shaw cast at full size; find the Adams Memorial recast in the gardens and the Pan Grove's marble pool in its birches. End at the Temple, where the colony's 1905 masque set became his family's tomb, and if the day allows, take the Ravine Trail or the Blow-Me-Down Trail out into the ground the colony walked.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The purchase of Aspet is dated two ways inside the Park Service's own publications. The park brochure states that Saint-Gaudens "finally purchased it in 1892," and the Saint-Gaudens Memorial repeats 1892. The Park Service's landscape history states that he rented in 1885 and was "purchasing the property in 1891," and a museum biography gives 1891 as well. Section 2 prints 1892, following the brochure; the 1891 figure is recorded here rather than reconciled.

The house's own date is given two ways. The brochure and the landscape history put its building in 1817 — the landscape history names the builders, Samuel and Jonas Huggins — while the Landmark-era nomination text of 1962 describes Aspet as "built about 1800 and once used as a tavern," and the park touring text repeated "built about 1800 as an inn." The 1817 date is followed here; the circa-1800 dating stands unresolved beside it.

The Little Studio's pergola is dated two ways. The brochure states that Saint-Gaudens added the pergola in 1889, after a trip to Italy; a photographic history of the property dates the pergola's addition to the old barn studio to 1894. Both dates stand. The building itself is dated 1904 by the Park Service and 1903–04 in Register-derived description — built across 1903–04 and completed in 1904 would reconcile the two, but no source fetched states it in that form, so both are printed.

The park's acreage is stated inconsistently. The park brochure calls it "the 190 acre park" and dates an expansion to October 2010; older reference listings give 370 acres in total, 175 of them federal. No current primary statement of acreage was fetched in this pass, so neither figure is asserted in §2, and the 2013 boundary increase is recorded by its Register action rather than by acreage.

The summer period is closed two ways. The brochure states the family "continued to summer here until 1900"; the touring text and standard references describe the house as his summer residence from 1885 to 1897 and his permanent home from 1900 — leaving the Paris years between unaccounted in the brochure's telling. Both versions stand. Federal establishment is likewise layered: authorized August 31, 1964, donated in 1965, Register-listed October 15, 1966 — and dated to a formal establishment of May 30, 1977 in standard references, a date no Park Service source fetched confirms or explains.

One brochure caption sits oddly against the public record: the Shaw cast on the Bowling Green is captioned "Shaw Memorial, final version, 1900, bronze cast 1997," while the Boston memorial itself was unveiled in 1897. The caption is quoted as printed; what the 1900 date refers to is explained by no source fetched. The coordinates in §10 carry their own flag: they convert from the nomination's UTM reference, and the converted point falls roughly a kilometre southwest of the present visitor-center ground — the nomination's reference point and the modern entrance are not the same spot, and no source fetched reconciles them.
