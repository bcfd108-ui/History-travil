# HT-RES-PA-fort-necessity — A 53-foot circle of sticks alone in a wide green meadow
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A small round stockade in a natural meadow, built in weeks by a 22-year-old Virginia officer who had just fired the first shots of a world war — and surrendered in the rain five weeks later, in the only surrender of his military career.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a small fort in a big meadow: Fort Necessity National Battlefield, in Fayette County, Pennsylvania, preserving the Battle of Fort Necessity (the Battle of the Great Meadows) of July 3, 1754. The operator is the National Park Service. The NPS's own pages state the terms, and they are printed here as those pages carry them: the park grounds are open daily from sunrise to sunset, year-round; the Fort Necessity/National Road Interpretive and Education Center is open seven days a week from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. throughout the year except New Year's Day, Thanksgiving, and Christmas, weather permitting; and the reconstructed fort is a 300-yard paved walk from the visitor center, open year-round. The NPS plan-your-visit page allows 1½ to 2 hours for the main unit — the fort and Mount Washington Tavern — and another hour for Braddock's Grave and Jumonville Glen. The NPS pages consulted name no entrance fee for the park, and none is printed here. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000664, per the NPS's own National Register record).

## 2. The story
In 1753 the governor of Virginia sent a young George Washington to the Ohio lands to discourage French settlement. The French persisted, and in the spring of 1754 Washington was sent back, this time — in the NPS's words — to build a military supply road to the forks of the Ohio, where the British hoped to build a fort and needed a road to supply it. The French won that race: by the time Washington reached Pennsylvania they had captured the British fort at the forks, rebuilt it, and renamed it Fort Duquesne; Pittsburgh occupies its site today.

Arriving at a natural clearing called the Great Meadows in May 1754, Washington set up a base camp to wait for further reinforcements. He did not expect to fight there. On May 23, 1754, Joseph Coulon de Jumonville and thirty-three men left Fort Duquesne on a mission the NPS describes as discovering the British forces on French-claimed ground and delivering an official summons. On the stormy night of **May 27, 1754**, Washington and about 40 men made an all-night march. About dawn on **May 28** he met his ally the Half King — Tanaghrisson, a Seneca leader — and together they surrounded the unsuspecting French camp in the glen that now carries Jumonville's name. A shot was fired; the NPS states plainly that no one really knows by whom. The skirmish lasted about 15 minutes. When it was over, 13 Frenchmen were dead and 21 captured on the NPS historyculture page's count, one Frenchman escaped to Fort Duquesne, and Washington's casualties were one man killed and two or three wounded. Among the French dead was Jumonville himself, killed after the fighting by the Half King — the act the French would call an assassination of their envoy, and the opening shots of the French and Indian War.

Washington returned to the Great Meadows and fortified his camp around his storehouse. What he built was not a typical military fort: the NPS, following J. C. Harrington's archaeological report *New Light on Washington's Fort Necessity*, describes a small storage shed in the center of a round stockade **53 feet in diameter**, with earthworks outside it. When he built it there were only 160 men with Washington; by the battle there were 400 British at Fort Necessity.

The answer came five weeks later. Jumonville's older brother, Louis Coulon de Villiers, came up with a French and Indian force the NPS Cultural Landscapes Inventory describes as nearly double Washington's number, with seven American Indian tribes fighting with the French at the battle — Huron, Huron of Lorette, Nipissing, Algonquin, Odawa, Shawnee, and Abenaki, per the NPS's Fort Necessity article. On **July 3, 1754**, the French appeared at the far end of the meadow, took the tree lines, and surrounded the fort. The fight ran eight to nine hours in heavy rain, downpours, mist, and fog. That evening the French called out to ask whether Washington wanted to talk surrender; after three to four hours of negotiation he signed. In signing the surrender document, Washington agreed to having assassinated Jumonville — though Washington said the English translation given him did not use that term. On **July 4, 1754**, the British marched out and returned to Virginia, and the French burned Fort Necessity. It was, the NPS Cultural Landscapes Inventory states, the first and only time Washington surrendered in his military career.

Washington came back through this ground the next year under Gen. Edward Braddock, whose army improved the road Washington had built, split its force to move faster, and was ambushed approaching Fort Duquesne; Braddock and more than half of the roughly 1,200 men with him died. His soldiers buried him near Fort Necessity, concealing the grave in the roadbed by marching over it. The resting place lay hidden until 1804, when workmen discovered a body reported to be the general's; a monument built in 1913 marks the site over the reinterred bones, about a mile west of the visitor center.

The road outlived the campaign. In 1806 the federal government built the first totally federally funded highway — the National Road, today U.S. Route 40 — closely paralleling the Washington/Braddock route at its start. On a hill overlooking the fort site stands Mount Washington Tavern, a stagecoach tavern in the park, named for Washington, who once owned the land it sits on; the NPS article dates its building to the 1830s and its operation to 1855, when rail replaced much of the wagon traffic (but see §6 — the NPS's own handbook gives an earlier date).

The fort itself had to be found twice. For over a century only low ridges and shallow depressions marked the site, and experts debated whether the fort was triangular or diamond-shaped; a 1901 study read the ridges as a diamond. Fort Necessity was established as a National Battlefield in 1931, archaeology that year located the stockade, and the 1932 reconstruction was built as a large diamond-shaped stockade — seriously flawed, the NPS says, but interpreted to the public as correct. In 1952 and 1953, archaeologists looking for a triangular fort found the truth: the fort was neither a diamond nor a triangle, but a circle. That work fixed the true size, shape, and location, and is the basis for the reconstruction visitors see today.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a level, grassy bottom — the Great Meadows — with a 53-foot round stockade and a small storehouse at its center, earthworks outside, tree lines close enough that a surrounding force could fire from them all day in the rain. **Now:** the reconstructed stockade and earthworks standing solitary in the same meadow, with the NPS reestablishing the surrounding battlefield to match the 1754 landscape as closely as possible, reached by a paved 300-yard trail from the visitor center.

**Then:** a diamond-shaped fort, confidently rebuilt in 1932 and shown to the public as correct. **Now:** a round fort, because the 1952–53 archaeology proved the footprint and location of today's stockade and earthworks are the same as the 1754 structures — the park's own record of being wrong, corrected on the ground.

**Then:** a military road cut toward the forks of the Ohio in 1754, improved by Braddock in 1755, with a general's grave hidden in its roadbed. **Now:** the trace of the Braddock Road in the park, Braddock's grave monument of 1913 a mile west of the visitor center, and U.S. Route 40 — the National Road — carrying the same corridor past a stagecoach tavern on the hill.

## 4. What's still there
- The reconstructed Fort Necessity: the round stockade and earthworks (the low grassy mounds) on the 1754 footprint and location, per the NPS place record, a 300-yard paved walk from the visitor center
- The Great Meadows battlefield itself, reestablished to approximate the 1754 landscape (NPS place record and Cultural Landscapes Inventory)
- Remnants of the original earthworks Washington's men built, per the NPS Fort Necessity article
- Mount Washington Tavern, the National Road stagecoach tavern on the hill overlooking the fort, furnished to reflect the road's mid-1800s heyday (NPS)
- Braddock's Grave, about 1 mile west of the visitor center off U.S. Route 40, marked by the 1913 monument (NPS)
- Jumonville Glen, approximately 7 miles from the visitor center — the secluded rocky bluff where the May 28, 1754 skirmish was fought, open seasonally (NPS)
- The trace of the Braddock Road, the military road built and used by Washington in 1754 and improved by Braddock in 1755 (NPS Cultural Landscapes Inventory)
- The Fort Necessity/National Road Interpretive and Education Center, with its orientation film and exhibits on the 1754 campaign and the National Road (NPS)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Interpretive and Education Center:** Start where the NPS starts visitors — the orientation film and the exhibits on the 1754 campaign and the National Road, in the center the NPS keeps open 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m.
2. **On the paved trail, 300 yards:** Walk from the visitor center to the fort, crossing the Great Meadows on the route the park itself lays out.
3. **At the reconstructed fort:** Stand inside a 53-foot circle and reckon with the tree lines — the distance that decided July 3, 1754 — and with the earthworks outside the stockade.
4. **On the hill:** Mount Washington Tavern, the stagecoach stop that the road Washington cut eventually made necessary, named for the man who once owned the land.
5. **The separate units, as the NPS shapes them:** Braddock's Grave, 1 mile west off U.S. Route 40, and Jumonville Glen, approximately 7 miles away, where the campaign's first shots were fired on May 28, 1754. Each is its own ground, with its own record.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Jumonville Glen is a disputed event in the NPS's own telling, and it is printed here as a dispute. The NPS states that accounts of the skirmish vary greatly, that no one really knows who fired the first shot, and that the French considered Jumonville's death the assassination of an envoy while the British accounts differ; the surrender document Washington signed at Fort Necessity carried the word "assassination" for Jumonville's death, and Washington maintained his translation did not use the term. The NPS's own pages also count the French loss differently: the historyculture page gives 13 Frenchmen dead and 21 captured, while the NPS place page phrases the same morning as 17 of the French dead or wounded with the rest surrendering. Both phrasings are carried as their pages state them.

Mount Washington Tavern's building date conflicts inside NPS sources: the NPS Fort Necessity article says the tavern was built in the 1830s and operated until 1855, while the NPS Historical Handbook text for the park says Judge Nathaniel Ewing erected the house beside the new road in 1818, about the time the National Road opened through the area. This draft prints both and resolves neither.

The fort's shape was a live dispute for over a century — triangular versus diamond in the literature, diamond in the 1901 study and the 1932 reconstruction — until the 1952–53 archaeology established the circle. That dispute is closed by the NPS's archaeological record, and is printed as history, not as an open question.

No NRHP nomination text for this site was consulted: the NPS National Register record consulted is the NRIS asset record (66000664), which carries the listing date, criteria, and location line but no coordinates and no narrative.
