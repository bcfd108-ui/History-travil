## 2. Story

Leslie Vercher’s grandfather John trained horses for the Hertzog family at Magnolia. In a January 28, 2015 interview the interviewer first suggested that Leslie’s father had trained the horses. Leslie redirected the account toward John. He explained how skilled work ran in the family: “Both of my grandfathers did.” The printed conversation also has him discussing his family’s time as sharecroppers at Melrose. The NPS introduction says he lived at both Magnolia and Melrose as a child and that descendants of John's family were the last to leave the Magnolia quarters. Those are separate pieces of the account. [5]

The quarters continued to hold families long after emancipation. In its ethnography of Magnolia, NPS researcher Muriel Crespi describes several labor arrangements and cites the plantation’s 1957 work book. Short-term field work drew people from families in the quarters and the surrounding area. The work book tracks labor and hours. A worker quoted in the study remembered when a day began: “you would be going to the fields when the sunrise.” The worker is unnamed in the passage. These sources describe work from different positions. A payroll book can record an hour. The interview tells what those hours felt like to someone who still had to walk home. [5–6]

At the federal unit, the quarters, store and gin buildings bring home different parts of one economy. People lived in the cabins, bargained for or bought supplies, grew and picked cotton, and moved it through the gin. The nomination records a larger historic property. NPS’s public unit covers only part of that property. The present-day boundary allows a reader to follow the workers’ story while keeping the separately held house and any closed ground outside the visit. [2–4, 6–9]

## 3. Then and now

Then — Labor in the fields, the horse-training work the Verchers remembered and the work at the gin involved different skills and family responsibilities. The quarters records and 1957 work book show why the story continues well beyond emancipation. [5–9]

Now — Begin at the federal parking area and store. The surviving cabins and store help illustrate that history. The 2002 HABS image below documents one cabin. A verified same-view present-day image is absent. [2–4, 7, 10–11]

## 5. Public progression

Follow the NPS road directions to the Magnolia unit. Its signed arrival and the store information point connect to public interior interpretation when open. Take the posted inland route toward the quarters only as conditions and signs allow. During a road or park closure, use the depot during its operating hours for a separate public exhibit visit or read remotely. [1–4, 13]

**Strongest story moment — The walk home from the cotton field.** From the lawful cabin exterior path, return to the worker’s short recollection of the long harvest day and walk home. Pause without reenacting or seeking any closed ground. [2, 6]

- The compendium says daily except federal holidays. Use the narrower Wednesday–Sunday schedule given by the NPS visitor-hours and guided-tours pages. - The interviewer guessed that Leslie Vercher’s father trained horses at Magnolia. Vercher identified his grandfather John and also mentioned his maternal grandfather’s horse training. The interview’s opening describes life on both plantations, while the particular sharecropping exchange refers to Melrose. The allocation of exact fields or labor contracts for each family member at Magnolia remains open. [5]
