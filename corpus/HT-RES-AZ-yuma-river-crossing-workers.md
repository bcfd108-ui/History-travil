## 2. The story
**Joseph C. Ives's personally authored 1861 report** supplies a contemporary observer's account, not a worker's oral history. In the narrative surrounding arrival at Fort Yuma, he describes the work supporting steam transport: people cutting fuel wood, repair delays, and a small settlement with a blacksmith's shop. Later he describes the crew's physical burden and alternating work/rest watches. The phrase **“the labor ... is by no means small”** is an excerpt with an explicit omission, not an uninterrupted quotation. [3, printed pp. 41–43, 51–52]

The report names **Captain Robinson** and **Mr. Carroll**, but they do not speak for themselves in these passages. The primary evidence establishes what Ives observed and chose to record. It does not provide payrolls, every worker's name, or their own assessment of conditions. Relevant OCR passages were directly read; the entire volume and original manuscript were not examined. [3]

The central idea is simple: a line on a historical transport map hides the repeated human work needed to keep goods moving. Interpretation should reveal that work without teaching historical navigation or inviting visitors to reproduce dangerous tasks.

## 3. Then / now
The park operator dates the former Yuma Quartermaster Depot's Army supply function to **1865–1883**, describing goods transferred between ocean transport, river steamboats and mule-drawn wagons. Ives's earlier account belongs to the preceding transport world, not to a claim that he watched this later depot operate. [2]

The site's subsequent uses and present river interpretation are separate layers. Nothing here recommends visiting or inspecting water infrastructure. Today's museum is the lawful interpretive anchor, not evidence that all surrounding historical features are public attractions. [2]

The military supply story also belongs to expansion and conflict on Indigenous homelands. An Army author's viewpoint is not a substitute for published Tribal accounts. His racialized and derogatory language is not adopted as this dossier's narrative voice; it is a source limitation, not neutral description. [3]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- A worker-authored diary is cataloged but not yet read. The present draft satisfies the personally authored contemporary-observer branch; **direct worker testimony remains an explicit research gap**. [3,4]
- The University of Arizona indexes the ferryman diary as a **1928 publication** covering **1855–1857**. The publication date is not the diary's event date. [6]
- A different Jaeger collection, **AZ 133**, concerns an **1849** voyage, boarding-house operation and carpentry. Its page also carries an image caption dated 1892. Do not substitute that collection for the Yuma ferry diary or silently reconcile the caption. [7]

- OCR errors are possible. The short labor excerpt should be checked against the page image before public publication. No longer transcription is offered as a verified facsimile.
