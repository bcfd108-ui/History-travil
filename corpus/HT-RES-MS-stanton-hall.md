# Stanton Hall — the Natchez tradespeople behind the painted oak

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP02 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Use the guided-tour entrance at 401 High Street, Natchez. The individual ticket lists hourly starts from 9 a.m. to 3 p.m. Allow roughly an hour. Check the date-specific ticket if traveling on a holiday or during severe weather. The neighboring block is outside the visitor boundary. [1–2]

## 2. Story

Stand in the center hall and imagine pricing the doors as solid oak. The April 5, 1858 Mississippi Free Trader assigned the painted oak grain to John Wells and his crew. The reporter also credited Charles Reynolds and James Brown’s masonry, Price and Polkinghorne’s plaster, and foreman John A. Saunders’s carved wood. It called Saunders’s pattern source “Lefevre’s” models, a reference architectural historians identify with Minard Lafever’s design books. The newspaper survives here in a later transcription. [2–3]

Frederick Stanton earned money from cotton as a planter and commission merchant. He and Hulda Stanton called the house Belfast. Imported pieces were fitted into a building made by Natchez tradespeople. The paper places the marble work in New York and the source of the mirrors in France. Behind the entertaining rooms an attached wing housed a kitchen, pantry and quarters for enslaved household workers. A long list of contractors made the paper. The paper gives no comparable accounting for the people who cooked, carried, or cleaned. A 1939 letter between Stanton granddaughters does preserve one woman’s name: Catherine White. Historic Natchez Foundation researchers traced her in additional records that described her work as a companion. The letter comes from the family that enslaved her; her own account remains open. [4] [2–3]

## 3. Then and now

The house acquired its present name during its period as a girls’ college. The Pilgrimage Garden Club purchased it in 1938. Much of the main interior survives, while the service wing is substantially shorter than in the Stantons’ day. Its stable survives under separate ownership. [2–3]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Guided rooms — center hall, carved and painted finishes wherever included on the day’s tour. Documented fabric — original lighting fixtures survive, with the maker attribution qualified. The surviving kitchen and pantry help explain the dining room’s placement, but guest access to service rooms requires the guide’s permission. The stable is outside the tour. [1–3]

## 5. Public progression

Start with the plastered masonry of the exterior. Inside, follow the line where wood has been carved, then painted to resemble a more costly material. **Strongest story moment:** the guide can point out the graining, and the 1858 record lets you put a local painter’s name, John Wells, beside the work. Read the dining room in relation to the people working beyond it. [2–3]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

1857 is the usual building date and April 1858 is the dated announcement of completion. Thomas Rose has strong documentary support as builder and designer. The 1858 report assigns supervision to Rose, and the architectural historians who studied its fabric identify Rose as designer. A signed plan or original payment was not examined here. The newspaper was read in transcription. The original 1939 letter and White’s own testimony remain to be located. [2–4]
