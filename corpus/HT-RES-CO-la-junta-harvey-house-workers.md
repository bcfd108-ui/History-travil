## 2. The story

**Named firsthand witness: Delbert “D. K.” Spencer**, author of the published reminiscence *Fred Harvey Hotel – La Junta, Colorado, 1941–43*, within the Santa Fe Railway Historical and Modeling Society's compilation. PDF physical pages 2–7 were read. He recalls joining the hotel's storeroom staff as a teenager, and remembers the waitresses treating him like a younger brother. He identifies **June**, daughter of baker **Ernest Stangl**, as a lunchroom waitress. This is a named coworker's recollection, **not June's own testimony**; no female first-person account was established. [3]

His brief statement, **“I learned a lot about dealing with people”**, brings the story back to hospitality as work rather than railway spectacle. [3, PDF physical p. 7]

**Original interpretive voice:** A comfortable journey is never made by a train alone. Someone has to welcome the stranger, make the meal feel ordinary, and remain courteous when the room is busy. The familiar company name can hide that work as effectively as it advertises it. Here, the useful historical question is not whether the traveler had a good dinner. It is whose working day made that dinner possible, and which of those people has been allowed to speak in the surviving record.

Spencer supplies a local eyewitness, but he cannot supply the women's interior lives. Do not convert his affection or the company's public image into evidence of universal satisfaction, fair treatment or equal opportunity.

## Connected ground — The Harvey Houses on the Santa Fe Line

La Junta's Harvey House, taken over in 1879, stood on the Santa Fe main line west of Florence, Kansas, and east of Winslow, Arizona, in the chain of eating houses the Fred Harvey Company ran under contract with the railroad.
