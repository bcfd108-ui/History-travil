**Original interpretive voice:** A hotel's name can outlive the names of the people who made it welcoming. Restoring those names changes the story. Hospitality becomes work done by particular women, rather than a costume, a romantic legend or a seamless company image. The most useful historical starting point is modest: a name, a documented association with a place, and a clear boundary around what that evidence does not yet tell us.

No quotation is invented. Pay and the specific working arrangements of McHood and Bass remain gaps. The story is research-led, not an assertion that all Harvey employees shared the same experience.

### The two-to-ten shift

Verna Northcott Welsh told Alison Owings she had hoped to work at a hotel desk. At La Posada she waited tables. In the published 2003 interview narrative she describes alternating shifts across a full day and night. Her own preference was plain: “I preferred the two to ten.” Notice the connection between that preference and a railroad town where meals had to meet a timetable. Early service and overnight service demanded somebody else’s sleep. Owings’s interview also publishes Welsh’s memory of the rule that staff lifted the glass to refill it, a small working detail that gives a visitor something more specific than the uniform to remember. [7]

Owings’s report then hands the wartime story to Virginia Graff. Welsh had already left by the time Graff remembers serving troop trains. Graff recalled the change in equipment and food as familiar china and silver disappeared under wartime volume. The timing accounts belong to distinct women and circumstances. The schedule is part of the evidence for how the service worked. [7–9]

## 3. Then / now

The Old Trails Museum interprets Winslow's railway, Harvey Girls and other local histories. It describes a collection assembled from residents' donated objects, photographs, documents, textiles and oral histories. That provides a legitimate civic setting for the story, without entering the place of employment. [1,3]

The museum itself occupies a former financial-institution building dating to 1921; it opened as a museum in July 1989. It is not claimed to be a former Harvey Girls workplace or residence. [2]

**Distinct from La Junta:** the La Junta dossier used male coworker D. K. Spencer's recollections. This entry follows Winslow women through locally specific archival identification and independently documented interview participation. Spencer's anecdotes, dates and coworkers are not transferred here. The connection is the company network, not interchangeable lives.
