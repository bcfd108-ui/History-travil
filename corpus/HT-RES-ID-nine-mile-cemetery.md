## 2. The story
The five men who died in the Nicholson adit were carried out on packhorses. Their names are on a monument at Nine Mile Cemetery outside Wallace: **an unidentified man, William Learmouth, Richard Woods, Joe Ferro, and August Berger**.

The monuments were not the institution's idea. Pulaski spent **twelve years** pressing the Forest Service for proper grave monuments for the firefighters killed in 1910. In **1921** Congress finally awarded $500, and the two cobblestone monuments went up. It is the same pattern as the rest of his record: the care came from the man, the institution followed afterward, at its own pace.

His own aftermath belongs in this entry too. *“I was blind and my hands were burned… My experience left me with poor eyes, weak lungs, and throat; but, thank God, I am not now blind.”* There was no workers' compensation for federal employees in 1910 — the Federal Employees' Compensation Act did not exist until 1916, and the plight of the 1910 firefighters is part of what forced it into being. Relief money eventually moved for the 1910 injured as a group, but Pulaski's own petitions were never granted; he paid for his own care. In 1923 he won first prize — **$500** — in *American Forestry*'s ranger story contest for the account quoted across this catalog, and by the Forest History Society's account he needed it to pay for eye surgery. A magazine contest paid to fix the eyes his government wouldn't.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** graves that went unmarked for over a decade while the man who led the crew petitioned for stones. **Now:** the two cobblestone monuments stand at Nine Mile Cemetery, and the Wallace 1910 Fire Memorial is itself on the National Register (#84001180).

## 4. What's still there

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Timothy Egan's The Big Burn recounts that colleagues' donations helped cover Pulaski's treatment after the Service refused; that detail rests on Egan's reporting rather than a primary document. The central fact — no compensation, ever — is stated by the Forest History Society and conceded by the Forest Service itself in its NWCG training guide.
