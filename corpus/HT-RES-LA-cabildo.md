# The Cabildo — Spanish government house, Louisiana Purchase transfer, and the Sala Capitular
HT-RES-LA-cabildo · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The ground was set aside before the building. In the plan of the new town of New Orleans laid out in 1721 by the French military engineer Adrien de Pauger, the site flanking the public square was reserved for government use, and a corps de garde — police station and guardhouse — was begun there in 1729, one of the first all-brick structures in New Orleans. After thirty years it was replaced by a building designed by Ignace Broutin and Bernard Deverges; parts of its walls are incorporated in the present Cabildo. The site has therefore been a government and guard site, continuously and in one fabric, for nearly three centuries, with the older French masonry still inside the walls. [3]

Spanish rule renamed both the body and the building. On August 18, 1769, Don Alejandro O'Reilly took formal possession of Louisiana for Spain, and one of his first acts was to establish a Spanish cabildo — a municipal council — for the government of the city, replacing the French Superior Council, and to order a new town hall on the site of the prison of the corps de garde. The nomination quotes the word plainly: the name is derived from the Spanish municipal governing body set up in 1769. The new town hall, built on the foundations of the old structure in brick-between-post (colombage) construction rather than masonry, had begun to decay by 1787, just as plans for its replacement were under way — and then the city burned. The Great New Orleans Fire of March 21, 1788, Good Friday, destroyed the old Cabildo and much of the town; a corps de garde, prison and jailer's quarters rebuilt on the site were burned again in the fire of 1794. Between the fires the council met first in the Government House, which had escaped, and then in a rented house. [3, 5]

The rebuild was paid for by one man. Don Andrés Almonester y Rojas, a wealthy Spanish nobleman, offered and was chosen to rebuild the Cabildo — having already agreed, after the 1788 fire, to rebuild the Cathedral and the Presbytère. The plans for all three were drawn by Don Gilberto Guillemard, a Frenchman by birth long in Spain's military service, who had designed the cathedral and the Presbytère after the fire of 1788 and was selected to draw the new Cabildo as well. Guillemard reused as much as possible of the old brickwork left on the site — substantial remains of the massive walls of the old French corps de garde of the 1750s were built into the new structure, and the 1966–1969 restoration research confirmed beyond doubt that most of those old walls survived both fires and were incorporated in the present building, with the brick-on-edge floor of the French colonial structure found under layers of later flooring. Work began on December 4, 1795, under Guillemard's supervision, and the building was sufficiently completed for the Cabildo to occupy it on May 10, 1799. [3, 5]

What Guillemard built was a two-story stuccoed-brick town hall with a flat, balustraded tile roof topped by urns. The ground floor was an arcaded open gallery of slightly elliptical arches springing from square piers, with two Doric pilasters at the corners; the upper story was of similar design with Ionic pilasters between glazed arches. A central pediment crowned the center three bays, where engaged columns on both levels mark the entrance bay. Inside, two long rooms separated by a heavy wall with five arches served the guard; above, the Sala Capitular — the Meeting Room, the council chamber — occupied the new second-floor space, its window spacing set by the five windows of the old wall along St. Peter Street. The wrought iron of the upper-gallery and Sala Capitular window railings was made, most if not all of it, by Marcelino Hernández, a skilled craftsman from the Canary Islands; the nomination calls it perhaps the finest Spanish colonial wrought iron to be found in New Orleans. The pediment once held the arms of the Spanish crown; they were removed in 1804 and replaced with an eagle amidst trophies, arms and flags — the nomination dates the design to 1822, by Pietro Cardelli, who had worked on the United States Capitol, while the architectural historians' encyclopedia gives 1821. The steep mansard roof with scrolled dormers and cupola that gives the building its familiar third story was added in 1847 under city surveyor Louis Surgi; in 1850 the building was refinished with brown stucco and new wrought-iron entrance gates that, the nomination notes, were still in place in 1975. [3, 4]

Two transfer ceremonies in twenty days made this the room in which the map of America changed. The Louisiana Purchase treaty had been concluded in Paris in the spring of 1803, and the Senate ratified it on October 20 by a vote of 24 to 7. Spain, which had held Louisiana since 1762, formally returned it to France in the Sala Capitular on November 30, 1803: the Spanish commissioners handed the French commissioner Pierre-Clément de Laussat the keys of the forts, the Spanish flag came down in the square and the French flag went up, and for twenty days the building was known as the Maison de Ville, the Town Hall. On December 20, 1803, Laussat transferred Louisiana to the United States in the same room, and the French flag gave way to the American flag on the square, received by William C. C. Claiborne and James Wilkinson. The National Park Service's account states it without ornament: on Tuesday, December 20, 1803, in the Cabildo, the Louisiana Territory was officially transferred from France to the United States — and the nation doubled in size. The people living in that territory — French, Spanish, free people of color, enslaved people, and the Native peoples whose land it was — were transferred with it, without being asked. Claiborne's September proclamation, issued before the transfer, promised the inhabitants security "of your liberty, your property, and the religion of your choice," a promise the building's later history tests in the same room. [1, 3, 6]

The building stayed the center of civic life. United States troops were housed on the lower floor until 1805, when the mayor had them withdrawn and replaced by local police. By 1806 the Cabildo housed the mayor, city council, superior court and its clerk, the county judge and the city notary; in 1809, when the charity hospital burned, the upper gallery served as an emergency hospital; in the 1830s the rooms were a banquet hall for the Grenadiers and other companies of the Louisiana Legion; and through the years it housed libraries — the New Orleans Library Association in 1819, the Law Association Library from 1847 to 1910, and a public school library from 1857. The architectural historians' account gives the city-hall sequence with a nuance the museum's shorthand smooths over: the building served as New Orleans's city hall from 1803, and after the city was divided into three municipalities in 1836 it served as the municipal hall for the Creole population, until the city's reunification in 1852. The museum's own history says the Cabildo served as the center of New Orleans government until 1853; the difference is a matter of which government the sentence means, and it is printed in the gaps below rather than harmonized. When the Marquis de Lafayette visited New Orleans in 1825, the city gave him the use of the Sala Capitular as his residence, and the room's elaborate mantel was removed in the remodeling for him — the nomination notes it was then considered clumsy and old-fashioned. [3, 4, 5]

In 1853 the Louisiana Supreme Court moved into the Cabildo, after thirty years in the Presbytère, and the Sala Capitular became the courtroom, where five justices sat. The court's tenancy is the building's second national story, and it is the one that tests Claiborne's promise. The Law Library of Louisiana and University of New Orleans account, published in the New Orleans Historical project, names three lines of cases argued there: the Myra Clark Gaines cases, the longest continuous litigation in United States history, turning on women's property rights and inheritance; the Slaughter-House Cases, deciding the rights of private butchers to work their trade; and Plessy v. Ferguson, in which Homer Plessy, a mixed-race resident of New Orleans, challenged the legality of racially separated railroad cars under Louisiana's Separate Car Act of 1890. The museum's history says the Plessy decision originated in the Cabildo in 1892 — the Louisiana phase, heard in the Sala Capitular by the state's court. The final judgment of 1896 was the United States Supreme Court's, in Washington, establishing the "separate but equal" doctrine that stood until Brown v. Board of Education overturned it in 1954. The same room that received the territory for the United States thus also received, ninety years later, the legal argument that would structure American segregation for sixty years. This dossier states both halves in the same breath, in the building, without euphemism. The court remained in the Cabildo until 1910. On May 7, 2012, the justices of the Louisiana Supreme Court returned to the Sala Capitular for a day to hear oral arguments, the first time the court had sat in the Cabildo since 1910 — a ceremony marking the bicentennial of Louisiana statehood and of the court's establishment. [1, 4, 5]

The 1870s put the building under fire. The same court-history account records that in that decade the Cabildo came under gunfire on three separate occasions, all the product of Reconstruction-era politics and racial tension. No incident in that sentence is dated in the source inspected, and this dossier does not add dates to it. The building was transferred to the Louisiana State Museum in 1908, the court moving to a new building, and the Cabildo opened as the museum in 1911; the lower floor, which had housed the police station and the Second City Criminal Court, was not turned over to the museum until 1914. [3, 4, 5]

Two restorations define the building a visitor stands in. The first followed research begun in 1966, with Maxwell and Le Breton as architects and Richard Koch and Samuel Wilson, Jr., as associates. It confirmed the survival of the French corps de garde walls and floor, restored the second-floor rooms to their appearance at the time of the Louisiana Transfer in 1803 — including the gallery doors and their over-door pediments, the surviving example sculptured by Cristobal Le Prevost in 1799 — and was completed in June 1969, the museum reopening on April 30, 1970, the anniversary of the signing of the Louisiana Purchase Treaty. The second followed the fire of 1988, which destroyed the third floor and the cupola. The State Museum's account states that the landmark was severely damaged and, within five years, authentically restored using 600-year-old French timber-framing techniques, reopening to the public in 1994; the architectural historians' account confirms the fire destroyed the third floor and that Koch and Wilson, Architects, restored it matching the old heavy-timber construction, replicating the buff-tan exterior color used in 1847. The exact day of the 1988 fire is widely given as May 11, but no primary source for that day was retrieved in this pass and this dossier does not state it. [1, 3, 4]

Today the three floors carry the museum's exhibitions of Louisiana history, culture and global connections: *Gálvez and Louisiana in the American Revolution*, on Louisiana's role in the fight for American independence; *Bienvenue Lafayette*, on the Marquis's enduring connection to Louisiana; *People & Power: Building History at the Cabildo*, on the people, politics and events that shaped the building's legacy; *The Battle of New Orleans*; and *Sportsman's Paradise*, a waterfowling exhibition supported by Ducks Unlimited. A Calas Café residency in the museum offers the Creole calas rice fritters once sold in the French Quarter by African-descended street vendors, Thursday–Sunday 11:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m. The building is listed in the National Register as a National Historic Landmark, with its individual reference number 66000373, and as a contributing property of the Vieux Carré Historic District. [1, 2]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The government site on Jackson Square (the Place d'Armes) in New Orleans' French Quarter, Orleans Parish — set aside for government use in the 1721 town plan, occupied by the corps de garde from 1729, by the Spanish cabildo's town hall from the 1770s, and by the present building since 1795–1799. The Cabildo stands at 701 Chartres Street at St. Peter Street, flanking St. Louis Cathedral on its left, with the Presbytère on the cathedral's right. [1, 3, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The Cabildo, Louisiana State Museum, 701 Chartres Street, New Orleans, LA 70116. Use the public museum entrance on Chartres Street and the museum's three exhibition floors, including the restored Sala Capitular on the second floor. Jackson Square itself is public ground in front of the building and is part of the same visit, as the transfer ceremonies spilled from the balcony onto the square. [1, 2, 5]

## 5. Public progression

Enter from Chartres Street and stand in the arcade first — the arches are the oldest public gesture of the building, and the second-floor windows above them set the spacing of the Sala Capitular. Go to the second floor and give the Sala Capitular the time it needs: read it as the room where, on November 30, 1803, the keys of the forts changed hands from Spain to France, and where, on December 20, the territory passed to the United States — and where, ninety years later, the state's supreme court heard the argument that would become Plessy v. Ferguson. Take the exhibitions floor by floor — *People & Power* for the building's own biography, *Gálvez* and *Bienvenue Lafayette* for the alliances and visits that moved through it, *The Battle of New Orleans* for the war that followed the Purchase, *Sportsman's Paradise* for the wetlands economy that underlies the state. Finish on the square, facing the building, and note the pediment and the mansard as a ledger: Spanish crown removed, American eagle added, French roof laid over a Spanish house. **Strongest story moment · The Sala Capitular.** One room received an empire, handed it on, and then heard the case that defined who could sit where on a train. Stand in it long enough to hold both transfers and the segregation argument in the same space — the building's claim to national significance is that it contains the contradiction, not that it resolves it. [1–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Address conflict.** The current museum address is 701 Chartres Street. The 1975 nomination form lists 709 Chartres Street for the property and 751 Chartres Street — the Presbytère's address — as the museum's mailing address. The present 701 address governs for the visit; the older numbering is printed so a reader matching old records is not misled. [1, 3]
- **City-hall chronology conflict.** The museum's history says the Cabildo served as the center of New Orleans government until 1853, when it became the Supreme Court's headquarters. The architectural historians' account says it served as city hall from 1803 to 1836, then as the municipal hall for the Creole population after the city split into three municipalities, until reunification in 1852. Both are printed; the nomination's fuller sequence — mayor, council, superior court, county judge and notary all in the building by 1806, with multiple public offices continuing until the museum took over in 1911 — supports the shorter statement being shorthand for the building's civic primacy rather than a single unbroken city-hall tenure. [1, 3, 4]
- **Supreme Court dates.** The museum says the Cabildo became the Supreme Court's headquarters in 1853 and, in the same history, that the Plessy decision "originated" in 1892; the Law Library account dates the court's tenancy 1853–1910 and places Plessy among the cases argued in the Sala Capitular, with the United States Supreme Court deciding it in 1896. Secondary summaries sometimes give the tenancy as 1868–1910; that variant was not verified and is not followed. The Louisiana phase of Plessy is 1892 in the museum's wording; the federal decision is 1896. [1, 4, 5]
- **Eagle pediment date.** The nomination dates Cardelli's eagle design to 1822; the architectural historians' encyclopedia gives 1821. Unresolved by one year; the sculpture is the same object either way. [3, 4]
- **Museum transfer date.** The State Museum's history says the building was transferred to the Louisiana State Museum in 1908; the nomination says it became the museum in 1911, with the lower floor not turned over until 1914, and the architectural historians' account gives 1911 as the opening. The 1908 transfer and the 1911 opening are compatible as stages of one handover, but no source inspected ties them into a single sequence, so both dates are printed. [1, 3, 4]
- **National Historic Landmark date.** The National Register record gives the entry date as October 15, 1966, with reference number 66000373 and significant years 1795, 1803 and 1847. Landmark status in 1960 is stated in secondary summaries, with the day October 9 widely published, but the designation day was not verified against the NPS Landmark record in this pass and is not printed as a verified date. [2]
- **Fire day of 1988.** The fire that destroyed the third floor and cupola is dated only to 1988 in the sources inspected here; the commonly published day, May 11, was not verified from a primary or agency source in this pass and is not stated. [1, 4]
- **Demolition-threat story.** Secondary summaries state that in 1895 the building was proposed for demolition and that artist William Woodward led a successful preservation campaign. No primary or museum source for that campaign was retrieved in this pass, and the episode is not stated as fact in the story above; it remains a lead for a future pass. [1]
- **Reconstruction gunfire.** The court-history account records three occasions of gunfire at the Cabildo in the 1870s, arising from Reconstruction-era politics and racial tension, without dates or incidents in the text inspected. No incident-level account is given here. [5]
- **Haunted lore.** Tourism and social-media copy calls the Cabildo one of the French Quarter's most haunted sites and repeats a story of a hanged British spy. No contemporary source for either claim was retrieved in this pass; the lore is recorded here only to be kept out of the documented record. It has no place in the museum progression. [5]
