## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** Moving a household is not just replacing a building. It can break the everyday geography through which people work, visit, help each other and remain a community.

**Named sovereign voice actually read:** **Marcus Dominic Wells Jr.**, identified in the hearing as chairman of the Three Affiliated Tribes of the Fort Berthold Reservation, testified before the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs on **November 1, 2007**. On printed page **60**, he described families moved from fertile bottomlands to uplands previously used for grazing. His short statement, **“The result was not by our own choosing,”** rejects the framing of voluntary relocation. He connected dispossession to agricultural loss and fragmented community services. This is official public testimony read from the hearing text, not a household diary or a recording heard here. [4]

**Sovereign historical context:** MHA Nation's own history describes the disassembly of Elbowoods and the scattering of families rather than re-creation of their small village communities. The Nation's account must not be reduced to a federal project's engineering timeline. [5]

**Continuing community voice:** the publicly published *Our River, Our Home* page attributes to **Monica Mayer**, identified there as a Tribal councilmember, a statement grounding identity in the land and river. This is a published sovereignty position, not an invitation to visit a particular location or a ruling on a current legal dispute. [6]

**Original interpretation:** A household's losses include relationships and ways of living that cannot be counted by acreage alone. Center the people describing those losses. Do not turn their account into a scenic reservoir story, and do not end it as if the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara ceased to exist when their homes were taken.

## 3. Then / now

The Nation's history describes relocation as complete by fall **1954**. That date concerns relocation, not every stage of construction, water rise, compensation or later claims. [5]

The 2007 hearing is a later official account of continuing consequences. It is not a statement made at the moment of household removal. The sovereign history and modern public cultural center demonstrate ongoing community interpretation rather than a people confined to the past. [2,4,5]

This draft does not claim to resolve compensation, treaty interpretation or present litigation. It uses published voices to show how people describe the consequences, with their institutional roles and dates intact.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Before travel:** begin with the chairman's distinction between imposed relocation and choice. Read the date and role along with the statement, so testimony is not presented as an anonymous caption.

**At the cultural center, only if the specific gate is confirmed:** follow the visitor areas the operator has opened. Let the Nation's own presentation set the terms. No private archive, event, ceremonial space or neighboring community area is included by implication.

**Deepen:** compare what a numerical project summary counts with what the Nation describes as community loss. Do not make a numerical discrepancy into a quiz or demand that the visitor decide whose suffering counts.

**Strongest closing moment:** return to the meaning of home as a web of relationships, not an interchangeable structure. End with ongoing sovereignty and the community's authority to tell its own history. No lake overlook, submerged-location search or industrial tour is needed to complete the encounter.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

1. **Counts remain attributed:** Wells's spoken testimony describes **156,000 acres** and bottomlands that were home to **over 90 percent** of members. The Nation's public campaign describes **90 percent forced to relocate**. Those are related but not identical measures. [4,6]
2. **Different published figures:** North Dakota Studies gives **152,360 acres**, **325 families** and **approximately 80 percent** of membership, citing historical scholarship. This is state educational synthesis, not a separately examined federal household census. No blended “correct” count is invented. [7]
3. **Source identity matters:** the hearing is federally published, but Wells speaks as Tribal chairman. Conversely, a state curriculum's summary is not automatically the Nation's own statement. The numbers' underlying denominators and counting dates remain unresolved.
4. **Firsthand boundary:** no named displaced individual's full household oral history was read. This dossier meets the sovereign-official-voice branch of the assignment and must not be described as a survivor interview collection.
5. **Dates:** agreement, legislative taking, construction, filling and relocation dates describe different actions. The hearing date and publication date also differ. Do not flatten them into one anniversary.
6. **Access and rights:** exact winter visit, public display content and photographic permission remain unverified. No permission to enter sensitive ground or reproduce culturally significant material is inferred.
