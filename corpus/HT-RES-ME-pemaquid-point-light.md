# HT-RES-ME-pemaquid-point-light — The Light on the Maine Quarter

Division ME seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

At the end of Bristol Road in Bristol, Maine, a working light stands on folded rock ledges at the entrance to Muscongus Bay. The station was established in 1827 under President John Quincy Adams; the tower that stands today is the 1835 replacement for the first, failed tower; the keeper's house of 1857 holds the Fishermen's Museum; and the Town of Bristol runs the park around all of it. In 2003 the Mint put this tower on the Maine state quarter.

## 1. Trip gate

Pemaquid Point Lighthouse Park is run and maintained by the Bristol Parks and Recreation Department. The park address is 3115 Bristol Road, New Harbor. The town's park page gives the season as the middle of May until mid to late October — the closing date varies each year — open 9 am to 5 pm in season.

Entrance fees, as the town's page states them: adults and children 12 and older, $4; children 5–11, $1; children 4 and under, free; Bristol taxpayers and residents, free with a renewed annual park pass. Admission includes the Fishermen's Museum, the Art Gallery, and the Learning Center. The Parks and Recreation page adds: at 5:00 pm all of the buildings and the restrooms close, but visitors may still walk the grounds, and the park itself closes only for extreme weather. The town's tobacco-free parks policy dates to May 4, 2012; public restrooms are on site.

The tower is a separate operation inside the park, operated by Friends of Pemaquid Point Lighthouse and staffed by volunteer docents. The town's park page has the tower open for climbing, weather permitting, from Memorial weekend until early October, and asks visitors to call ahead to check availability; the Parks and Recreation page gives the climbing hours as daily, 11 am to 5 pm, depending on weather. A $1 suggested donation, collected at the tower by the Friends, covers the climb. The height rule is the town's own wording: "Please note — there is a 46 inch height requirement to climb the tower when it is open. This is for safety reasons and will not be waived."

The rocks below the light are the park's other attraction and its standing hazard, in the town's printed park brochure's words: "For the safety of our visitors, please stay off rocks when there is high surf. There is a very real danger of being washed off the rocks if you walk too close to the water. The lower rocks are slippery, and even though the sea may not appear rough, an occasional rogue wave unexpectedly reaches higher points on the rocks. PEOPLE HAVE LOST THEIR LIVES HERE FROM THESE WAVES!!" The same brochure adds, in capitals, "BEWARE OF POISON IVY NEAR THE ROCKS!!" Its park rules allow dogs on a leash and prohibit drones and vending.

## 2. The story

The point divides two bays. The National Register nomination calls Pemaquid Point Light an important navigational mark between the entrance to Johns Bay and Muscongus Bay, set on a high promontory above the long ledge running southwesterly from its base. In spite of the light, the nomination records, the point has been the scene of a number of tragic shipwrecks owing to that ledge. The ground was a lookout before it was a light station: on September 5, 1813, hundreds of people stood here to watch the single-ship action between the British brig Boxer and the United States brig Enterprise, in which the Boxer was defeated.

The United States Government established the original Pemaquid Point Lighthouse in 1827. The Coast Guard Historian's fact sheet records it directly: President John Quincy Adams ordered the original structure built in 1827. The sheet's keeper list begins with Isaac Dunham, appointed 3 November 1827. The original lens was Argand lamps.

The first tower failed fast. The Penobscot Marine Museum's catalog record for the station recounts why: salt water was used in the mortar mix, the workmanship was poor, and the lighthouse began to crumble. It was replaced in 1835. The second contract stipulated that only fresh water be used, and Keeper Isaac Dunham oversaw the construction; Dunham reported to the U.S. Lighthouse Establishment that the agreement was upheld. The Coast Guard fact sheet's data field agrees: "Year Current Tower(s) First Lit: 1835." The tower that stands today is the 1835 tower — conical, of rubble stone, white with a black lantern, 34 feet high, exhibiting its light at 79 feet above sea level.

In 1857 the station was rebuilt around the tower: the keeper's house was built that year — the register carries 1857 as a significant year, and the Penobscot Marine Museum's catalog record states the date directly. The wood-frame house replaced the original stone dwelling and stands beside the tower now.

The working station accreted its outbuildings in the 1890s. The Fishermen's Museum's own account records that the light burned sperm oil for a time, and that in 1896 a brick oil house was built for the lamp oil. In 1897, by the museum's account, a brick engine house was built on the south side of the tower for a hand-operated fog bell: in fog the keeper wound the Stevens machine to raise the weights, and the bell rang at regular intervals for six to eight hours as they descended. The fog bell was removed in the 1930s — the museum dates the Coast Guard's removal of the original bell to 1937 — and a smaller replica bell, donated by the Coast Guard, now hangs off the brick bell house.

Eleven keepers held the light. The Coast Guard's list runs from Dunham (1827) through Gammage, Curtis, Tibbetts, Fossett, and Lawler to Marcus A. Hanna, appointed 30 July 1869 — the only man ever awarded both the Medal of Honor and the Gold Lifesaving Medal — and on to Leroy S. Ewell, appointed 1 July 1922. The keeper era ended on October 1, 1934, when the station was automated — the Fishermen's Museum dates it and notes the light was then fueled by gas. The Coast Guard's sheet likewise records automation in 1934.

The ground around the tower is now the town's. The tower itself stayed federal — Coast Guard property, leased to the American Lighthouse Foundation and maintained by the Friends, per the town's park brochure. In 1972 the Fishermen's Museum opened in the keeper's house, run by Bristol residents. In 2003 the United States Mint's Maine quarter, released June 2, 2003, carries what the Mint calls "a rendition of the Pemaquid Point Light atop a granite coast and of a schooner at sea." The tower in the design is the standing 1835 tower.

The newest chapter is storm work. In January 2024, back-to-back storms tore open the bell house — its ocean-facing wall knocked in — and the Town of Bristol rebuilt it over the following months, salvaging about two-thirds of the original bricks, completed by September 2024. The museum records the same buildings heavily damaged by two storms in 1991 and restored the following year.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the station was a household that worked a light. A keeper and his family lived in the house attached to the tower's work: oil — sperm oil for a time, by the museum's account — stored in the brick oil house, lamps lit at dark, and in fog the keeper winding the Stevens machine so the bell would ring for six to eight hours while the weights descended.

Now, the light still works — an automated aid to navigation, its fourth-order Fresnel lens turning out the station's signal with no keeper in the house — but everything around it has changed hands. The keeper's house is a museum run by the town's own residents. The tower is climbed by visitors under volunteer docents, for a dollar donation and a 46-inch height rule. The point is a municipal park with an art gallery and a learning center. And since 2003 the view has been currency: the Mint's rendition made this tower the Maine lighthouse most Americans have handled. What has not changed is the ground — the folded ledges still run a third of a mile into the sea, the surf still reaches higher than it looks, and the town still prints its warning in capital letters.

## 4. What's still there

The 1835 tower stands and works: rubble stone, conical, white with a black lantern, 34 feet of tower showing the light at 79 feet above the sea; the Coast Guard's fact sheet lists the station as operational. Its lantern holds a fourth-order Fresnel lens — the Fishermen's Museum displays an identical Fresnel lens in its Navigation Room, formerly of Baker Island Light, on loan from the Coast Guard.

The 1857 keeper's house stands connected to the tower, its ground floor given to the Fishermen's Museum. The museum's own gallery descriptions name its rooms: the Navigation Room, with the lens, Maine-coast lighthouse charts, and a wave-powered whistle buoy; the Fish House Room, with a "make or break" three-horsepower engine; the Net Room, with nets, traps, and models of fishing and lobster boats; and the Gallery Room, honoring Harvey Gamage, master boat builder of South Bristol. The museum also holds captains' logs and accounts of ships wrecked nearby.

The 1896 brick oil house stands, and so does the 1897 sound signal building — the brick bell house on the south side of the tower, with its weight tower and the replica fog bell hung outside it — twice storm-wrecked and twice put back, in 1991–92 and again in 2024. The park's Learning Center, opened in 2008, houses a display of the dangers of walking on the rocks. The Pemaquid Art Gallery, founded by local artists in July 1929 per the brochure, shows the Pemaquid Group of Artists. And under everything, the ledges: folded, banded rock running a third of a mile into the ocean — the granite coast of the quarter design.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Come down Bristol Road (State Route 130) to where it ends at the point; the park booth and the fee are the gate. Begin in the keeper's house, at the Fishermen's Museum, before the tower: the Navigation Room's lens — the twin of the one working above you — and the museum's wreck accounts give the station its terms: a light that exists because the ledge kept taking ships.

Take the tower when the Friends' docents have it open — weather permitting, and call ahead, as the town advises. The climb is gated by the 46-inch rule, which the town states will not be waived. From the lantern, read the mark as mariners do: Johns Bay to the west, Muscongus Bay to the east, and the ledge running out southwesterly below.

Come down and walk the working buildings in order of their building: the 1896 oil house; then the 1897 bell house, where the fog signal was wound by hand — read the replica bell and the weight tower against the museum's account of the Stevens machine, its brickwork the town's 2024 rebuilding of a wall the sea knocked in. Stop at the Learning Center's display on the dangers of walking on the rocks before going down to them.

Finish on the ledges, inside the brochure's rule — off them entirely in high surf, and high on them otherwise. From the upper ledges, looking back, the station composes itself into the Mint's rendition: tower, house, bell house, granite coast.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The federal records disagree on the tower's rebuilding year; the disagreement is printed here unresolved. The Coast Guard Historian's fact sheet carries, in its structured data, "Year Current Tower(s) First Lit: 1835." The same sheet's narrative paragraph says that "in 1857, President James Buchanan ordered the light reconstructed" — a sentence that cannot be squared with its own data field. The National Register nomination's description likewise states the station "was built originally in 1827 and rebuilt in 1857." The NPS maritime inventory, as carried in the Penobscot Marine Museum's catalog record, prints "Year first lit 1857 (current tower)" in the same record that dates the keeper's house to 1857. Section 2 follows the fact sheet's data field: 1835. The conflict stands in the sources.

The nomination's physical description is unreliable in two further particulars. It calls the light tower "square in configuration"; the Coast Guard's fact sheet records the tower shape as conical, and the tower as it stands and photographs is conical. And it describes the keeper's quarters as "also of stone," one story — the original 1827 stone dwelling, not the wood-frame house of 1857 standing today. The form (Maine Historic Preservation Commission; entered April 16, 1985) does not match the station it lists. Two smaller discrepancies ride along: the register database (NRIS 85000843) lists the area of significance as "Law," while the form itself checks "transportation"; and the database's periods (1825–1849, 1850–1874; significant years 1827 and 1857) sit beside a form description that spends 1857 on the tower.

The transfer of the land is also unsettled. The nomination's significance statement asserts that "in 1934 the keeper's house and surrounding land was given to the State and is now a state park." But 1934 is the year the light was automated — October 1, 1934, by the museum's account, confirmed by the Coast Guard's sheet — not a documented transfer date, and the park today is a municipal park of the Town of Bristol, not a state park. Secondary lighthouse histories record that in March 1940 Bristol residents voted at town meeting to purchase the property, excepting the tower, for $1,639. No town record of that vote was fetched for this dossier, so it is not asserted in §2.

The Fresnel lens's installation date could not be verified from a primary source consulted for this dossier. Secondary lighthouse histories date the fourth-order Fresnel lens to 1856. The museum confirms the working lens and displays its Baker Island twin; the Coast Guard sheet lists only the original Argand lamps. Neither dates the change. The year 1856 is therefore not printed in §2.

The bell house chronology divides on repair versus reconstruction. The Fishermen's Museum's account states that the engine house and tower were heavily damaged by two storms in 1991 and "restored by the Fishermen's Museum the following year." The Lighthouse Digest's lighthouse database describes the standing structure as a "1991 reconstruction of 1897 fog bell tower." The two are not resolved here. The January 2024 damage and the town's 2024 rebuilding rest, in this dossier, on lighthouse-press reporting — the U.S. Lighthouse Society's news service and Lighthouse Digest; no town or Coast Guard record of the 2024 work was fetched.

The park's price has moved between the town's own publications. The town's current park page charges $4 for adults and children 12 and older; the town's printed park brochure, an earlier edition, charges $3.00 for the same visitor, cash or check only. Section 1 follows the current page. The tower's hours divide the same way: the town's park page gives no clock hours, only the season and weather permitting; the Parks and Recreation page gives 11 am to 5 pm daily; an older American Lighthouse Foundation database listing gave 1 pm to 5 pm. The town's two current pages govern §1.
