# Historic Jefferson College and the meetinghouse where Mississippi wrote its constitution

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP05 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Historic Jefferson College in Washington is closed to visitors while the state rehabilitates eight buildings. The verified visitor stop for this story is in Jackson, at the Old Capitol Museum’s first-floor ‘Before the Old Capitol’ gallery. It is a separate trip and tells the statehood part of the story. The museum posts ordinary public hours Tuesday–Saturday, 9 a.m.–5 p.m. [1,2]

## 2. Story

Chartered in 1802, Jefferson College did not begin teaching until 1811. Its founders envisioned higher education in the Mississippi Territory. The school actually opened at preparatory level and never granted a bachelor’s degree. Families in Natchez and on nearby plantations did not always agree where their sons should study or where money for a college should go. Financial disputes and intermittent closures marked its history. [3]

## 3. Then and now

Delegates met in Washington in 1817, with territorial governor David Holmes presiding. The Historic American Buildings Survey identifies their meetingplace as the old Methodist church on the college grounds. They wrote and signed the state's first constitution there. The church has been demolished. Looking at a surviving college wing would therefore put the visitor in the wrong room even if the campus were open. The original constitution is preserved in the state archives. [4–6]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Representation was at stake as well as admission to the Union. The convention gave each county representation and allowed population to affect how many additional legislators it received. Political rights were restricted, including by race and sex. The institution that lent the convention its surroundings continued to change. An 1841 fire destroyed the main college building, its library and part of the territorial archives. The school closed permanently in 1964. [3,6]

## 5. Public progression

In Jackson, the first-floor exhibit compares the 1817 and 1832 constitutions. The building around the visitor opened in 1839 and was the setting for later conventions. The 1817 delegates met in Washington. This record identifies no original 1817 manuscript on permanent display. [2,5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

An older report describes occasional events and arrangements on the college grounds. MDAH’s current closure controls the visitor ruling. A state marker record locates markers between the highway and college but gives insufficient evidence of a lawful stopping place, so there is no roadside stop in this record. [1,7]
