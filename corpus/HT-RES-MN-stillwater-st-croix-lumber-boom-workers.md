## 2 · Story — Sorting an entire river

A boom was a sorting station for something too large for a building. As drives brought logs into the lower St. Croix, a marked log still belonged to a particular owner. The boom held mixed timber while workers identified marks, measured logs and directed them into the groups that would be assembled into rafts. The landmark nomination calls the people who assembled the groups “Fitting-up crews.” Each raft linked separate woods, logging outfits and mills to one stretch of water. [2–4]

The boom operator’s territorial charter allowed a charge of 40 cents per thousand board feet sorted, according to the National Park Service. This was a charge for handling, separate from a worker’s wage. At heavy flows the work reached far upstream. Peak backups are variously estimated at nine and fifteen miles in the federal sources, with different descriptions of what was being measured. The present river reach cannot be used to reconstruct an exact length. [2–4]

The National Park Service labor account documents a 1902 strike for a ten-hour day. Sawmills shut for about a week, and many rivermen and rafters joined in sympathy. The same account says around 200 men and boys remained at work at the boom. It describes a seasonal labor pool that included boys and young men helping support families, as well as workers passing through. The operative questions were who worked, how many hours and who could afford to stop. The article's numbers and identities are secondary evidence pending a source payroll or union roll. [4]

A June 17, 1914 *Stillwater Gazette* clipping, transcribed in a 2014 article, reports that Captain W. F. McGray “also hitched the last log.” It ties his first log in 1856 to the closing operation at 2:30 p.m. June 12, 1914. A separate contemporary trade report in *Commercial West* corroborates the month but does not give that hour. It printed “the river is cleared” while wondering about the river’s future commercial use. The Gazette details come from a reprint, and the *Commercial West* wording comes from the digitized July 11 issue, page 17. [5–6]

## 3 · Then / now

The sorting site and river belonged to an industrial network that extended to downriver mills. The present anchor is the DNR’s roadside wayside. It interprets the former works from public ground developed in part as a Depression-era recreation area. The designation file recounts a 1975 survey locating remnants after no traces had been known. Even verified archaeological remnants are different from maintained visitor paths. The company house and barn have a separate register record. [1–2,7]

## 5 · Trip progression

From the north lot, stop at a public interpretive panel. Read how different owners’ logs became separate rafts. Where sightlines permit, look at the broad water from the designated upper area. **Story landing — The last hitch.** Read McGray’s reported final act beside the account of crews sorting and fitting logs. If the north lot or approach is unavailable, read away from the site. [1–6]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- The July 1914 *Commercial West* item gives 13,079,000,000 feet of logs marketed from the tributary country. DNR estimates 15.5 billion feet at the Boom Site and the landmark wording says more than 15.5 billion passed through. Geographic reach and accounting units or definitions may differ. No ledger reconciles the estimates in this draft. [1–2,6]
- The landmark nomination describes stored logs across nine miles at peak; NPS’s logging overview describes a backup of 15 miles in the 1880s. Do not use either as a measured section for today. [2–3]
- DNR's longitude uses a minutes figure above 60 as printed. It is not ingested or converted. Use the signed highway approach. Current plowing, shore visibility, and day-use approach beyond the panel area were not confirmed. A search returned the separate Highway 243 bridge works, which does not establish access to this wayside. [1,9]
- Original Gazette clipping, individual payroll, and union records were not obtained. The Stillwater library newspaper inventory supplies further source leads. [4–6,10]
