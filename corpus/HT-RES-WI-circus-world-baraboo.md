# HT-RES-WI-circus-world-baraboo — A winter's worth of barns: the circus as a Baraboo industry
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

For thirty-four winters the Ringling Bros. circus came home to Baraboo and behaved like what it was: a major employer with a plant. Along Water Street the brothers built wagon shops, animal barns, a print shop, a wardrobe building, a rooming house for their help, and offices — the winter quarters where the show was housed, fed, repaired, printed, and costumed between seasons. The ground is now Circus World, a Wisconsin Historical Society site: 64 acres and 30 structures around the surviving quarters buildings, which were designated a National Historic Landmark in 1969.

## 1. Trip gate
The operator is the Wisconsin Historical Society, owner of the site, and the visit runs through Circus World, the Society's historic site on the Ringling Bros. winter quarters ground along Water Street in Baraboo. The Society's site pages describe the ground, its 64 acres, and its structures; no current hours or admission price are printed in this entry, because the Society pages checked do not state a schedule or price in a form this entry can date — Circus World operates seasonally, and its performance and exhibit calendar is the Society's to publish.

The boundary that defines this district comes from the Society's own National Register record, and it is quoted in the locator record below: "Some of the Ringling buildings are open to the public as part of the Circus World Museum, while others remain privately owned. Please respect the privacy of the owners." The winter quarters district is a living street grid — Water, Briar, Lynn, and East Streets — not a fenced park. Buildings in it that the museum does not open are private property and are not stops, however historic their fronts. The Robert L. Parkinson Library and Research Center at 415 Lynn Street, the museum's research library in the former Effinger Brewery office building (Society Architecture and History Inventory record), stands within the same street grid on the district's edge.

## 2. The story
In 1882, in Baraboo, five Ringling brothers formed the Ringling Brothers Classic and Comic Concert Company (Wisconsin Historical Society National Register record). Two years later, in 1884, they opened in Baraboo as a small wagon show — the Register record is plain about how small: it had no bandwagon, no menagerie, and no wild animals. The growth after that was industrial in pace. By 1890 the traveling circus filled 18 train cars and carried leopards, kangaroos, a zebra, and a hippopotamus (Register record).

A show that size could not simply park for the winter; it had to be housed, and housing it built a plant. The Register record lists the construction: to house the circus during the winter, the brothers built wagon shops, animal barns, a rooming house, offices, and other specialty shops. They bought additional property along Water Street and kept building — new buildings went up through 1915, and the Register record dates the district's contributing buildings 1884–1915. A second complex near the main bridge into Baraboo held the railroad workshop and the storage barn for the circus train (Register record). The National Historic Landmark photo file preserves the plant's own paper: a period photograph of the Print Shop and Wardrobe Building, and a Water Street view of the quarters with the buildings keyed by letter like a factory campus, A through F. The Society's 1915 photograph of Water Street (§7) shows the street as company ground — the Society's image record says of it: "The whole street was circus property."

The Register record states the economic fact this entry is built on: "The growing circus had a large economic impact on the Ringlings' home city of Baraboo. In addition to employing people within the circus, the show also provided work for local trades people and supported local businesses." Winter quarters work was Baraboo work — the wagons repaired in the wagon shops, the animals kept in the barns, the posters printed in the print shop, the costumes made and stored in the wardrobe building, the crews lodged in the rooming house and the Winter Quarters Hotel, all of it staffed through a Sauk County winter.

The consolidation ended the Baraboo winters. The brothers had acquired the Barnum & Bailey circus — the National Park Service's nomination for the Charles Ringling House dates the purchase to 1907 — and the combined shows' winter headquarters moved to Bridgeport, Connecticut. The Society's Register summary dates the consolidation and the move to 1919; the Register's own record closes the period of significance at 1918 and lists 1918 among the significant years, and the Charles Ringling nomination says the move happened in 1918 (the conflict prints in §6, unreconciled). The Society's site history frames the ground's circus era simply: Ringling Bros. Circus, 1884–1918. After the move, the Ringlings sold most of their Baraboo buildings to local businesses (Register record). The Landmark photo file, photographed in 1968, shows the afterlife plainly: the Baggage Horse Barn carrying a farm equipment company's signs, the Camel Barn in use as a museum building with a telephone booth at its door — the plant dispersing into ordinary Baraboo commerce and, in part, into a museum.

The museum came from inside the business. John M. Kelley, the Ringling brothers' personal attorney, retired to Baraboo and led the effort to preserve what the circus had left, joined by members of the Gollmar family — first cousins of the Ringlings and performers themselves (Society site history). Fundraising produced a facility, and Circus World opened on July 1, 1959; the property was transferred to the Wisconsin Historical Society, which operates it today (Society site history). The Legislative Fiscal Bureau's 2017 budget paper adds the operating detail: the Society has owned the facilities, artifacts, and site since the 1959 transfer, and in 1960 arranged for a private foundation — now the Circus World Museum Foundation — to operate the museum on the state's behalf, with the 2017–19 budget providing for Circus World to operate as a historic site within the Historical Society.

The museum grew the way the circus had — by acquisition. It began with less than an acre and six wagons (Society site history). In 1960 Charles Phillip "Chappie" Fox became director and championed the acquisition, preservation, and restoration of circus wagons; the Great Circus Parade was established in those years with Ben Barkin and Schlitz Brewing Company sponsorship (Society site history). Today the site holds 64 acres, 30 structures, seven National Historic Landmark structures, and over 260 circus wagons (Society site history and home page, checked November 26, 2026). The winter quarters district was listed in the National Register and designated a National Historic Landmark on August 4, 1969, and entered in the State Register on January 1, 1989 (Society Register record; NRIS 69000032).

## 3. Then / now
**Then (1884):** a wagon show with no bandwagon, no menagerie, and no animals, opening in its hometown. **By 1890:** a show filling 18 train cars, with a menagerie, wintering in a purpose-built plant on Water Street. **Now:** the plant's ground is a 64-acre museum site, and the Legislative Fiscal Bureau's paper describes Circus World as the largest circus history museum in the world.

**Then:** Water Street as wholly circus property — shops, barns, offices, a hotel for the help, the whole street working for one firm through the winter. **Now:** a mixed district on the same streets, where museum buildings and privately owned Ringling buildings stand side by side, and the Society's Register record has to ask visitors to respect the owners' privacy.

**Then:** a winter quarters built campaign by campaign, 1884 to 1915, to house a growing show. **Now:** nine contributing buildings counted in the Register record, seven of the quarters structures carrying National Historic Landmark status in the Society's own count, and the show's rolling stock — over 260 wagons — preserved as the collection.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route. Museum stops assume the Society's season and its published arrangements; the street-grid stops are exterior readings only, and privately owned buildings are not stops.

1. **On Water Street, at the district's edge:** Read the street as the plant's floor before entering anything. The §7 photograph was taken on this street in 1915, when the whole street was circus property; the buildings on both sides were one firm's winter plant.
2. **In the quarters buildings the museum opens:** Take the Register record's building list as the itinerary — wagon shops, animal barns, the print shop, the wardrobe building, the rooming house and hotel, the offices. Read each as a workplace with a trade in it, not as a backdrop.
3. **At the wagons:** The quarters' output and its upkeep. The restoration program the Society's history credits to Chappie Fox from 1960 continues the wagon shops' work under museum auspices — the plant's trade, preserved as the collection's trade.
4. **At 415 Lynn Street:** The Parkinson Library — the paper end of the enterprise, the archive of a business that ran on contracts, posters, and route books, standing in the same street grid a short walk from the barns.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The end of the Baraboo winters prints two ways. The Society's National Register summary says: "The brothers consolidated the Ringling Brothers and the Barnum and Bailey shows in 1919 and moved the winter headquarters to Bridgeport, Connecticut." The same Register record's property details close the period of significance at 1918 and list 1918 as a significant year, and the National Park Service's Charles Ringling House nomination states: "In 1918 the Ringlings moved the combined circus winter quarters from Baraboo to Bridgeport, Connecticut." This entry uses the Society site history's frame — the ground was the circus's home 1884–1918 — and prints the Register summary's 1919 date here, unreconciled. The Barnum & Bailey purchase is dated 1907 in the Charles Ringling House nomination; the Society's Register summary does not date the purchase, and secondary accounts vary.

The building counts are three counts of three different sets, and this entry keeps them separate: the Society's Circus World pages count seven National Historic Landmark structures; the Society's Register record counts nine contributing buildings in the district; the Legislative Fiscal Bureau's paper likewise counts seven National Historic Landmark winter quarters buildings while describing a larger museum complex. None of the three is a count of "Ringling buildings still standing," and no such count is asserted here.

One street name conflicts between the two Register sources: the National Park Service's NPGallery record bounds the district by "Water, Brian, Lynn, and East Sts.," while the Society's Register record gives "Water, Briar, Lynn, and East Sts." Briar is used in this entry, following the state record for the state's own site.

The museum's founder is described two ways by two state sources: the Society's site history names John M. Kelley, "the personal attorney to the Ringling brothers," as the man who retired to Baraboo and led the preservation effort; the Legislative Fiscal Bureau's 2017 paper describes the museum as "founded by a former employee of the Ringling Brothers and other circus enthusiasts." The site history's fuller account is used in §2; both descriptions print here. Operation has a similar seam: the Fiscal Bureau paper records that from 1960 the Circus World Museum Foundation operated the museum on the state's behalf, and that the 2017–19 budget provided for operation as a historic site within the Historical Society; the Society's current site pages present Circus World simply as one of its historic sites. The locator names the Society as owner and operator and dates the foundation arrangement as the Fiscal Bureau paper gives it.

No street number for the museum appears in the primary sources checked. The Register record locates the district by its bounding streets, and the Society's property records give numbers for individual buildings (202 East Street; 232 and 434 Water Street; 415 Lynn Street). The widely published 550 Water Street address is not printed in this entry because no primary source checked states it. Coordinates are blank in the locator for the same reason: the National Park Service record carries no point, its digitized file for this property is a photograph file, and the Society's Register record bounds the district by streets only.
