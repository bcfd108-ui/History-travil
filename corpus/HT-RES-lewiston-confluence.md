# HT-RES — Lewiston confluence: two rivers, continuing homeland, altered waterfront
Research date: 2026-11-19. Research draft for Dash QC; no outreach, current photograph alignment, or field inspection. Proposed trip-opening stop, not a certified itinerary. Lewiston/Clarkston use Pacific Time.

## Trip-shaping choice
Use **Lewiston Levee Parkway's confluence area** as the principal waterfront stop. It has an official USACE public-recreation listing. Keep **Steelhead Park** as a separate, conditional Clearwater-side extension, not another name for the confluence. The Corps identifies Steelhead as a day-use boat-launch/trail-access area, but the available official description is older and does not establish today's opening or exact pedestrian entrance. Do not route a visitor through a launch lane to make a photograph. [S1, S2]

Proposed interpretive progression, subject to posted conditions: (1) arrive through a signed public confluence/levee entrance; (2) read the rivers as a homeland and travel network before reading the expedition panels; (3) compare the earlier river landscape with the engineered waterfront; (4) consider the continuing salmon relationship rather than ending the story with settlement. This is a thematic sequence, not a surveyed walking loop. No duration, distance or wheelchair gradient is claimed.

**Strongest story moment:** stand on the public path and ask why water can remain visibly present while a river's habitat and people's relationship to it have changed profoundly. The interpretive contrast is supported by EPA's historical habitat comparison and the Tribe's own account, not by an asserted modern photo match. [S3, S4]

## Original narrative: do not begin with an empty place
The Nez Perce Tribe describes the Nimiipuu as a self-governing people whose movements across the region connected fishing, hunting and trade. Its own history explains that the 1855 negotiations retained approximately 7.5 million acres as a reservation. Gold brought trespass; the federal government forced another treaty in 1863, reducing that reservation to approximately 750,000 acres. The Tribe also emphasizes that fishing at usual and accustomed stations and other reserved rights extend beyond reservation boundaries. This is the Tribe speaking about its own history and sovereignty, not a settler-era claim that an unused waterfront awaited discovery. [S4]

NPS describes the Clearwater and tributaries as routes connecting relatives, fishing and trade. Its account of Donald MacKenzie's 1812 post is revealing: local people were interested in exchanging food and horses, not in becoming beaver trappers for the trader. The commercial plan failed. Frame that as Indigenous economic agency, not a failed attempt to bring commerce to people without it. NPS pages disagree between August and September for the post's establishment; retain **1812**, with month unresolved. [S5, S6]

The county museum describes Lewiston's 1861 growth around river transport serving goldfields. It attributes the name to a shipboard suggestion by purser Charles Frush and discusses the move to the confluence after difficult Clearwater navigation. That civic narrative should be read alongside the Tribe's description of mass trespass and coercion, not as a substitute for it. Its historical place-name spelling and translation are museum attributions; no linguistic standardization or pronunciation has been independently confirmed here. [S7, S4]

The river economy changed again. EPA's 2020 assessment dates Lower Granite's completion to 1975 and contrasts the earlier confluence's habitat complexity with deeper, slower impounded water. A historical waterfront is therefore not simply the present bank with older buildings pasted onto it. The report's historical images are useful precisely because they invite attention to channels and shallow areas, not merely architectural nostalgia. [S3]

A living Tribal voice belongs in this story. In the Tribe-hosted newsletter, Shannon Wheeler explains: **“In our creation story, the salmon had given himself to us first.”** This is a short quotation from publicly published material, not permission to retell an entire cultural narrative or turn ceremony into a game. The indexed newsletter passage was readable; the full PDF exceeded the browsing fetch limit. Its complete article context and original publication attribution still need examination before extended quotation. [S8]

## What is publicly documented, and what remains unverified
USACE describes Lewiston Levee Parkway as a day-use corridor with picnic facilities, toilets, water, interpretive facilities and a 13-mile trail system. It lists **year-round, 7:30 a.m. to sunset**, with ADA-accessible facilities. Those are operator-published general provisions, not a real-time assurance that every facility or segment is usable. The access corridor parallels Snake River Avenue and the Lewiston Levee Bypass, with another reach along Highway 12. [S1]

A September 2025 Corps reopening notice separately identifies confluence parking/restrooms near D Street and the Lewis & Clark Interpretive Center. This is useful locator evidence, not confirmation of November 2026 conditions; the full notice returned 403 and only indexed text was available. [S9]

For a broad landscape alternative, NPS documents **Confluence Overlook**, the established highway pullout above Lewiston, with auto parking and historical interpretation. Official coordinates: **46.461539, -116.991800**. This is a distinct elevated stop, not the location of the former trading post or a guaranteed repeat-photo station. Use the designated pullout; never stop in a traffic lane or cross US 95 to chase a view. Current weather, visibility, closures and the accessible route from parking were not checked. [S5]

Steelhead's official master-plan poster says boat launch and trail access, and attributes ramp operation to Nez Perce County. Current secondary accounts call the park city-maintained; these may describe different responsibilities. Do not silently resolve that into one operator. Exact official park coordinates, hours and current ramp/trail status remain blank. [S2]

Do not confuse the public levee with **Port of Lewiston Confluence Riverfront**. The Port describes a cruise-dock development with Phase 1 completed in 2025 and future improvements. That project description is not permission to enter operational docks, restricted areas or a particular current camera position. [S10]

## Candidate physical finds, not collection tasks
1. **A public interpretive panel at the confluence.** Onsite question: which river or bank does the panel place the event on, and where is the panel itself? Photograph panel plus immediate public-path context. Answer key blank until field-verified. A marker need not occupy the historical event's exact spot.
2. **A visible engineered bank from the signed levee path.** Question: what bank material and water-edge form can you actually see today? Record observation, not a claim about salmon abundance. Stay off riprap and away from water.
3. **The designated overlook's interpretive marker**, optional separate stop. Question: what activity does its account say local people preferred over trapping? Also record one visible landmark named by the marker. No answer key or claim about today's visibility is prefilled.

These are observation candidates, not a scored live scavenger hunt. Do not collect objects, seek archaeological sites, photograph private people without consent, or use cultural knowledge as a test of authenticity.
