# HT-RES-NJ-morristown-nhp — The Hard Winter: a headquarters house in town, ten thousand men in the hills
Division NJ seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

In the winter of 1779–80, the coldest winter of the Revolutionary War, George Washington ran the war from a Morristown mansion while some ten thousand of his soldiers wintered in log huts in the hills at Jockey Hollow a few miles south. Morristown National Historical Park holds both halves of that winter — the Ford Mansion headquarters in town and the encampment ground — as one park in two units, and this entry covers them together, as the National Park Service carries them.

## 1. Trip gate
The park's own terms, as the National Park Service states them on its Morristown hours page (checked November 26, 2026; page last updated November 2, 2026), govern this entry. The park grounds are open year-round, daily, sunrise to 5:00 PM. The buildings keep a shorter week: Washington's Headquarters Museum and the Jockey Hollow Visitor Center are open Thursday through Monday, 9:30 AM to 4:30 PM, and the Wick House is open Thursday through Monday with hours that vary. The Ford Mansion is entered by guided tour only: tours begin in the Headquarters Museum, and free tour passes are given out first-come at the Museum desk — the Service takes no phone or online reservations, so the pass is got in person, on the day. All park buildings close for a winter stretch each year (January 4, 2027 to February 12, 2027 in the current posting) and on Thanksgiving Day, December 25, and January 1. Cost is not a barrier the sources raise: the Service's fees page states that no entrance pass is required and that "All park grounds and facilities at Morristown National Historical Park are FREE of charge."

The park is not one fenced site. Its two principal units are the Washington's Headquarters unit in Morristown — the Headquarters Museum and the Ford Mansion at 30 Washington Place, Morristown, NJ 07960 (phone 973-539-2016 ext. 210) — and the Jockey Hollow unit, whose Visitor Center and the Wick House stand at 586 Tempe Wick Road, Morristown, NJ 07960 (the Service itself marks this address approximate; phone 973-543-4030). Two smaller areas complete the park as the Service lists it: Fort Nonsense at 16 Chestnut Street, Morristown (address approximate; no structures and no restrooms), and the New Jersey Brigade / Cross Estate area at 61 Jockey Hollow Road, Bernardsville, NJ 07924 (no restrooms). A visitor who wants both halves of the 1779–80 winter drives between the units; nothing in this entry treats them as one walkable ground.

## 2. The story
Morristown mattered to the Continental Army before the famous winter. Washington first brought the army here in January 1777, after the victories at Trenton and Princeton, and the first Morristown encampment ran from January 6 to May 28, 1777, with Washington quartered at Arnold's Tavern. The army returned in the fall of 1779 and stayed into the summer of 1780, and that second encampment — the one this park principally interprets — is the ground of this entry.

The headquarters was a private house. The Ford Mansion, the home of Colonel Jacob Ford Jr. of the New Jersey Militia, was complete by 1772 in the Park Service's account. Ford died in the house in January 1777, of pneumonia, during the first encampment. When Washington returned, he made the widow's house his headquarters: he lived and worked there from December 1, 1779 to June 7, 1780. His wife Martha joined him, arriving December 31. The household the Service describes is crowded and working: five aides-de-camp — the youngest of them Lieutenant Colonel Alexander Hamilton — and eighteen servants attended the commander, while the widow Theodosia Ford and her four children kept to separate parts of their own house. Across the road stood the camp of Washington's Life Guard, 200 to 250 men; General Henry Knox's Artillery Brigade camped near what is now Burnham Park.

The army itself lay south of town, at Jockey Hollow. There, in the Service's figures, eleven brigades — roughly ten thousand men — built a log city of about 1,200 huts through the fall and winter; nine of the brigades lay in the Hollow itself, and the soldiers cut on the order of 2,000 acres of trees to build and burn. General Arthur St. Clair's staff quartered with the Wick family, whose farmhouse still stands on the ground. The winter they built through was the "Hard Winter" of 1779–80, the severest of the war: the Service counts over twenty snowfalls — its brochure says at least twenty snowstorms — across the encampment.

The winter's events, as the Service's encampment summary carries them, were not quiet. Benedict Arnold's court martial sat at Morristown from December 23, 1779 into January 26, 1780. In mid-January, January 14–15, the army raided Staten Island. In April the Spanish envoy Juan de Miralles died at Morristown (April 28). On May 25, Connecticut regiments mutinied in camp. On June 7, 1780, Washington left Morristown as the British moved — the fighting at Connecticut Farms that day and at Springfield on June 23 closed the New Jersey campaign season that the Morristown winter had made possible.

The ground was kept. In the account the New Jersey State Library carries, the National Park Service became the lead agency in 1933 and Morristown National Historical Park was established, linking the Ford Mansion with the sites of the Continental Army encampments south of Morristown; the park's Headquarters Museum building, designed by John Russell Pope to house the park's Washington collections, was completed in 1937 (park brochure). The National Register record for the park (reference 66000053) dates to October 15, 1966, the Register's first year.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** December 1779 to June 1780 — a commander, his wife, five aides, and eighteen servants working out of a militia colonel's widow's house, while ten thousand soldiers froze and built in 1,200 log huts a few miles south, through more than twenty snowfalls. **Now:** the house stands in town and is shown by guided tour; the Hollow is park ground, its hut city gone, with the Wick farmhouse still standing on the farm the army camped around.

**Then:** an army that had to be fed, housed, and held together within sight of the enemy's New York garrison, in the worst winter of the war. **Now:** a free national park in two units — a museum and mansion in Morristown, a visitor center and encampment ground at Jockey Hollow — open on the posted terms in §1.

**Then:** a widow's household sharing its own house with the headquarters of a war. **Now:** the Ford Mansion preserved as Washington's Headquarters, its rooms the center of the park's interpretation of that shared winter.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — and the conditions are the park's: buildings open Thursday through Monday only, the mansion by tour pass only, and the two units reached by road, not on foot:

1. **Washington's Headquarters Museum, 30 Washington Place:** Start here, and start early in the building day — the Ford Mansion tour passes are first-come at this desk, and the museum's own exhibits carry the winter's documents and objects.
2. **The Ford Mansion, by guided tour:** The headquarters rooms — where Washington, Martha, five aides, and eighteen servants worked and lived while the Fords kept their own part of the house. Tour only; there is no self-guided entry.
3. **Jockey Hollow Visitor Center, 586 Tempe Wick Road:** The encampment's orientation — eleven brigades, ten thousand men, 1,200 huts — before walking the ground itself.
4. **The Wick House and the Hollow ground:** The surviving farmhouse at the camp's heart, and the open hillsides where nine brigades lay. Read the scale against the Service's figures: 2,000 acres of trees went into this city of huts.
5. **Fort Nonsense, 16 Chestnut Street (if time allows):** The small earthwork above town — no structures, no restrooms — the army's precaution that the winter never required.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The Ford Mansion's building date does not print the same way in every account. The National Park Service's account used here says the house was "complete by 1772." Secondary accounts commonly give 1774 as the year the house was built. This entry follows the Service's wording in §2 and prints the 1774 form here as the unreconciled variant; the desk did not find a primary building record in this pass to settle it.

The park's establishment prints two ways as well. The New Jersey State Library's history says the National Park Service became the lead agency in 1933 and the park was established then, linking the Ford Mansion and the encampment sites. Secondary accounts give a precise day — March 2, 1933 — and describe Morristown as the first National Historical Park in the system. Neither the day nor the "first" designation was verified from a primary source in this pass, so §2 prints the year and the State Library's account, and this section carries the rest as unverified against a primary record.

The encampment's figures — eleven brigades, about ten thousand men, about 1,200 huts, about 2,000 acres of trees cut, twenty-plus snowfalls — are the Park Service's own summary figures and print as such, rounded as the Service rounds them. They are not independently reconciled here against muster rolls or orderly books, and no source checked in this pass contradicts them.

No coordinate point for this entry was verified from a primary source in this pass. The National Register record carries a location line only (see §10), and no Park Service page checked states a latitude/longitude for the headquarters or the Hollow. The locator in §10 therefore prints no point, under this entry's rule that a coordinate prints only when a primary source itself carries it.
