# HT-RES-MO-missouri-state-penitentiary — The stone front on Lafayette Street
Division prisons-asylums-tours-001 • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A prison that received its first inmate on March 8, 1836, and was still receiving inmates 168 years later — the Missouri State Penitentiary in Jefferson City is the oldest penitentiary ground west of the Mississippi, and what stands on Lafayette Street today is a partial survival: housing units, dungeon cells, a courtyard, and a small stone execution building, in a city that grew up around the walls.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a guided tour, and only a guided tour: the Jefferson City Convention and Visitors Bureau operates Missouri Pen Tours at the former penitentiary, and its own site states that visitors "will walk through historic housing units, the upper courtyard, and the gas chamber." Tours start at 115 Lafayette Street, Jefferson City, MO 65101. The operator's homepage states the season plainly — "Tour season runs March – November" — and lists the standard history tour from $30: "Step back in time and discover the intriguing history of the infamous Missouri State Penitentiary on this guided tour! Hear about escape attempts, riots, female inmates, and more." The operator's pages checked state no daily tour hours, and none are printed here. The bureau also runs the Missouri State Penitentiary Museum in the lower level of the Col. Darwin W. Marmaduke House — the former warden's residence — at 700 E. Capitol Avenue; the bureau's Jefferson City page states museum admission is $2 per person, or free with a prison tour, and describes a replica cell and displays on prison industries, contract labor, and life inside the walls. The prison itself is still owned by the State of Missouri, per the operator's history page. The ground sits in downtown Jefferson City, above the Missouri River — not at the edge of town, but embedded in it: this prison was built to anchor a capital city, and the city kept it at its center for 168 years.

## 2. The story
The penitentiary exists because Jefferson City needed a reason to remain the capital. The Missouri State Archives' finding aid records the sequence: Governor John Miller was the first to advocate a penitentiary in the new capital; the 1829 law on the permanent seat of government directed land-sale revenue toward both a seminary of learning and a penitentiary; and on January 16, 1833, the General Assembly approved "An Act for the Erection of a Penitentiary within the State of Missouri," to be located in Jefferson City. In 1835 a contract for stonework was approved. The first inmate, Wilson Eidson, was received on March 8, 1836. The Archives states the standing the prison carried from that day: it "would be the first penitentiary west of the Mississippi River."

From one inmate in 1836 the institution grew into Missouri's primary maximum-security prison and, for long stretches, one of the largest in the country. The Archives records that the site grew to 147 acres (see §6) and that tens of thousands of men and women passed through its doors, including federal prisoners. Its database indexes the Register of Inmates Received from 1836 to 1931 and a separate Register of Military Prisoners, 1864–1875 — the paper trace of a prison that held Civil War military prisoners alongside its sentenced population.

Work was the prison's economy. Inmates quarried stone on the site, built the walls and much of what stood inside them, and labored in prison industries; Missouri Preservation's account lists the penitentiary at its height as a major supplier of saddle trees, shoes, brooms, shirts and work clothes, license plates, furniture, and metal products. The stone housing unit now called A Hall — Housing Unit 4 — was completed in 1868, designed by Warden Horace Swift and built mainly by inmate labor from on-site stone; it remained in continuous use until the prison closed (see §6). Its basement held the dungeon cells — windowless stone cells used for solitary confinement.

Overcrowding was chronic; secondary accounts put the population at about 5,200–5,300 at its 1930s peak (see §6). Among the thousands, a few names carry the prison's public history. James Earl Ray was held here in the 1960s and escaped on April 23, 1967, concealed in a bread shipment leaving the prison — eleven months before he assassinated Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. in Memphis. Charles "Sonny" Liston, later heavyweight champion, was held here in the early 1950s and, by the bureau's account, learned to box inside.

Executions were centralized here late. Before 1937, Missouri death sentences were carried out by county sheriffs, principally by hanging. In 1937 the state moved executions to the penitentiary and built a gas chamber in a small separate stone building, constructed by inmates from prison-quarry stone. The operator's executions page lists the people put to death in that building one by one: the first, William Wright, on March 4, 1938; the last by gas, Lloyd Leo Anderson, on February 26, 1965. Bonnie B. Heady, executed with Carl Austin Hall on December 18, 1953, was the only woman executed in the chamber, and theirs was the only double execution, a second chair having been installed for it. One further execution took place in the building on January 6, 1989 — by lethal injection, the method having become an option in 1987. The operator's list thus supports 40 executions in the building, 39 by gas and one by injection (see §6). The building survives and is on the tour route, recorded here as a fact of the ground, in the same plain terms as the housing units.

The prison's worst single night was September 22, 1954. Two inmates feigned illness; when guards responded, they were overpowered and their keys taken, and inmates began releasing others across the cellblocks. Buildings were set on fire. The Highway Patrol, the National Guard, and police from Jefferson City, St. Louis, and Kansas City were called in, and control was regained the following morning. Four inmates were dead — contemporary wire reporting specified that two of the four were killed by rioters — no inmate escaped, and damage estimates diverge sharply between the contemporary press and later accounts (see §6). The Archives' photograph collection holds the visual record: its scope note lists the 1954 riot and post-riot construction first among its subjects.

The end came by replacement, not by scandal. A new Jefferson City Correctional Center was built east of the city, and the Archives' timeline records the closure in a single line: on September 15, 2004, the penitentiary closed and its inmates were transferred to the new center — some 1,350 maximum-security inmates moved in a single operation, per the Department of Corrections' own annual report account. The prison had operated for 168 years and was, at decommissioning, the oldest continually operating prison west of the Mississippi. Parts of the wider site were then redeveloped for state use, a number of buildings were demolished (see §4), and the surviving core passed to the tour operation that runs it today.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a walled industrial city inside a city — stone housing units, factories, a hospital, a dining hall, and thousands of men living and working under guard, on ground the inmates themselves had quarried and built. **Now:** a partial complex held as a historic landmark in downtown Jefferson City, entered only on a guide-led tour, with several housing units, the upper courtyard, and the gas chamber building standing, and other buildings gone.

**Then:** on the evening of September 22, 1954, fires in four buildings and several thousand inmates out of their cells, with troopers, Guardsmen, and city police converging on a prison in the middle of the state capital. **Now:** the Archives' riot photographs, the operator's riot history, and a tour that walks the housing units where it began — the burned buildings are among those that did not survive.

**Then:** a warden's residence across Capitol Avenue, built in 1888 for Colonel Darwin W. Marmaduke, from which the prison was administered for more than a century. **Now:** the same house, listed on the National Register in its own right (see §6 and §10), holding the penitentiary museum in its lower level.

## 4. What's still there
- Housing Unit 4 (A Hall), completed in 1868 — the oldest surviving housing unit, built of on-site stone by inmate labor, in continuous use until 2004; damaged by the May 2019 tornado, which took its roof, and returned to full tour access after repairs in the early 2020s, per the bureau
- The dungeon cells below Housing Unit 4 — windowless stone solitary cells, part of the operator's visit
- Other historic housing units on the tour route, including the women's housing covered on the longer history tour
- The upper courtyard (upper yard) within the walls
- The gas chamber building — a small separate stone building erected by inmates after the 1937 centralization of executions; on the tour route
- Lengths of the stone perimeter wall and the Lafayette Street front shown in this entry's first photograph, with the prison's name and a clock cut into the central tower
- The Missouri State Penitentiary Museum in the lower level of the Marmaduke House (the Warden's House), 700 E. Capitol Avenue — replica cell, memorabilia, and displays on prison industries and life inside the walls
- What is **not** there: Missouri Preservation records that after closure the former women's prison and at least eight other buildings — including the administration building, hospital, dining hall, and a housing unit — were demolished, with further demolitions funded in 2010; the surviving complex is a core, not the whole prison

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the museum, 700 E. Capitol Avenue:** Start in the Marmaduke House lower level — the replica cell and industry displays — so the prison's economy of quarried stone and factory labor is in hand before the ground itself.
2. **At the Lafayette Street front, 115 Lafayette Street:** Read the stone front the first photograph shows — the name, the clock, the barred windows — the public face the state built for its first penitentiary.
3. **Inside Housing Unit 4 (A Hall):** The 1868 stone cellblock and the tiers within it — the oldest surviving housing on the ground, in use from the post–Civil War years to 2004.
4. **Below it, the dungeon cells:** The solitary cells under A Hall — the punitive interior of the same building, in darkness by design.
5. **At the upper courtyard and the gas chamber building:** The yard where the population moved, and the small separate building where the state's executions were carried out between 1938 and 1989 — recorded plainly, as the tour records it.
6. **Across the wider site:** What redevelopment and demolition have taken — the empty ground is part of the record, and the bureau's and preservation accounts name what stood there.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The acreage does not agree across the state's own records. The Archives finding aid says the site "grew to 147 acres." The Office of Administration redevelopment page gives 142.00 acres. The prison's famous nickname — the "bloodiest 47 acres" — rests on a 47-acre figure. The three numbers likely describe different boundaries at different dates, but no record checked reconciles them, and all three print here rather than a merged figure.

The nickname's attribution also conflicts. The operator's history page states the site was "once named 'the bloodiest 47 acres in America' by Guy Magazine." The bureau's pages and much secondary writing attribute the phrase to *Time* magazine, dated 1967. Both attributions print; this entry uses neither as fact.

The 1954 riot's damage and injury figures diverge. Later accounts give $5 million in damage, four inmates dead, and roughly fifty injured. The contemporary wire report carried in the *Medford Mail Tribune* of September 23, 1954 — the morning after — reported four convicts dead, two of them killed by rioters, twenty-three injured, and $10,000,000 in damage. The death toll of four is consistent; the injury and damage figures are not, and both versions print.

The execution count is often stated loosely. Secondary sources variously say "40 inmates were executed using lethal gas." The operator's own executions page, listing each execution by name and date, supports a narrower statement: 39 executions by lethal gas between March 4, 1938, and February 26, 1965, and one by lethal injection, in the same building, on January 6, 1989 — 40 in the building in total, one of them a woman. This entry prints the operator's list-based count.

A Hall's dates carry a small spread: former Deputy Warden Mark Schreiber, quoted in 2015 reporting on the nomination effort, described Housing Unit 4 as "built circa 1865 and opened in 1868"; most accounts date the building simply to 1868, which the text uses.

The closure date has a secondary-source shadow. The Archives timeline and Department of Corrections records put the closure and transfer on September 15, 2004. Some secondary accounts give October 14, 2004. No primary record checked supports the October date, and the Archives date is used.

The National Register position must be stated exactly, because it is commonly misstated. The prison complex itself is **not** listed. A nomination was prepared under the redevelopment programmatic agreement, was pulled from the Missouri Advisory Council's May 15, 2015 agenda at the property owner's request, was resubmitted with reduced boundaries, and was then rejected by the State Historic Preservation Office as not meeting National Park Service guidelines, per the City of Jefferson's Certified Local Government annual report for 2015. What **is** listed is the Warden's House alone: 700 E. Capitol Avenue, listed October 24, 1991, reference No. 91001518, per the NPS NPGallery record. Any source describing the penitentiary as a whole as National Register–listed is wrong on the record checked.

Population peaks are approximate in every source checked: figures of 5,200 and 5,300 both circulate for the 1930s maximum, and no biennial-report figure was verified at the desk. The Archives names the first inmate as Wilson Eidson; some secondary accounts add that his offense was stealing a watch. The Archives record checked does not state the offense, and it is not printed as fact here.

## 8. Standing on the ground
What a visitor can check against the record, on site, in plain terms:

- The stone front on Lafayette Street can be read against this entry's first photograph: the carved name, the clock in the central tower, the bricked-up arches, and the bust below the name panel are all still legible from the public side of the wall.
- Inside Housing Unit 4, the building itself is the check on the 1868 and inmate-labor accounts: the stone is the on-site quarried material the records describe, and the tiers and catwalks show the housing form in use until 2004.
- The dungeon cells below A Hall check against the solitary-confinement record by their fabric alone: windowless stone cells, with light only at the door.
- The gas chamber building stands apart from the housing units, as the 1937 account describes — a small separate stone building, not a room inside a cellblock.
- At the museum, the replica cell gives the dimensions of confinement that the housing units confirm at full scale, and the industry displays can be read against the quarried stone of the buildings outside.
- The gaps are checkable too: where the administration building, hospital, and dining hall stood is now open or redeveloped ground, matching the demolition record in §4.
