## 2. The story
**Willie Mims**, interviewed on **January 6, 1994** by Jefferson Middle School Black History Club students, remembers arriving in Eugene as a child without normal housing options. He also remembers friends, games and movies. The two parts belong together: material exclusion did not erase childhood or the relationships adults built around it. [3–4]

At approximately **13–14 minutes**, Mims distinguishes overt verbal abuse from a quieter social isolation. His phrase **“But there was other ways: isolation.”** is reproduced from the published transcript. At school he had acquaintances, but the social boundaries outside school remained. This is his retrospective testimony, not a claim every child's experience was identical. [4]

His work memories move from berry and bean picking to shoe shining, manually setting bowling pins and construction work to buy a car. The account ends with his own business. Read him as a person describing work and agency, not simply as a witness available to authenticate a tragedy. [4, approximately 16–19 minutes]

The **Black Pioneer Families of Lane County**, in a statement signed by president **David Nettles**, describes home-based hair care among community women because Eugene's salons did not serve Black hair. The organization treats the Mims property as a continuing community inheritance, not an empty historical stage. Its published account places the destruction of the Across the Bridge neighborhood in **August 1949**. [5]

The Mims Houses organization's history gives **August 24, 1949**, and describes the governor's 2024 acknowledgment of the forced demolition. It also explains the hospitality C.B. and Annie Mims offered Black visitors and students. [6] Oregon Humanities' 2017 introduction to Nisha Burton's film notes a useful distinction: the house served as a safe haven but **was not listed in the Green Book**. Do not promote it as a documented Green Book entry. [7]

**Distinct story:** this is Eugene's housing exclusion, mutual support, displacement and ongoing community memory. It is not another Vanport flood story and has no Jacksonville connection. Flood exposure may explain conditions; it must not replace the specific governmental demolition in the narrative.

## 3. Then / now
- **Community then:** families made a neighborhood under discriminatory constraints, with work, friendships and mutual help. Mims's school account captures exclusion that does not depend on a posted sign. [4–5]
- **Demolition:** the descendant organization and Mims history identify 1949; the current City park account instead says 1950. Keep that disagreement visible. [1, 5–6]
- **Public landscape now:** a developed city park provides a lawful place to reflect. It is not a surviving neighborhood reconstruction. [1]
- **Community now:** descendants preserve memories and relationships in their own publications. Reading those publications does not authorize visits to homes or requests for personal testimony. [5]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
A compact reflection sequence, not a scavenger route:
1. **Before arrival:** read the opening minutes of Mims's interview and the source note explaining who asked the questions. Begin with his childhood, not a bulldozer.
2. **At a verified-open public park position:** read the short isolation passage. Let the ordinary civic landscape raise the question of who could participate in city life, and on what terms. There is no need to locate his childhood home.
3. **Stay in that same lawful position:** compare the public demolition dates, without staging a reenactment or making the disagreement into a puzzle game.
4. **End with the descendant community:** read Nettles's account of women caring for one another. The strongest moment is understanding that exclusion reached daily life, while people actively made belonging. Finish with living community authorship, not a claim that history ended when buildings disappeared.

If the park approach is closed, the same sequence works remotely. Do not convert a blocked visit into a search for an alternate private viewpoint.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **1949 / 1950:** August 24, 1949 is the date given by the Mims organization; August 1949 by the descendant organization. The City currently says 1950. These may reflect different phases or a source error; this research has not proved which explanation applies. [1, 5–6]
- **Collection date:** Oregon Black Pioneers describes the early project broadly as 1993, but this specific Willie Mims interview is dated **January 6, 1994** in its catalog and transcript. [3–4, 10]
- **Duration:** the finding aid's longer digital file includes student exercises beyond the interview; the interview item itself is **19:38**. Do not present the longer mixed file as continuous testimony. [3, 11]
- **Legal wording:** the Mims site's generalized “Exclusion Laws” language is not used here as a precise finding about an operative 1940s ordinance. Specific statutes, covenants and enforcement mechanisms require separate documentary work. [6]
- **Unverified:** exact historic household footprints, contemporary field conditions, new memorial completion, an accessible camera station and image reuse rights.
- **Source limit:** the transcript was read; the full recording was not independently auditioned against it. Potential transcription errors remain. Offensive language occurs in the source and need not be reproduced to tell this story.
