# Cottonwood Air Force Station and the people who watched the sky

Research draft · Cottonwood, Idaho County, Idaho · November 20, 2026

## 1. Trip gate

Reflection stop. The public stop is the Historical Museum at St. Gertrude, 465 Keuterville Road, Cottonwood. A short walk leads from museum arrival to the Welcome Center. The museum lists Monday–Saturday, 9 a.m.–4:30 p.m. Pacific, year-round, with a Thursday-through-Saturday Thanksgiving closure (November 26–28 in 2026). Its public galleries are indoors. Check with the museum before a winter trip specifically for the Air Force Station placard. A photograph documents the display in 2023. Its presence today is unconfirmed. The former radar grounds, correctional institution and residences are outside this visit. [1–3, 7]

## 2. The story

In July 1958, fourteen airmen were in Cottonwood preparing a station that could not yet house them. The barracks furniture had not arrived. Major Frank S. Palmer and his family rented a house in town. Other airmen found apartments while the first contingent organized meals, supplies and vehicles. The Cottonwood Chronicle reported that equipment installation and outstanding contracts still stood between the new post and activation. A personnel arrival date alone gives a misleading picture of when the watch began. [4]

The Air Force identifies the unit as the 822d Aircraft Control and Warning Squadron. It had formed at Geiger Field in Washington by February 1957 and moved to Cottonwood around July 1958. By July 2, 1959, the local paper described the squadron operating three radar towers on the butte. That month, construction superintendent Verl Rutter explained that an existing tower would come down to make way for a new one. He expected to hire local workers. Cottonwood was simultaneously a working station and a building site. [4, 5]

The job was to detect aircraft, report what operators saw and help direct interceptors. Cottonwood supplied information to a regional air-defense network. The 822d became the 822d Radar Squadron (SAGE) on September 1, 1960. SAGE stood for Semi-Automatic Ground Environment. The squadron came under the Seattle Air Defense Sector in June 1963. Defense Command's own planning history identifies Cottonwood as station SM-150. [3, 5, 6]

There was a human network on the prairie, too. Dana Lohrey remembered taking turns with family members in Clearwater listening and watching for aircraft, using what he called “old wall-ringer telephones” to report contacts. He recalled reports going to Cottonwood or Spokane. His account spans the broader Ground Observer Corps era and cannot establish the first day Cottonwood received a call. Jim Rehder remembered riding to school in an Air Force vehicle that also picked up children from the station housing. In 1974 he went to work as a teacher at the correctional institution that followed the base. Local reporter Lorie Palmer, herself the daughter of a Cottonwood radar technician, recorded both recollections. [3]

The announced defense cuts reached Cottonwood in November 1964. The Army's radar history attributes the early shutdown to a bearing failure in December. By May 1965, local coverage described crews removing equipment and personnel leaving for other assignments. In its unit lineage, the Air Force records June 25, 1965, as the squadron's discontinuation. These records document different endings for the equipment, the people and the organization. [3, 5, 8, 9]

## 3. Then / now

The Air Force spread the station over three places. The radar operators worked high on the butte. Airmen ate and slept at the lower support post. Family housing stood near town. A later account of the Corps of Engineers' property record identifies 27 original family units. The lower post became a Job Corps center, then the North Idaho Correctional Institution. Idaho's corrections department confirms it still operates there. Its resident-visiting arrangements are separate from the museum visit. [3, 7, 10]

The housing had another history after military service. Palmer reported that two local purchasers bought 18 of the former homes at a state auction. Keeping rental housing available in Cottonwood was one purchaser's stated reason. The houses belong in the story as homes and are outside the visitor boundary. [3]

## 5. Public visit and strongest story moment

Enter through the museum's public Welcome Center during posted hours. Ask whether the Air Force Station placard is on view. If it is, look first at what the aerial image leaves out. The towers explain why the Air Force chose a butte. Rehder's ride to school explains how the station became part of Cottonwood. If the display has rotated out, the dated newspaper account carries that story from anywhere. The visit ends at the museum. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

- **First watch.** A later local account uses July 1, 1958, as the operational date. The July 10 Chronicle still describes preparation. The Army history gives 1959, and the July 2, 1959, Chronicle explicitly describes operating radars. The first logged watch remains unverified. [3–5, 8]
- **Unit and service end.** The Army history assigns Cottonwood to the 821st. The Air Force's adjacent entries put the 821st at Baker, Oregon, and the 822d at Cottonwood. Period reporting also names the 822d. The veterans' site gives July 1, 1965, as its inactivation date. Use June 25 for formal squadron discontinuation, and keep the last operational shift open. [4, 5, 8, 9]
- **Later use.** A summary of the 1990 property record gives 1972 for state acquisition. Local historical reporting gives 1974 and documents Rehder working there that year. Acquisition, lease and operational start may have had different dates. The conveyance instrument remains to be checked. [3, 7, 10]
- **Current evidence.** The public museum is confirmed. The placard's current display status, present condition of the historic tower and original day of radar operation remain separate gaps. No sourced haunting tradition appears in the records used for this entry.

## 7. Historical photo record

A Wikimedia Commons file credited to LarryB55 shows a large enclosed dome above a timbered hillside and a smaller dome on a separate tower. Its description dates the scene to about 1964. Its date field is November 8, 2011, and the upload followed the next day. The uploader declares own work and CC BY-SA 3.0. The image was inspected. Original exposure and scan provenance remain open, so it is retained as a candidate. The newspaper placard photograph and the 2018 site photographs are evidence citations only. No image has been adopted for display and no repeat-photo position is assigned. [3, 11, 12]
