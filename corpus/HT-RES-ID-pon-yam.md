## 2. The story
**Distinct interpretive proposition:** approach this building through the merchant household's objects, not another retelling of Idaho City's gold discovery or Pinney's postal rebuilding. The town overview and the separate Post Office Block dossier were read for overlap. Neither develops the excavated tableware evidence. [7]

During restoration, the Foundation removed and numbered floor planks and collaborated on archaeological work. That is the bridge between a surviving building and evidence of everyday life, rather than an invitation to imagine everything behind its walls. [2]

Archaeologist Renae J. Campbell directly analyzed material from the 1997–98 excavations. Two illustrated ceramic fragments carry pecked Chinese characters: an English-made Close & Co. whiteware plate and a Chinese Four Seasons Flowers bowl. [3, printed pp.55,70–72] These are not merely imports on a merchant's stock list. Someone altered them after manufacture. Campbell discusses ownership marking and shared meals as interpretations; neither identifies the person who made these particular marks. [3]

**Original material voice:** 美 and 錦 appear in the photographed artifacts and caption. They are inscriptions, not a recovered statement by Pon Yam. Do not turn the fragments into his signature or invent a conversation around a table. [3, p.71]

Suggested visitor narration, original interpretation rather than quoted testimony: *A shop can connect a town to the world. A mark added to a dish asks a smaller question: whose place was this at the table? The record gives us the mark, but not the hand.* This leaves room for the household and customers instead of attributing every object to its most famous resident.

## 3. Then / now
The operator describes Pon Yam's premises as both home and business, serving community needs beyond retail. Its account places his return to China in late 1904 and death there in June 1905. The Foundation acquired the property in 1996, stabilized it and developed interpretation of Chinese experiences. [2]

Today this is a museum within a living town. Archaeological evidence should deepen that visit without turning a museum room into a guaranteed display list. A published artifact image proves a studied object, not that it is currently in a case visitors can see. No current case inventory was obtained.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
A single-site micro-visit that can attach to the Idaho City itinerary, not another town circuit or a separate long drive sold on an unconfirmed opening.

1. **Orient before arrival.** Check the Foundation's notice for the actual date. Save the scholarly source link for reading, not an unlicensed image republished into the app. Allow a provisional 20–30 minutes outside; this is editorial planning allowance, not operator duration. [1,3]
2. **Recognize the right building.** From lawful public space at Montgomery/Commercial, distinguish the surviving place from the former post office's different corner. No precise standing point has been surveyed. [2,4,7]
3. **Shift scale.** Read about floor removal and the recovery of material evidence. Compare the large public facade to the tiny surviving marks in the publication. The proposal is a reading stop, not an instruction to inspect floorboards. [2,3]
4. **If explicitly open:** follow staff directions and look for interpretation of household and commercial life. If the two dishes are not exhibited, do not imply they are missing from the collection or demand access. An actual display list remains unverified.
5. **End with the strongest story moment:** the two altered dishes. Invite the visitor to separate what can be seen, what a researcher infers, and what no source yet identifies. The ending is not “we solved who owned this.” It is the recognition that commerce and belonging can leave evidence in an ordinary object.

No cemetery detour, private yard shortcut or hunt at a closed door is needed. In poor conditions, the reading component can happen away from the site; it does not count as a verified physical visit.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The operator says Wong Chong & Co. bought the building in 1873 and Pon Yam took over after the company's 1875 dissolution. The Heritage Trust instead presents a likely lease around 1874 and twenty years of operation. These descriptions are not interchangeable proof of deed ownership, tenancy or business succession. Original deeds/partnership records were not independently inspected here. [2,4]

The existing town dossier already flags conflicting 1865/1867 construction dates; no new construction ruling is made. This dossier does not repeat the large-diamond story, population claims or an assumed generic Chinese experience as its explanatory center. [7]

The dissertation is **primary archaeological analysis**, but its historical newspaper/deed citations are mediated unless independently opened. No merchant-authored ledger, letter or sworn statement was read. The inscriptions belong to unidentified hands. Material from a mixed home/business context cannot automatically establish who used each object. No current display placement, artifact loan status, conservation condition, rights release or visitor photography rule was verified. No Tribal history specific to this parcel is asserted; no absence of earlier Indigenous history is implied.
