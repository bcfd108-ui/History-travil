## 2. The story
**James Graves Jones**, writing on **April 21, 1906**, tried to tell family he was alive. UC Davis's California History–Social Science Project publishes the text of his message written on a shirt collar, with California Historical Society provenance. His opening is direct: **“All safe but awfully scared.”** The unusual writing surface makes communication itself part of the household story. The reproduced text was read; the original artifact was not inspected. [3]

**Charles Ross**, writing to **A. M. Von Metzke on June 12, 1906**, describes a different stage. Bancroft's digitized transcription records continuing outdoor cooking, household repair delays and a newly obtained printing-shop job whose permanence was uncertain. He expresses both concern about the emotional aftermath and confidence in rebuilding. These are one writer's observations, not citywide health statistics or a universal recovery experience. The complete short PDF transcription was downloaded and read. [4]

Together, the accounts resist ending the story when the flames stopped. Sending news, obtaining food, repairing a household and finding paid work operate on different clocks. The contrast between immediate reassurance and later uncertainty is this draft's interpretive frame, not proof that either author represents every displaced resident.

A third household lead is **Abbie's April 25 letter to Julia**, described by SFPL photo curator Christina Moretta in 2013. The curator reports lost home/store possessions and continued use of the same clothes. The scanned page retrieval failed; these details remain attributed to the curator's description, not independently transcribed handwriting. [5]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**A quiet document encounter, executable online; conditional research-room reading only if its gate is confirmed.**

1. Begin with the immediate problem of telling family that someone survived. Distinguish original writing material from a modern reproduction.
2. Move forward in time to the later letter. Look for the difference between the survival message and the practical work of rebuilding a household and income.
3. Read the curator's household lead as a different evidence layer. Do not fill missing handwriting with a reconstructed quotation.
4. Finish with the strongest reflection: a city can describe itself as recovering while individual households are still waiting for ordinary life to function.

No earthquake simulation, fire reenactment, emergency-response exercise or competitive loss comparison. The sequence ends with the records, not a visit to where someone lost a home.
