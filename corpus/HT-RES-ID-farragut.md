## 2. The story
The Navy built a temporary inland training city with extraordinary speed. Its official postwar construction history records an initial 20,000-person capacity plan enlarged to 30,000 in the July 29, 1942 contract while work was already underway. It dates commissioning to September 15, 1942. The scale was not just barracks: housing, food, administration and medical care supported the transformation of civilians into sailors. No weapons-system detail is needed to understand that task. [3]

The Idaho Military Museum dates the first recruits' arrival to **September 17, 1942**, and the last graduating class to **March 10, 1945**. It distinguishes **293,381 recruits** from **more than 25,000 service-school attendees**. Those categories should not be silently added or presented as one independently audited total. Commissioning, arrival, graduation and eventual closure are different milestones. [4]

**Harold Joseph Dorn** provides a first-person view of the supporting work. Wisconsin Veterans Museum oral history **OH 401**, interviewed by **Tom Colby on July 23, 2003**, records his service as a Navy hospital corpsman. Dorn trained at Great Lakes, then served at Farragut; he was not a Farragut boot-camp recruit. On learning and working he recalled: **“First you went to school to learn it, but then from there you went to work.”** [5]

His reminiscence covers care for recruits, dental work and baseball. It is a later participant account, not a wartime headcount or a medical instruction. The transcript was read; the original sound recording was not played. Dorn's ordinary working day makes the station intelligible as a community sustained by support staff, not merely a headline-sized number of trainees. [5]

The land's history did not begin in 1942. The **Coeur d'Alene Tribe's own Ancestral Lands account** includes permanent sites on Lake Pend Oreille's shores within a broader homeland and emphasizes continuing relationships with that landscape. This is regional context, not a claim identifying a village or ceremonial site inside a visitor itinerary. No culturally sensitive place is mapped. [6]

## 3. Then / now
A training station organized around collective routine became a public landscape with different uses. The surviving Brig museum is one interpretive doorway into that transformation, not the whole former station reconstructed. IDPR identifies memorial artwork at the plaza beside the museum and a collection of station memorabilia. [7]

An archival record of Farragut's later Scout gathering reminds the researcher that postwar reuse is a separate chapter. It should not be mislabeled as a Navy photograph or used to infer that its gathering field is open for unrestricted access today. [8]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Proposed interpretive sequence, not a verified measured route:
1. Arrive at the signed public visitor center during published hours, allowing for posted exceptions. Establish which public areas are open and safe that day.
2. Contrast the Navy's planned capacity and construction timeline with the simple support work described by Dorn. Read his account without treating it as everyone's experience.
3. In museum season, add the Museum at the Brig. In November, replace the interior visit with document reading; do not peer into locked areas or seek a back entrance.
4. Only if the operator confirms a safe open public approach, finish at the Memorial Plaza. Otherwise finish at the visitor center or another designated public resting place.
5. **Strongest story moment:** a temporary city could process vast numbers because individual people learned jobs and then did them, every day. Dorn's transition from school to work brings the institutional scale back to a human life.

Onsite question: **Which ordinary service would this temporary city have been unable to function without, and whose work does the interpretation make visible?** No technical weapons activity, re-enactment, sensitive-site visit or operational military observation is proposed.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- September 15 commissioning and September 17 recruit arrival refer to distinct events. March 1945 graduation should not be equated with final station closure. [3,4]
- The 2003 Dorn testimony is retrospective. He trained elsewhere before Farragut; avoid calling him a recruit at this station. Transcript preparation is credited to James Erwin in 2008 and checking to Joan Bruggink in 2011. [5]
- Winter recreation promotion and a structured field saying no groomed Nordic tracks do not establish actual grooming today. No current snow report was independently confirmed. [1]
- H. B. Goldfarb letters, **MG 5726**, were located in University of Idaho catalog metadata, but the letter itself could not be retrieved. Its content is not quoted or reconstructed. [9]
- The large IDPR brochure exceeded the reader's size limit. Museum details here come from accessible operator text, not an invented reading of that PDF.
