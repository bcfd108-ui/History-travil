# HT-RES-MD-baltimore-fort-mchenry — Baltimore: the flag that was still there
Division MD seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

On the morning of September 14, 1814, after some twenty-five hours of British bombardment, the garrison of Fort McHenry raised its great 30-by-42-foot flag over the star fort on Whetstone Point — the flag Francis Scott Key saw from a truce vessel and turned into a song. The fort’s later lives, as a Civil War prison and a World War I surgical hospital, are part of this ground too.

## 1. Trip gate

Fort McHenry is a staffed National Park Service unit at the end of East Fort Avenue on Whetstone Point. The lawful public anchor is the park visitor center and the official path into the Star Fort Historic Zone. The NPS currently reports year-round operation except Thanksgiving Day, December 25, and January 1, ordinarily 9:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m.; the Star Fort, visitor center, shop, and restrooms close before the outer gate, and last entry precedes closing. The Star Fort Historic Zone is a fee area. Hours, fees, weather closures, ceremonies, and special-event controls must be checked again before travel.

This entry does not direct visitors onto waterfront work areas, neighboring industrial property, seawalls outside marked access, or any closed military feature. The fort’s wartime, prison, and hospital history is treated as witness ground, not a game.

## 2. Story

Whetstone Point guarded the approach to Baltimore before the present fort existed. The United States began the masonry star fort in 1798 and named it for James McHenry, a Maryland physician, Revolutionary officer, and secretary of war. By 1814 Major George Armistead commanded a defensive system that extended beyond the pentagonal fort to batteries, troops, and earthworks protecting the harbor and city.

Armistead wanted a garrison flag visible from far downriver. In 1813 he commissioned Baltimore flag maker Mary Young Pickersgill to make two flags: a smaller storm flag and a garrison flag about 30 by 42 feet. Pickersgill’s work depended on a household and shop workforce, including her daughter Caroline and the young Black indentured servant Grace Wisher. The large flag’s survival as a national object should not erase the labor that made it.

British naval forces reached the Patapsco after burning Washington. Beginning on September 13, 1814, bomb vessels and a rocket ship attacked Fort McHenry from beyond the effective range of most American guns. The bombardment continued for roughly twenty-five hours while a British land advance was checked east of Baltimore and a waterborne flanking effort failed. Armistead’s garrison did not surrender. On the morning of September 14, the large garrison flag was raised over the fort as British ships withdrew.

Francis Scott Key had watched from an American truce vessel after negotiating for the release of Dr. William Beanes. Seeing the flag, he drafted words first printed as “Defence of Fort M’Henry.” The poem was set to an existing tune and circulated long before Congress made “The Star-Spangled Banner” the national anthem in 1931. The surviving Pickersgill flag is now held by the Smithsonian, not at the fort; a flag flown at Fort McHenry today is interpretive, not the 1813 textile.

Fort McHenry’s history did not end in 1814. During the Civil War it served as a United States military post and prison. Confederate soldiers, suspected sympathizers, and political detainees were confined there; the prison layer requires attention to individual confinement, due process, and divided civilian loyalties rather than triumphal shorthand. In World War I the reservation became U.S. Army General Hospital No. 2, a large surgical and rehabilitation complex. Most temporary hospital buildings later disappeared. The site returned to National Park Service administration after subsequent federal use, and Congress gave it the combined National Monument and Historic Shrine designation.

## 3. Then and now

The fort that endured the 1814 bombardment was a working coastal-defense installation with a star-shaped masonry core, dry moat, barracks, powder magazines, ramparts, and water-facing batteries. Its defensive landscape extended beyond the masonry walls. Later artillery changes, Civil War occupation, hospital construction, commemoration, restoration, and park development altered the ground.

Today the pentagonal Star Fort remains the clearest surviving structure associated with the bombardment. The outer battery carries nineteenth-century Rodman guns; these help interpret later coastal-defense periods and should not be presented as the weapons that decided the 1814 action. The visitor center introduces the site before a paved route reaches the historic zone. Inside are barracks and exhibit rooms, magazines, ramparts, parade ground, and the flagpole area. The harbor, modern terminals, roads, and city skyline make the defensive geography legible while also showing how thoroughly Baltimore grew around the point.

The adopted July 1954 Navy aerial captures that relationship in an earlier industrial era: the star fort and earthworks dominate the foreground while shipyard and waterfront facilities fill the north side of the point.

NPS describes the visitor-center-to-fort route as paved. Within the historic zone, many brick and gravel paths and most exhibit buildings have accessible entrances, while some passages remain stair-only. Accessibility needs should be checked against current NPS information rather than inferred from the historic fabric.

## 5. Public progression

Start in the visitor center, then follow the official paved approach toward the Star Fort. Pause outside to read the star plan and water approaches before entering. Inside, move through the publicly opened barracks and magazines, then reach the ramparts and flagpole interpretation. Keep three layers separate: the 1814 defense and Pickersgill flags; the Civil War garrison and prison; and the World War I hospital. End on lawful park ground facing the Patapsco, where the range and direction of the British naval attack can be understood without leaving the maintained visitor area.

The strongest story moment is the contrast between the bomb fleet’s long-range attack and the fort’s continued occupation when daylight returned—not a staged recreation of bombardment. Reflection belongs equally to defenders, prisoners, hospital patients, and the civilian labor behind the flag.

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

- Popular retellings often imply that the large garrison flag flew throughout the bombardment. NPS interpretation distinguishes the smaller storm flag used during much or all of the rain and bombardment from the large flag raised on the morning of September 14. This dossier keeps that distinction.
- The precise moment Key began or completed the poem is retold in several forms. The supported claim is that he observed the flag from the truce-vessel position and drafted the poem from that experience; this draft does not invent a minute-by-minute composition scene.
- Fort McHenry’s surviving guns represent several periods. The visible Rodman guns date to the later nineteenth century, not the War of 1812.
- HABS lists the fort’s initial date as 1798 and multiple subsequent-work periods. Surviving masonry includes repair, restoration, and alteration; “the original fort” must not mean every brick is unchanged since 1814.
- The July 1954 aerial is captioned “Official Navy Photograph” in the HABS record. No individual photographer is named, so this draft credits the U.S. Navy rather than inventing one.
- Coordinates are intentionally blank until two independent authoritative sources agree within the project standard.
