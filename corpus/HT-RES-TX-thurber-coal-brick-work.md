# Thurber — coal under the town, brick under other cities
Research draft · Texas OP03 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

### The company built the town that it paid

Thurber worked as a company town. The state library’s Railroad Commission exhibit describes a business that owned the houses, stores and services as well as mines, and paid in company scrip. Tarleton’s history records the fence and exclusion of organizers, and the recruitment of workers from a wide range of places. The exhibit dates successful United Mine Workers organization to 1903. The fence and scrip placed commercial control inside the workers’ home lives; the union story belongs beside the company’s sales literature [2–3].

### The same company sold the fuel and the street

The 1911 Texas Almanac page names Edgar L. Marston, W. K. Gordon and general sales agent Arthur S. Goetz. The inspected company ad tells buyers to write Goetz “for samples, prices and such other information as is desired.” On that same page the firm sells coal to locomotive and boiler customers and claims brick projects across Texas. The sales copy competes directly with asphalt and other materials. Tarleton’s industrial history explains how small coal fired the kilns and a dedicated spur carried shale [2, 4].

### One market left, then the other

As railroads converted away from coal, the mines lost a main customer. The paving business faced cheaper asphalt and the contraction of the Depression. Tarleton dates permanent mine closure to 1926 and kiln closure to 1930. Stores and people did not disappear in the same year. The state library describes most miners having left by the end of 1927. The two timelines describe different events [2–3].

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Start at the public visitor museum with the overview of company wages, scrip and town ownership [1–3].
2. Compare the company’s 1911 coal and brick pitches on the actual Almanac page, keeping its claims as advertisements [4].
3. **Strongest story moment — The coal that made the street.** Read the small-coal firing account inside the visitor gallery beside the company’s brick sales pitch. The trade left its work in distant city streets even as much of the company town became private or disappeared [2, 4–5].
