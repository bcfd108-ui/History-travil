# HT-RES-WI-aztalan — A walled Mississippian town on the Crawfish River, misnamed for the Aztecs
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

A fortified town of flat-topped pyramidal mounds stood on the west bank of the Crawfish River near present-day Lake Mills — a 21-acre village inside a clay-plastered stockade twelve feet high, built by Middle Mississippian people whose tradition centered far to the south at Cahokia. Two of its platform mounds and parts of its stockade stand reconstructed today in Aztalan State Park, and the Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources (DNR) calls the ground one of the most important archaeological sites in Wisconsin. The name on the gate is a mistake: an 1830s judge named it for the Aztecs' legendary homeland, and the National Park Service's landmark nomination says plainly that he named it "erroneously."

## 1. Trip gate
Aztalan State Park is a Wisconsin state park operated by the DNR. The DNR's park page states: "Aztalan State Park is open year-round from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m.," and under Fees: "A vehicle admission sticker is required." The park page gives no dollar amounts, and none are printed in this entry. The park address as the DNR prints it is N6200 Cty Hwy Q, Lake Mills, WI 53551. The DNR's State Natural Areas page for Ancient Aztalan Village gives the driving approach: "From the intersection of I-94 and State Highway 89 in Lake Mills, go south on 89 for 0.9 miles, then west on County B for 2.6 miles, then south on County Q about 0.4 miles to the park entrance." (The same DNR natural-areas page gives the park's hours as 6 a.m. to 10 p.m.; the two DNR pages conflict, and the conflict is printed in §6 rather than resolved here.)

The visitor boundary is set by what this ground is. The landmark nomination records burial mounds outside the stockade and a crematorium structure excavated on the northwest mound: this was a village and a burial and ceremonial ground, not only a town plan. The archaeological site is also designated a State Natural Area (Ancient Aztalan Village, No. 408, designated 2003), and the DNR's prohibited-activities list for its natural areas bars collecting "archaeological artifacts" and any other natural material, alive or dead. The DNR's park coverage describes two miles of trail through open prairie and along the river giving visitors a view of the reconstructed stockade and mounds; the visit this entry describes stays on the park's own trails and public ground. What a visitor walks among is plainly labeled by the DNR itself as reconstruction: "Portions of the stockade and two mounds have been reconstructed in the park." Nothing in this entry directs a visitor off the park's trails, into the Crawfish River, or onto the private and Historical Society parcels the landmark nomination notes at the site's edge (see §4).

## 2. The story
The DNR's natural-areas record states the place in one paragraph: Aztalan "showcases an ancient Middle Mississippian village and ceremonial complex," one of the northernmost outposts of the Middle Mississippian Tradition whose notable center is Cahokia in west-central Illinois. Mound and stockade construction, house construction, pottery decoration, and agricultural practices at Aztalan resemble Cahokia's, the DNR records, though many artifacts of the local Woodland people are also present in large numbers. The DNR's account has the settlers traveling north from Cahokia by the Mississippi, Rock, and Crawfish rivers and settling on the west bank of the Crawfish near Lake Mills, where they hunted, fished, and farmed the floodplain. The village prospered and then emptied; in the DNR's words, "The reason for this abandonment remains a mystery." (The dates the sources attach to the flourishing conflict; see §6.)

The landmark nomination, prepared by the National Park Service's Historic Sites Survey (form dated January 8, 1975), carries the physical description. The site "consisted of a 21-acre village surrounded by a stockade of wooden posts, 12 feet high plastered with clay. Within the village walls were two earthen truncated pyramids and a third low platform mound used for ceremonial purposes. Outside the stockade were associated burial mounds, some of which are still in excellent condition." The palisade carried watchtowers — bastions — at regular intervals. Early observers thought the walls had been built of brick, and that supposed uniqueness drew early interest; not until Dr. S. A. Barrett's 1919 excavations found the post molds did the wall resolve into what it was — wooden poles covered with clay plaster, in places burned, and the burnt plaster is what had been mistaken for brick. With that, Aztalan took its place as a relative of Cahokia rather than an anomaly.

Within the stockade stood three platform mounds, in the northwest, southwest, and northeast corners; the rise in the southeast corner was determined to be natural. Increase Lapham's 1850 survey, published in his *Antiquities of Wisconsin* (Smithsonian Institution, 1855), measured the southwest mound at 53 feet square at the top and the northwest mound's summit at 60 by 65 feet. Excavation showed the southwest mound built in three to five stages, with a 42-foot-square wooden post structure on its second stage and a charred pole centered below it on the mound top; the northwest mound, excavated in 1954, had three building stages and carried a crematorium surrounded by a wooden post structure on its second stage. The northeast mound was built at one time, over a cleared area that had held a structure 45 by 90 feet, with a similar 45-by-90-foot structure then built on top of it. Houses stood inside and outside the walls — circular houses, rectangular wall-trench houses, a rectangular pit house — excavated in the northeast sector and elsewhere across the 1949–1968 seasons; one house reconstructed in 1972 "was destroyed by vandals shortly thereafter." Lapham's 1850 map showed 44 mounds on the terrace west and north of the stockade; only 10 remained in good condition by Barrett's 1919 excavations, which found large post pits at the centers of the mounds at their bases. The nomination's artifact list runs from grit- and shell-tempered pottery, small triangular points, knives, drills, and scrapers to celts, a stone cup, an ear spool, shell hoes and ornaments, bone awls, antler points, and a discoidal "chunkey" stone.

The recorded history begins with a naming error. The nomination: "Aztalan was first discovered in 1836 with the initial white colonization of the Wisconsin Territory. In that year, it was described by Judge N. F. Hyer in an article in the Chicago American. In 1837, this initial report was amplified in an article in the Milwaukee Advertiser. Hyer named the site Aztalan because he erroneously thought it was the original homeland of the Aztecs who later migrated to Mexico." Lapham's 1850 survey and map — made "before cultivation destroyed any of the site" — became the guide for later reconstruction. Barrett's excavations of 1919, 1920, and 1932 "identified the site as a close relative of Cahokia." The nomination records that the site became State property in 1948 (its scanned text reads "1848"; see §6) and that the Wisconsin Archeological Survey excavated intermittently in 1949, 1950, 1951, 1952, 1954, 1964, 1967, and 1968 "in an attempt to gain enough scientific information for its proper reconstruction and development." Two of the platform mounds and parts of the stockade were reconstructed as the park developed. The DNR records the designations in sequence: the site became a state park in 1948 (the DNR's own news account separates the steps — the DNR bought much of the site in 1948 and the park opened in 1952; see §6), was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964, was listed in the National Register of Historic Places in 1966, and was designated a State Natural Area in 2003.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** Lapham's 1850 survey — a stockade ridge still standing as a measurable earthwork, three platform mounds with the summit dimensions above, and 44 mounds on the terrace west and north of the walls. **Now:** by Barrett's 1919 excavations only 10 of those 44 terrace mounds remained in good condition, and the park visitor sees the southwest and northwest platform mounds and portions of the stockade as reconstructions built on the excavated evidence, with the remaining associated mounds north and west of the fortified village still on the ground (nomination).

**Then:** a town the nomination calls probably "the most populous prehistoric center in the area during the period of its occupation," behind twelve-foot plastered walls with watchtowers. **Now:** a 172-acre state park (DNR) of prairie, river frontage, and a large tract of forest on the Crawfish's east bank, holding a 25-acre designated State Natural Area around the village ground itself (DNR natural-areas record) — the acreage figures measure different things and are printed scoped in §6.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- Portions of the stockade, reconstructed by the Wisconsin Archeological Survey on the lines its excavations established (NPS landmark nomination; DNR park page) — a reconstruction, presented as one
- The southwest and northwest platform mounds, reconstructed (nomination; DNR park page: "Portions of the stockade and two mounds have been reconstructed in the park")
- The third, northeast platform mound's ground — the nomination records the mound itself and its excavation history; the reconstruction the sources name covers the southwest and northwest mounds
- The remaining associated mounds north and west of the fortified village (nomination, continuation sheet) — of the 44 Lapham mapped on the terrace, 10 were in good condition by 1919
- Two miles of trail through open prairie and along the Crawfish River, with views of the reconstructed stockade and mounds (DNR park coverage, 2025 bulletin)
- The Crawfish River frontage and the east-bank forest tract within the 172-acre park (DNR)
- A small zone of the landmark outside the park boundary, part owned by the Aztalan Historical Society and part, at the nomination's writing, for sale, containing one mound (nomination, 1975) — private and society ground, not a stop

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the conditions are the park's: state park hours and the vehicle admission sticker as §1 carries them, the natural-area collecting prohibition, and the park's own trails:

1. **At the park entrance on County Q:** Read the ground before the earthworks — a river terrace on the Crawfish's west bank, chosen, as the DNR records, by people who came up the rivers from the south.
2. **At the reconstructed stockade line:** The wall as Barrett's post molds proved it — wooden poles under clay plaster, twelve feet high, with bastions at intervals; the burnt plaster is what the first observers swore was brick.
3. **At the southwest mound:** The largest platform, 53 feet square at the summit in Lapham's 1850 measurement, built in three to five stages with a 42-foot-square structure on its second stage — read as a reconstruction standing on excavated stages.
4. **Across the plaza to the northwest mound:** Three building stages, and on the second a crematorium inside a post structure — the ceremonial and burial weight of the town, from the ground, without leaving the trail.
5. **At the terrace mounds north and west:** What is left of Lapham's 44 — ten in good condition by 1919 — and the difference between a mapped town and a surviving one.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The occupation dates do not reconcile across the primary sources, and each prints as its source carries it. The DNR's natural-areas page: the complex "flourished between 1000 and 1200 AD," the village "prospered until 1200 AD, after which all the inhabitants left." The DNR's park page: a village "that thrived between A.D. 1000 and 1300." The NPS landmark nomination gives specific dates "approximately 1100-1300 A.D." and an occupation of "about A.D. 1100-1300." No single range is adopted in this entry; §2 is written to the sources' shared substance (a Middle Mississippian town of the centuries around 1000–1300) and the three datings print here unreconciled.

The park's establishment prints two ways inside the DNR's own publications. The natural-areas page: "Aztalan become a state park in 1948." The DNR's news account of the park separates acquisition from opening: the DNR bought much of the site in 1948, historical-society parcels were donated, and "Aztalan State Park was opened" in 1952. The landmark nomination's history sentence, as scanned, reads "In 1848, the site became State property" — against every other source's 1948, and treated in this entry as a transcription artifact of the scanned form, flagged rather than silently corrected. The hours conflict the same way: the DNR park page says the park is "open year-round from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m."; the DNR natural-areas page says "The park is open year-round from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m." Both are the operator's own current pages; §1 prints the park page's hours and this section prints the conflict.

Acreages measure different things and are kept scoped: the walled village, 21 acres (nomination); the nominated landmark, approximately 120 acres (nomination); the state park, 172 acres (DNR); the designated State Natural Area within it, 25 acres (DNR natural-areas record). The discovery story also has an unverified layer: the nomination's account begins with Hyer's 1836 description. The discovery by a young man named Timothy Johnson in December 1835, carried in wide secondary circulation, was not verified in the primary sources checked for this entry and is not printed as fact in §2. The day-level designation dates in general circulation (National Historic Landmark July 19, 1964; National Register listing October 15, 1966) rest on the Register's database record for the property (NRIS 66000022); the nomination form itself is a later Landmark Review Project form dated January 8, 1975, and gives the years only — the years are what §2 prints from it.

Finally, coordinates: the nomination carries a latitude-and-longitude table defining a rectangle around the property, but the figures in the scanned copy checked for this entry are not reliably legible, and no primary source checked carries a single verified point for the site. No coordinates print in §10.
