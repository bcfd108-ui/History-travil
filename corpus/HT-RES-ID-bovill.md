# HT-RES-ID-bovill — The town that moved the logs

Not a mill town — a junction town. Bovill existed to move logs out of the finest white pine stands in North Idaho, and more logging crews shipped out of it than any town around. The prime cut was done by about 1930. The town is still there.

## 2. The story
The town is named for **Hugh Bovill**, an Englishman who built a hotel there in **1899**. The railroad was constructed into Bovill in **1906**, and the town's whole reason came with it. John B. Miller — Bovill's own historian, whose *The Trees Grew Tall* (1972) old-timers regarded as the record — put the distinction plainly in his 1973 oral history: Elk River was a mill town, Potlatch was a mill town, and *“Bovill was primarily a town that was set up to move logs.”* Not owned by the Potlatch Company, but established because of its forests — a junction point for the railroads in, as Miller said, the prime part of the white pine: *“there was none of it that equalled that stands that were around Bovill.”*

More logging crews moved out of Bovill than out of any other town in the region — crews for Elk River and Deary operations shipped from Bovill too. The woods work ran on horses before it ran on machines: Potlatch wintered some **260 head** of draft horses across the meadow from the depot, none under a ton. Miller judged the low point at about **1930**, *“when the very hard logging of the prime timber in the near vicinity of Bovill was finished.”*

The town's memory is unusually well kept: in the mid-1970s the Latah County Historical Society's Oral History Project recorded **more than three hundred interviews** with the logging generation, Bovill heavily among them — now digitized at the University of Idaho. The town counted 305 people in 2000 and 191 in 2020. Small numbers. The archive is not small.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a humming rail junction — horse barns, side tracks to plow out all winter, crews shipping to camps in every direction, a main street of banks, hotels, and pool halls (the street in the photo, before the fire of July 1914 rebuilt it). **Now:** a quiet town of under two hundred where the historic timber heritage still shows in the rail beds of the spur lines that hauled logs out of the woods — you walk the grades the log trains ran.

## 4. What's still there
- Main street itself — stand where the historic photo was taken: the First State Bank corner and the Spokane Hotel block. The photo shows the street before the fire of July 1914 rebuilt it; the record does not establish that the pictured buildings themselves stand today — the corner and the block are the ground
- The old rail beds and spur-line grades in and around town, the physical network that moved the logs
- Village Park, donated by Potlatch Corporation in 1994, and Caroline Park's brick-and-cobblestone 'Billy Walk'
- Up the road: Elk River, the sister mill town — homesteaded 1897, home of the first electric sawmill in the country (1910), its mill closed in 1932 after the bigger Lewiston mill took the work
