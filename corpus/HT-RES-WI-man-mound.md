# HT-RES-WI-man-mound — A horned figure walking west, cut by a road and saved for $300
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a terrace at the north base of the Baraboo Range, in the Town of Greenfield northeast of Baraboo, a horned human figure 214 feet long walks west in earth. The Man Mound is, in the words of its National Historic Landmark nomination, "the only surviving monumental earthen anthropomorphic effigy in North America" — a Late Woodland effigy whose lower legs were cut away by a county road just after its first survey, whose feet were worn level in a cow pasture, and whose survival is owed to a 1906–1907 fundraising appeal that bought the ground for a park dedicated in 1908. The figure from the knees up stands intact in Man Mound County Park; its lost legs are painted white on the pavement of Man Mound Road, and its feet are marked by white plastic cut-outs in the pasture across the road.

## 1. Trip gate
Man Mound Park is a Sauk County park, managed by Sauk County Parks and Recreation. Sauk County's page for the site states: "Picnic tables and benches are provided at this park. There is no entrance fee required at this park." The Wisconsin Historical Society's register record states the visitor terms in full: "The Man Mound may be visited during regular park hours." No source checked for this entry states specific hours, and none are printed here. Sauk County locates the park "northeast of Baraboo, on Man Mound Road," Town of Greenfield. (A numbered street address appears in secondary listings; no primary source checked carries one, so none prints in §10.)

The visitor boundary here is not a fence line; it is the nature of the ground. Sauk County's page states that "the majority of the property is a cataloged burial site monitored by the Wisconsin Historical Society," and that all mound forms in the county "have been found to contain human remains and are protected by state law." The landmark nomination's integrity discussion records that the mound's low-maintenance prairie cover, set against the mown park lawn, "provides a strong contrast in the vegetation that discourages foot traffic on the mound." The visit this entry describes is made from the park ground, the fence line, and the public road — the figure is read from beside it, not walked on. The feet lie on private property: the Wisconsin Historical Society records that the feet of the mound are on private land north of Man Mound Road, in the Weirich family's parcel. They are viewed from the public road, not visited. The painted legs and plastic feet are modern markers of the figure's extent, not the mound itself, and §4 keeps them labeled as such.

## 2. The story
The landmark nomination (Amy Rosebrough, Wisconsin Historical Society, February 28, 2013) places the Man Mound in the Late Woodland effigy mound ceremonial complex of south-central Wisconsin, built about A.D. 750–1200: mounds raised as birds, mammals, water spirits, and — rarely, and here uniquely in survival — a human form. The figure is a horned humanoid, head to the south, its feet originally to the north and pointing west, the whole figure walking westward. The first measurements are Increase Lapham's, printed in his short 1859 article and quoted in the 1978 National Register nomination: total length 214 feet — head 30 feet, body 100 feet, legs 84 feet — with "two projections or horns" on the head. The bronze tablet set at the park in 1908 carries the same measure plus the shoulder width — "Mound located and platted by W.H. Canfield in 1859. Length 214 ft., width at shoulders 48 feet" — and Sauk County's page gives the figure the same 214 by 48 feet. The setting, per the Wisconsin Historical Society, is the floodplain of an intermittent tributary of the Baraboo River at the north base of the North arm of the Baraboo Range.

The mound entered the written record on July 23, 1859, when William H. Canfield — who had come to Sauk County in 1842 and been told of the mound by farmer Abram Hoege — surveyed it. Lapham's 1859 description, quoted in the landmark nomination, still reads as field notes from a man standing on the figure: the shape is "very clearly and decidedly, the human form, in the act of walking, and with an expression of boldness and decision which cannot be mistaken." The damage came almost immediately. The landmark nomination records that a road — now Man Mound Road — was cut east-west through the mound's lower legs "just after Canfield's first visit," about 1859/1861 by the nomination's dating. North of the road, the feet lay in farmland that passed from J. Wing to Ed Barstow to J. T. Palmer and finally to Jacob Weirich, in whose family the parcel remains; there, in the nomination's words, the feet were "worn level with the ground surface by bovine trampling." Other man-shaped mounds recorded in the nineteenth century — a 125-foot figure near La Valle found in 1872, another in Richland County found in 1886 — were destroyed. The Greenfield figure, cut and pastured, was largely forgotten.

In 1905, Harry E. Cole and Arlow B. Stout relocated the mound — just ahead of the plow: owner Alba Hoege was planning to plow the site. Stout took an option on the land, and on December 27, 1906, the goal was set at $300. Julia A. Lapham, Increase Lapham's daughter and landmarks chair of the Federation of Women's Clubs, was a key fundraiser; by July 8, 1907, donations had exceeded the purchase price. On October 12, 1907, the Wisconsin Archeological Society and the Sauk County Historical Society jointly purchased the site from the Hoege family — the landmark nomination notes that legal issues prevented title passing jointly to the Federation of Women's Clubs as first intended. The park was dedicated August 8, 1908, among the first archaeological sites in the United States purchased for preservation. John M. True, accepting the park, called the mound of "the greatest interest and importance of all Wisconsin's celebrated emblematic earthworks."

The park grew eastward in the 1960s (the county acquired the Hoege schoolhouse land in 1964 and a 0.22-acre parcel in 1968; the schoolhouse was sold and stands as a private residence just east of the boundary). The Wisconsin Archeological Society quit-claimed its interest to the Sauk County Historical Society in 1966. The property was listed in the National Register of Historic Places on November 30, 1978 (reference 78000138), and a boundary increase listed April 26, 2010 (reference 10000211) brought the feet land north of the road inside the listed boundary. At the centennial re-dedication on August 9, 2008, where the 1908 ceremony had invoked a Native presence without a Native voice, Ho-Chunk Nation representatives were among the speakers, and Sauk County and the Governor proclaimed "Man Mound Day." The missing legs and feet were marked the same year: the Wisconsin Historical Society records that in 2008 the contours of the feet and legs were painted on the road and pasture using Canfield's original survey measurements — the white painted legs on Man Mound Road and the white plastic cut-outs of the feet in the pasture that a visitor sees today. The property was designated a National Historic Landmark on October 31, 2016. Today most of the landmark is owned by the Sauk County Historical Society, an L-shaped piece east of the parking lot is owned by Sauk County, and the whole is managed by Sauk County Parks and Recreation under lease (Sauk County).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** Canfield's July 23, 1859 plat and Lapham's 1859 measurements — a complete figure, 214 feet from horned head to feet, walking west across an unbroken terrace. **Now:** the figure from the knees to the horned head intact in the park's western half; the lower legs' course carried across Man Mound Road by white paint on the pavement; the feet's ground in the pasture north of the road, its surface form worn level, its outline held by white plastic cut-outs pinned by volunteers, the parcel itself brought into the listed boundary in 2010 (landmark nomination; Wisconsin Historical Society).

**Then:** the 1908 park — the rescued mound, a bronze-and-stone marker, open ground. **Now:** the marker moved at the 2008 centennial re-dedication east of the post-and-rail fence near the entrance, with a newer marker beside it; a small gravel parking lot; the park's eastern half mown lawn and shade trees with playground equipment from the Hoege Schoolhouse at its far east; the landmark boundary drawn at 1.51 acres around the whole figure, including the parts no longer visible in and north of the road (landmark nomination).

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The effigy itself from the knees to the horned head, intact, in the western half of Man Mound Park, kept in low-maintenance prairie grass and clear of trees and shrubbery, separated from the park's lawn by a post-and-rail fence (landmark nomination, 2013)
- The 1908 bronze-and-stone marker, moved at the 2008 centennial re-dedication to east of the fence near the park entrance, carrying the tablet text quoted in §2 (landmark nomination; Wisconsin Historical Society place-file record)
- The white painted legs crossing Man Mound Road and the white plastic cut-outs of the feet in the pasture north of the road — modern representations placed in 2008 from Canfield's survey measurements (Wisconsin Historical Society; landmark nomination)
- The small gravel parking lot east of the fence, from which, the nomination states, "a visitor to the site can stand in the parking area and view the Man Mound in its entirety" (landmark nomination)
- The Hoege Schoolhouse, standing as a private residence just east of the park boundary — context, not a stop (landmark nomination)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the conditions are §1's: regular park hours as the Historical Society states them, no entrance fee per the county, a cataloged burial site read from beside the figure and never walked on, and private land north of the road viewed only from the public road:

1. **At the parking area east of the fence:** Take the whole figure in first, as the landmark nomination frames the view — the critical perspective on the mound is from this side, across the fence.
2. **At the 1908 marker:** Read the tablet — Canfield's 1859 plat, 214 feet, 48 feet at the shoulders — then look back at the grass figure and find the horns, the head, the body's long line west.
3. **Along the fence line:** The figure from the knees to the head, in prairie grass against mown lawn — the contrast the nomination says is maintained to keep feet off the mound, and the reason the form reads at all from ground level.
4. **At Man Mound Road:** The white painted legs on the pavement — where the road took the figure's lower legs in the years just after 1859, drawn back in 2008 from Canfield's own measurements.
5. **From the road, looking north:** The white cut-out feet in the Weirich pasture — private ground, a cataloged part of the landmark since 2010, and the end of the visit: the figure whole again in outline, and none of it under your feet.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The National Register listing year prints two ways inside the landmark record itself. The Wisconsin Historical Society's register record for the property and the landmark nomination's own previous-documentation section both give the original listing as November 30, 1978, reference 78000138 — the date §2 prints. The landmark nomination's narrative history, however, states the mound "was listed on the National Register of Historic Places… in 1977." The narrative figure is treated here as an error in the nomination text, printed in this section only rather than silently dropped. A second Historical Society record carries the later boundary-increase designations (State Register July 17, 2009; National Register April 26, 2010), which §2 prints as the boundary increase's dates, not the original listing's.

The superlative prints at two scopes. The landmark nomination: "the only surviving monumental earthen anthropomorphic effigy in North America." The Wisconsin Historical Society's register summary: "the only surviving earthen anthropomorphic effigy mound in the Upper Midwest." Each is printed where it is used, scoped as its source scopes it; this entry does not merge them. The road's date likewise stays approximate: the nomination ties the cut to "just after Canfield's first visit" and to about 1859/1861, while Sauk County's page says only that road construction destroyed the legs "in the 1800s" — and the county page attributes the feet's loss to "agricultural use" where the nomination specifies cattle trampling in pasture. The wordings print as their sources carry them. The 1908 dedication carries a one-day split: the Historical Society's place-file record gives the bronze tablet's unveiling as August 7, 1908, while the nomination and the register summary date the dedication ceremony August 8, 1908; §2 prints the ceremony date and the split prints here. The park's size also prints two ways in county material — the landmark nomination fixes the landmark boundary at 1.51 acres, while the county's master property plan describes "a small 1.78 acre park" — different scopes (landmark boundary against whole park), both printed, neither adopted as the other's correction.

Two further gaps stand open. The nomination mentions a small wooden viewing tower east of the mound's right arm in the late 1970s, in the past tense; no source checked establishes whether any viewing structure stands today, and none is claimed in §4. And the feet: both the nomination and the Historical Society agree the surface form is gone — worn level — while the location is documented and landmark-listed; any claim that the feet "survive" below the plow zone is an inference the sources checked do not make, and this entry does not make it.
