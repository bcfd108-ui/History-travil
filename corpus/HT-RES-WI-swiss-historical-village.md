# HT-RES-WI-swiss-historical-village — Fourteen buildings on a colony woodlot, keeping the Swiss colony's own record
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

In 1845 an emigration society in Canton Glarus, Switzerland, sent two men ahead to buy land and then sent a colony after them — 1,200 acres in Green County, Wisconsin, bought that July, and a settlement that kept its language, its church, and its cheesemaking for generations. A century later the colonists' descendants built a museum village on four acres of the colony's own woodlot to keep the record themselves. That ground — fourteen buildings, one of them a genuine 1850–51 settler's cabin that spent a hundred years hidden inside another house — is this entry.

## 1. Trip gate
The Swiss Historical Village is a working museum, and the visit is bounded by the operator's terms exactly as the New Glarus Historical Society states them. The Society's own page (checked November 26, 2026) gives the 2026 season as May 23 to October 11, 2026, closing at its fall fest; hours Tuesday–Sunday, 10 a.m.–4 p.m., last guided tour 3:15 p.m., a full tour about an hour and a half. The same page states a regular adult price of $12 and a $10 group price; both print here as the operator states them, dated to this check, and no other fee is asserted. Outside the stated season and hours the ground is a closed museum, not a public park, and this entry directs no one onto it then.

Within the gate, the Society states its own access terms: a main entrance ramp, and "accessible access to all but two of our 14 buildings." Two of the fourteen buildings are therefore not accessible even by the operator's own account. The ground is four acres near the village's western edge (Freitag, §9 [4]), surrounded by a living village whose streets, church, and cemetery are the community's own and not part of the stop. One status point belongs in the gate: the museum ground carries no National Register listing. The Wisconsin Historical Society's Architecture and History Inventory record for the property (HI230375) states "Not listed" for both registers; the listed Swiss property in New Glarus is the Chalet of the Golden Fleece, a different building across the village and a different entry's subject.

## 2. The story
The colony was an organized act, not a drift. The New Glarus Historical Society's timeline records that in 1845 Appeals Judge Niklaus Dürst and blacksmith Fridolin Streiff, sent by the Emigration Society of Canton Glarus, searched Illinois, Missouri, Iowa, and Wisconsin and purchased 1,200 acres in Green County on July 17, with an additional 80 acres of woodland bought for the colony. In August, 131 of the 193 colonists who had left Glarus in April arrived at "New" Glarus. The difference between those two numbers is not explained by the timeline and is printed as a gap in §6.

The first winter's arithmetic was hard. The Society's timeline records that a territorial census taken in 1846, in preparation for statehood, showed only 13 homes and about 85 people remaining in the colony. John Jacob Tschudy arrived from Glarus in 1846 to help Streiff manage the colony. A second group, from the Canton Glarus village of Bilten, arrived in July 1847 and settled south of the village in what became known as the Biltental. In 1850 the residents organized the Town of New Glarus in the log schoolhouse built the year before, and the Rev. Wilhelm Streissguth arrived and organized a Reformed congregation that used the schoolhouse as its first church. The ten-year emigration agreement ended in 1855; the colony agents completed sales of all the land, the last deed issued in January 1856. The congregation built its first permanent church building in 1858 on land reserved in the center of the village.

The colony's second act was cheese. The timeline records that in 1868 Niklaus Gerber developed the area's first cheese factory southwest of New Glarus, making limburger, and the following year began making Emmentaler-style cheese on the Nic Freitag farm between New Glarus and Monticello. The railroad — the Milwaukee and Saint Paul's Albany branch — reached New Glarus in 1887 and became, the timeline notes, one of the line's most profitable branches on milk shipments.

The museum was a Depression-era act of memory. Freitag's history (2022) traces it to late-1930s discussions that produced a county historical society and the idea of ethnic museums in the county; the Swiss of New Glarus acted first, setting up their own society informally — the Society's timeline dates its beginning to 1938, "as an outgrowth of the Green County Historical Society," its purpose "to develop a historical village museum to preserve items from the Swiss colony." A key early figure was Esther Stauffacher, a descendant of original colonists, who hosted visitors at her farm and kept the small organization alive.

Jacob Rieder, a local architect who had emigrated from Chur in 1913, drew a concept plan of nine log cabins. The site was four open acres near the village's western edge, once a colony woodlot; the owners were heirs of Gabriel Schindler, the village held title through a tax settlement, and the village granted the group a 100-year lease (Freitag). The first cabin, built with donated logs and native limestone despite the war, was dedicated on September 27, 1942 — a reproduction of a Swiss log home, as the Wisconsin Historical Society's inventory record states plainly (HI230375: "THIS WAS THE FIRST BUILDING ON THE MUSEUM COMPLEX AS A REPRODUCTION OF A SWISS LOG HOME BY JACOB RIEDER"). It was known as the "community building," and Freitag corrects the belief it invited: the colonists sheltered under a bowery until their first cabins went up in the fall of 1845, and those cabins were much smaller. Stauffacher served as the one-cabin museum's curator until her death in 1945; banker J. J. Figi carried the role after her. The society itself lapsed in 1947 and was reorganized in 1956 (Freitag).

Growth after that was one building at a time. A second log building, a replica of the colony's first schoolhouse — which had also served town meetings and church services — was dedicated September 7, 1952, funded principally by the Wilhelm Tell Community Guild; displayed as the church, it holds a communion pitcher and chalice made by a tinsmith who was an original 1845 colonist. A cheese factory building opened in 1961, set up like the old cooperative factories and equipped with items from the area's first Swiss cheese factory, much of the equipment collected and donated by the Monroe folk artist Carl Marty. Buildings followed for blacksmith tools, for farm machinery, and for the volunteer fire department's old equipment. After Wisconsin ended its one-room rural schools, the society obtained two of the buildings — one became a general store, the other remained a schoolhouse, later moved again for the 1979 entrance building. A print shop building followed in 1971, housing the New Glarus newspaper's whole shop, donated after the paper modernized in 1967.

The ground's one genuine colony dwelling arrived by accident. A 14-by-16-foot log cabin built on Swiss colony land in 1850–51 had been swallowed by additions into a frame house; when the house was torn down in 1975 the cabin was rediscovered inside it, recognized by museum volunteer Jacob Tschudy, dismantled under his supervision, and reconstructed at the village in 1976, donated by Harold Hoesly, on whose farm it was found (Freitag). The Hall of History, at the center of the complex, was essentially a gift of the people of Canton Glarus — designed by three Swiss architects with local architect R. Wayne Duerst, opened in 1969 and dedicated in August 1970 during the colony's 125th anniversary, before some 250 visitors from Switzerland; its first exhibit was the Glarus textile industry, the industry whose collapse had prompted the emigration. A Swiss-style bee house was built in 1994 to hold Gottfried Barth's Swiss beekeeping equipment. The count stands where the operator puts it today: fourteen buildings.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** 193 colonists left Glarus in April 1845; 131 arrived that August (Society timeline); by the 1846 territorial census the colony counted 13 homes and about 85 people. **Now:** the colony's ground is a village that calls itself America's Little Switzerland, and the four acres where this entry stands are a museum run by the colonists' own historical society — the record kept by descendants, later immigrants, and newcomers alike (Freitag).

**Then:** the first shelter was a bowery, and the first cabins, built in the fall of 1845, were much smaller than legend later made them (Freitag). **Now:** the museum's first building, the big 1942 "community building," is a reproduction — and the real scale of the first years survives in the 14-by-16-foot settler's cabin of 1850–51, rediscovered inside a frame house in 1975 and moved to the ground in 1976.

**Then:** cheese began in farm kitchens and a first factory in 1868. **Now:** the museum's 1961 cheese factory building demonstrates the cooperative-factory stage with the first factory's own equipment, and a small wheel of cheese is still made in it during the annual fall festival (Freitag).

**Then:** a one-cabin museum open Sunday afternoons, April through September, with Esther Stauffacher as curator (Freitag). **Now:** fourteen buildings on a published season — in 2026, late May to mid-October, six days a week (operator).

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The museum ground itself: four acres near the village's western edge, once a Swiss colony woodlot (Freitag, 2022), at 612 7th Avenue (Wisconsin Historical Society AHI record HI230375; operator)
- Fourteen buildings, per the operator's current page (checked November 26, 2026)
- The Pioneer Cabin of 1942 — the museum's first building, a reproduction of a Swiss log home by architect Jacob Rieder (WHS AHI HI230375, surveyed 2015)
- The settler's log cabin, 14 by 16 feet, built on colony land in 1850–51, rediscovered in 1975 inside a demolished frame house and reconstructed on the ground in 1976, furnished as an early colony home (Freitag)
- The log church — the 1952 replica of the colony's first schoolhouse — with 41 real pioneer gravestones from the old cemetery (the Alten Friedhof, now the front lawn of the Swiss United Church of Christ) displayed in front of it; four of the stones are original 1845 colonists', the oldest dated 1853 (Freitag)
- The cheese factory building (opened 1961), fitted as a cooperative factory with equipment from the area's first Swiss cheese factory (Freitag)
- The print shop (building constructed 1971) holding the New Glarus newspaper's complete shop — type cases, a Linotype, a Civil War-era flat-bed press (Freitag)
- The Hall of History (opened 1969, dedicated August 1970), the gift of Canton Glarus, now carrying the emigration story and a panorama of the Linth River valley (Freitag; Society timeline)
- The bee house (built 1994) holding Gottfried Barth's Swiss-style hives, ordered from a Swiss catalog for his own 1924 bee house (Freitag)
- The blacksmith, farm machinery, and fire equipment buildings; the general store and schoolhouse in two moved one-room school buildings; the Swiss-style entrance building of 1979 (Freitag)
- The carved maple ibex, part of Switzerland's exhibit at Chicago's 1893 Columbian Exposition, now displayed in the museum's entry building (Freitag)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the progression stays on the museum ground, inside the operator's season and hours as §1 bounds them:

1. **At the Hall of History:** Start where the tours start (Freitag) — the emigration itself: the Glarus textile collapse of the 1840s, the agents' purchase, the crossing. The building is the old country's gift to the new one; read it that way.
2. **At the 1942 Pioneer Cabin:** The museum's first building — and a reproduction. Hold it against the inventory record's plain wording before believing anything it seems to say about 1845.
3. **At the settler's cabin:** The genuine article, 14 by 16 feet, built 1850–51 and hidden inside another house for a century. The size difference between this and the 1942 cabin is the first correction the ground offers.
4. **At the log church and its gravestones:** The replica church is a copy; the 41 stones in front of it are not — they are the old cemetery's real markers, four of them colonists of 1845.
5. **At the cheese factory:** The colony's second act — cooperative dairying — set up with the first factory's equipment. If the visit falls on the fall festival, a wheel is made here (Freitag).
6. **Through the print shop, schoolhouse, general store, and bee house:** The small-town machinery of the colony's maturity — a newspaper's whole shop, a one-room school, a Swiss bee house.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The colonist count does not reconcile inside the Society's own timeline. It records 193 colonists leaving Glarus in April 1845 and 131 arriving in August, and offers no accounting for the 62-person difference — no cause for it is documented in the sources checked, so it prints here as unexplained. The 1846 territorial census figure in the same timeline — about 85 people in 13 homes — deepens rather than resolves the question, and no attempt is made in this entry to harmonize the three numbers.

The Hall of History carries two dedication dates. The Society's timeline enters the building under 1969 as dedicated that year. Freitag's article says the building opened in 1969 and "was dedicated in August 1970 when the community was celebrating the 125th anniversary of its founding." Both forms print; §2 and §4 carry "opened 1969, dedicated August 1970" as Freitag's fuller account gives it, with the timeline's 1969 dedication noted here.

The end of the one-room schools is dated two ways: the Society's timeline records the last of them closing under state mandate under 1961, while Freitag writes that the society obtained its two school buildings "[a]fter Wisconsin ended the use of one-room rural schools in 1962." The one-year difference is printed, not settled.

The 1942 cabin's identity needs the correction Freitag himself makes, because the building invites the error: it was "known originally as the 'community building,' [and] some thought it was like the first structure that housed the colonists. That was not true." The Wisconsin inventory record independently classifies it as a reproduction. Any description of the village that presents the 1942 cabin as an original settler structure is wrong on both records checked, and this entry does not repeat it.

The society's own founding is soft at the edges: the timeline dates its beginning to 1938 as an outgrowth of the county society, while Freitag describes late-1930s discussions and the Swiss setting up their own society "informally." The formal organization also lapsed in 1947 and was reorganized in 1956 (Freitag) — a fact the timeline does not carry. The 100-year lease on the museum ground is Freitag's account of the arrangement with the Village of New Glarus; the operator's current pages checked for this entry do not restate the lease, and its present status is not asserted here. Attendance figures sometimes quoted for the village — a peak of about 40,000 visitors a year in the 1970s and 1980s — come from Freitag's article and print nowhere in this entry as current fact. Finally, the register status: the museum property is not listed in the National Register (WHS AHI HI230375), and the often-photographed Chalet of the Golden Fleece, which is listed, is a separate property at 618 Second Street and no part of this entry.
