# Apple River Fort State Historic Site — the lead-country fort Black Hawk attacked on a Sunday afternoon
HT-RES-IL-apple-river-fort · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The ground was Sauk and Meskwaki country before it was a fort, and the site's own telling starts there. The conflict that produced the fort had its roots in the 1804 Treaty of St. Louis, in which, on the state site's account, Sauk and Fox leaders ceded 50 million acres to the United States for $2,234.50 plus a $1,000 yearly annuity, with the tribes permitted to remain on the land until it was sold. The discovery of lead in the Galena country in the 1820s brought miners and settlers in force. At the Apple River settlement — the community that became Elizabeth — miners built a cluster of cabins and opened the first general store in 1827. When the Sauk and Fox returned from their winter hunt early in 1829, they found settlers living in their villages; later that year the land was offered for sale and the Sauk and Fox were forced west across the Mississippi. That sequence — treaty, lead rush, return to occupied villages, removal — is the war's cause as the site itself states it, and the dossier keeps it at the front where the site puts it. [1, 3]

Black Hawk, a Sauk leader who had fought with the British against the United States in the War of 1812, held that the land remained his people's. On April 5, 1832 (April 6 in the site history's war dates), he came up the Rock River with a band the sources number at about 500 warriors and some 700 women, children and old people. Governor John Reynolds called out the Illinois militia. On May 14, Black Hawk's warriors routed the militia at Stillman's Run, and the Black Hawk War had begun. A young Abraham Lincoln served in the militia that summer; the state site records that Lincoln and his company camped near the Apple River site on June 9, before the battle. Settlers across the mining country panicked — many left Galena by steamboat — and at the Apple River settlement the miners quickly built a fort around their existing cabins in May 1832, adding a palisade and a second story to a blockhouse, retreating inside at night or at the first sign of danger. [1, 3]

The attack came on Sunday, June 24, 1832. The site's history page gives the warning its human form: four riders — Frederick Dixon, George W. Harkleroad, Edmund Welch and J. Kirkpatrick — carrying dispatches for General Henry Atkinson stopped at the fort for refreshments, rode on, and about 500 yards away were fired on, one man taking a bullet in the thigh; they got back to the fort and raised the alarm. Black Hawk himself, with some 200 warriors, then attacked — the only fort Black Hawk personally attacked in the war, on both the state site's and the site history's accounts. Inside were roughly 45 people. The men kept up a steady fire while the women molded musket balls, loaded weapons and rolled cartridges, and the children helped mold balls. Elizabeth Armstrong, one of at least three women named Elizabeth in the settlement, played a prominent role in organizing and rallying the women and children inside. After about 45 minutes, Black Hawk, believing the fort more heavily armed than it was, broke off the attack and raided nearby cabins for supplies as his band departed. The site history counts the fort's loss as one death and two wounded, and no losses on Black Hawk's side. A local telling holds that the village of Elizabeth was named for the Elizabeths of that day; the site history states it only as "is said to be," and the dossier files it exactly that way — as a telling, not a documented naming. [1, 3]

The war's end was not a battle in any equal sense, and the sources do not pretend otherwise. Black Hawk's band, hungry and shrinking, was pursued across Wisconsin to the Mississippi at the mouth of the Bad Axe River, where on August 2 the band was caught between the steamboat *Warrior* and Army troops. The state site's figures are stark: of the 1,200 who had started out with Black Hawk, only about 150 survived. The site history names the ending plainly the "Battle (or Massacre) of Bad Axe" and dates the war's end August 27 — a date conflict with the state site's August 2, printed in Gaps and not merged. Black Hawk escaped, was later captured, and the cessions that followed ended Sauk and Meskwaki residence east of the Mississippi in Illinois. The fort itself saw no second attack. It stood until about 1847, when it was torn down and its lumber used to build a barn. [1, 3]

The modern site is archaeology before it is reconstruction. In 1994 the Apple River Fort Historic Foundation was established to locate the original fort; in 1995 its work found the site, and excavation revealed the fort's complete footprint and a multitude of artifacts. The fort was reconstructed near the original foundations — the site's pages give 1996 for the reconstruction in one place and 1997–98 in another, printed in Gaps — and an Interpretive Center followed. The original fort site, not the replica, is what the National Register lists: entered November 7, 1997 under criteria for event and information potential, with significance in military history, agriculture, and historic non-aboriginal archaeology, the significant year 1832. The nomination places the site on a prominent knoll near a spring along what was once the Kellogg Trail, a short distance east of the present village, with the Apple River to the north. The State of Illinois took over operation in 2001 and administers it as the Apple River Fort State Historic Site. The Register protects the ground and what remains in it; the replica beside it is interpretation, and the dossier keeps the two distinct throughout. [2, 3, 5, 6, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The Apple River Fort Site occupies a knoll in and beside the village of Elizabeth, Jo Daviess County, a quarter mile east-southeast of the junction of Myrtle and Illinois Streets in the Register record's form — the same ground the operator reaches as 311 East Myrtle Street for the Interpretive Center, with the reconstructed fort a quarter mile further along Old Fort Road. The historic settlement stood here because the lead country, the Kellogg Trail and the Apple River met here; the village of Elizabeth is the settlement's continuation, not a separate place. [2, 4, 5, 6]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Apple River Fort State Historic Site — Interpretive Center, 311 East Myrtle St., Elizabeth, IL 61028 (the visit's start, parking, restrooms and exhibits), and the reconstructed fort reached from it on foot along the sidewalk and Old Fort Road or by car to the turnabout, in site hours only. The fort is self-guided; its buildings are read through doorway viewing gates, and interiors open only on special-event days or scheduled group tours. The archaeological site itself is Register-listed ground: stay on the site's paths and roads, and treat any ground off them as the protected record it is. The Casper-Koester Cabin on the path — an original 1840s cabin moved here from south of Scales Mound — is exterior only; its interior is under development and not open. [1, 2, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Begin where the site tells you to begin: the Interpretive Center on Myrtle Street, in hours. Take the exhibits in their own order — Sauk and Fox, early miners, the conflict between the two — and watch the film, because the Center's discipline is the visit's discipline: the war had causes, they are stated here plainly, and the fort makes no sense without them. Read the archaeology exhibits closely; the 1995 dig is the reason anything accurate stands up the road, and the artifacts are the original fort's surviving testimony. Then walk the quarter mile — sidewalk, then the tree-lined Old Fort Road, past the Casper-Koester Cabin read correctly as a moved 1840s neighbor, not fort fabric — reading the trail panels on Lincoln's June 9 camp, the fort's building, and the June 24 attack. At the fort, take the self-guided circuit slowly. Stand inside the palisade and do the arithmetic the defenders did: some 45 people, women molding balls and loading, children rolling cartridges, one Elizabeth organizing the inside while 200 warriors pressed the walls — and then do the other arithmetic, the one the Center gave you: a band that had come up the Rock River 1,200 strong in April to reclaim its land, six weeks from Bad Axe. **Strongest story moment · Forty-five minutes on a Sunday afternoon.** Four dispatch riders came back bleeding with the warning, and a lead-miners' stockade held because the people inside it — named women at the center of the work, not at its edge — loaded faster than fear. The fort's fame is earned. It is also one hour inside a war whose other hours, Stillman's Run to Bad Axe, ended a people's residence in their own country, and this ground asks the visitor to hold both without scoring either. Leave by Old Fort Road in site hours; outside them, there is no lawful visit here beyond the public street in Elizabeth. [1, 2, 3]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- War-end date conflict, printed in full and not merged: the state site states the Black Hawk War ended August 2, when Black Hawk's band was caught between the steamboat *Warrior* and Army troops at the Bad Axe. The site's own history page dates the war April 6 to August 27, 1832, and names its ending the "Battle (or Massacre) of Bad Axe." August 2 is the Bad Axe date in the standard record; what August 27 marks is not explained on the page checked this run. The dossier prints both and dates the war's violent end to Bad Axe without adopting August 27. [1, 3]
- War-start date variance: the state site's narrative has Black Hawk starting up the Rock River on April 5, 1832; the site history's date range begins the war April 6, 1832. One day; both printed.
- Casualty-count variance: the site history counts, of the 45 or so people inside the fort, one death and two wounded, and no losses among Black Hawk's band. Other accounts of the same fight in circulation give one killed and one wounded inside the fort. The state site page checked this run gives no casualty figure for the fort's defenders. The dossier follows the site history's one-and-two as the operator's own current telling and prints the variance here; no militia return or contemporary report was retrieved this run to settle it.
- Battle-duration variance: the state site and the site history both give about 45 minutes. Accounts in wider circulation give about an hour. The dossier uses the operator's 45 minutes and prints the variance.
- Reconstruction-date conflict, printed in full: the site history states that in 1996 the fort was reconstructed near the original foundations. The site visitor page states the replica was constructed in 1997–1998. The Foundation page dates the Foundation's establishment to 1994 and its locating work to the period before reconstruction. A 1996 start with 1997–98 completion would reconcile the two, but no source checked this run states that sequence; both dates stand as printed. [2, 3, 7]
- Film-length variance: the state site describes a 15-minute video; the site visitor page describes a dramatic 16-minute film, played on request. Both printed; the difference is one minute and one adjective.
- Village-naming telling, filed as lore: the claim that Elizabeth was named for the Elizabeths of the fort is stated by the site history only in the form "is said to be named in honor of the multiple Elizabeths." No naming record was retrieved this run. It is carried in the Story as a telling, exactly as sourced, and is not asserted as the documented origin of the village name. [3]
- Treaty characterization: the state site and the site history both present the 1804 Treaty of St. Louis cession (50 million acres; $2,234.50 plus $1,000 annuity) as controversial, and the state site states the remain-until-sold term. The dossier follows the operator's own framing. The deeper documentary dispute — the authority of the 1804 delegation — is beyond the sources checked this run and is left as a stated gap rather than summarized from lead-finder accounts.
- Address-form variance, not a conflict: the Register record locates the site as 0.25 miles east-southeast of the junction of Myrtle and Illinois Streets (describing the archaeological site); the operator's visitor address is the Interpretive Center at 311 East Myrtle Street. The two describe different points of the same site, a quarter mile apart, and the gate uses both in that order. [2, 4, 5]
