# INFINITY and the Mississippi test work behind Apollo

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP24 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
In October 1961 NASA chose Hancock County for tests of the launch vehicle that would carry Apollo missions. The agency commissioned Mississippi Test Operations and the Army Corps of Engineers opened a real-estate office in 1962. William C. Fortune was named the initial manager. Workers began clearing the site in May 1963 and Jackson Balch took over as director in 1965. These records track how the test facility came into being through land negotiations, physical construction and work that continued between launch headlines. [3, 4]

The site changed existing communities. NASA records the names of settlements that preceded it and identifies Logtown postmaster Lollie Bell Wright beside owner Roy Baxter when the local post office flag came down for the last time. Senator John C. Stennis’s recollection, reproduced by NASA, says, “the federal government assured the people of Hancock County and Mississippi that the facility would be used.” His statement records the federal promise as he understood it. It cannot stand in for each displaced household’s view of the transfer. [4, 5]

The facility’s first Saturn V test on April 23, 1966 involved the S-II-T, a second-stage test article. The archival photograph shows a huge cloud beyond the observers and a distant structure. Engineers and test crews had to demonstrate how large hardware behaved on the ground before it was committed to flight. Later photographs in NASA’s history show preparation of a first stage in 1967. That is another stage and another date. [3, 4]

The modern museum gives the visitor a way to confront the scale of the earlier work without entering the test installation. Its space gallery overlooks a Saturn V first stage. The museum attributes the article to the canceled Apollo 19 mission. It separately displays the Apollo 4 command module, from the first full Saturn V flight. The photographed test article, the outdoor stage seen from inside, and the flown command module are three distinct things. [3, 6]

## 3. Then and now
The historic installation remains an operating NASA center. The storefront for this visit is the separately operated INFINITY center. The historic test landscape and the museum’s indoor view document different parts of the history. The operator gives a modern interpretation path that includes the impact on local residents. No abandoned settlement, control gate, test structure or shore is part of the visit. [1–6]

## 5. Public progression
Start at the museum’s staffed public entrance. Use the indoor interpretation of land and workforce, then follow Apollo’s path through the gallery to the Apollo 4 module when displayed. End at **The Stage Beyond the Window**. Compare the enormous flight hardware outside the glass with the people and test stand in the 1966 image. It is a difference in stages, purpose and setting as well as size. [1–6, 8]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- NASA’s history separates the first photographed S-II-T second-stage test from the subsequent first-stage work. The display’s label for its canceled-mission stage is the museum’s attribution. Its paperwork and exact relationship to mission scheduling remain open. [3, 4, 6]
- NASA’s archival summary of acquisition, its recollection from Stennis, and early construction dates carry different kinds of evidence. Full deeds, family statements and compensation documentation were not read. No all-households judgment follows from one senator’s recollection. [3–5]
- The older sweep’s general “NASA tour ground” lead does not describe present public access. The current federal visitor page states there are no public tours of Stennis. [2]
