## 2. The story
The strongest artifact here is not the reassuring number on a memorial. It is a handwritten record whose wording is less tidy than the story later built around it.

OHS catalogs **George W. LeBreton's May 2, 1843 meeting record**, **PTD R76I12186**. Its introductory essay identifies the familiar **52–50** as a later historians' estimate. [2] The linked scan's first page was visually inspected: after an initial motion failed and confusion followed, participants divided for a count. The manuscript then says **“a great majority being found in favor of organization”**. It supplies no numerical tally in that passage. This is a transcription of the displayed handwriting, not an assertion about the physical manuscript's chain of custody. [3, p. 1]

That difference is the interpretive hinge: evidence for agreement to organize a settler government is not proof of the later precise count, and neither establishes that this vote transferred Oregon to the United States.

OPRD's own historical monograph explicitly corrects the national-origin claim in the legislation funding the monument: the provisional government had no legal connection to the United States. This correction deserves equal prominence with the monument, not a footnote hidden from visitors. [4, p. 6, note 1]

The land's history also does not begin with the people taking that vote. In the Grand Ronde Tribe's newspaper, **Smoke Signals**, staff writer **Nicole Montesano** reports Tribal Elder and Cultural Policy Analyst **Greg Archuleta's** January 15, 2026 presentation. He explains Champoeg through **“place of yampah,”** and describes the Tribe ensuring yampah was included in landscape restoration. The article describes first-food practices continuing today. This is a named Tribal voice published by the Tribe's newspaper, not OPRD speaking for Tribal families. [6]

**Interpretive position:** tell a significant settler-government event without calling the place previously ungoverned, treating Indigenous people as a vanished introduction, or presenting a memorial as a verbatim contemporary record. The restoration story belongs in the present tense. It is not an invitation to locate or harvest plants.

## 3. Then / now
- **1843:** the meeting record and later accounts concern provisional political organization. Read the actual wording before repeating the numerical legend. [2–3]
- **1901 / 1918 / 1922:** monument, Memorial Building and added pavilion are successive acts of commemoration, not surviving meeting-room fabric from 1843. [4, pp. 1–2]
- **1915–1919:** Theodore Gegoux painted *The Inception of the Birth of Oregon*. Its much later date matters: it interprets the event rather than documenting an eyewitness view. [4, p. 6]
- **2026:** Archuleta's public account places Tribal knowledge and restoration in an ongoing relationship with this landscape. [6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
A conditional interpretive sequence, not a navigational route:
1. **Start with the document**, online or at a confirmed-open public visitor facility. Show the manuscript passage and its date. Ask what it actually records, not which side a visitor would join.
2. **Add the memorial layer.** If operator-confirmed access permits, view interpretation at the public memorial precinct; otherwise use the published image. Compare the monument's later certainty with the record's wording. Do not reenact the division or vote.
3. **End with the living place.** Read the short, attributed Archuleta account back at the visitor center or remotely. The strongest final moment is the realization that political commemoration is one layer, while a Tribal relationship with this place continues. No garden search is needed to make that point.

Do not make riverbank access necessary for the story to land. If weather or closures prevent step 2, the document-and-image comparison preserves the complete argument.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **52–50 versus the record:** live park text repeats 52–50; OHS calls it a later estimate; the inspected manuscript passage says a great majority. Print this discrepancy, not an invented reconciliation. [1–3]
- **“First government” language:** the operator's promotional shorthand must not erase Indigenous governance. This dossier uses *settler provisional government*. [1, 6]
- **Annexation mythology:** the operator monograph itself rejects the monument-funding legislation's claim. [4]
- **Current access:** no observed field conditions, independently confirmed November museum hours or current painting display. No blanket all-clear for flood-prone ground.
- **Missing scholarship:** this pass did not independently collate every later participant recollection or determine a defensible exact vote count. No confident alternative number supplied.
- **Community authority:** one published Tribal voice is not comprehensive consultation or permission to represent every associated community.
