## 2. The story
A general strike sounds like nothing happening. The interesting human question is who does the work needed to make stopping work possible: who feeds a family, cleans hospital linen and decides which deliveries cannot stop?

Seattle's February 6–11, 1919 solidarity strike grew from the shipyard dispute. The University of Washington's research project describes elected strike coordination, food service, essential deliveries and disagreements over ending the action. Its account also distinguishes the union movement's interpretation from Mayor Ole Hanson's revolutionary-threat framing. Neither a romantic story of effortless unity nor a simple story of disorder captures those conflicts. [2]

**Contemporaneous participant record:** The History Committee of the General Strike Committee issued *The Seattle General Strike*, printed by the Seattle Union Record Publishing Co. in 1919. Its first numbered page names **May Young, Waitresses**, as chair and **Anna Louise Strong** as historian, alongside workers from several trades. The scanned page was visually inspected. This is a collective labor account, not Strong's solitary memoir or a neutral official finding. [3–4]

The heading **“Feeding the People”** matters: the committee described hospital laundry, milk distribution and meals as deliberate organizing. Its account admits that excessive kitchen milk orders created losses for the drivers' union. Cooperation included practical mistakes and costs, not only slogans. Printed pages 40–42 were inspected through extracted scan text; the OCR is imperfect and no precise loss amount is adopted here. [3]

May Young belongs in the story as a named waitress and history-committee chair. That title does not establish that she staffed a particular pictured kitchen. Do not invent her shift, a menu she cooked or her presence at this modern park.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** A network of workplaces, union rooms, dining stations, newspapers and households sustained and argued about the strike. It was not a single dramatic confrontation at one monument. The UW project locates organizing at the contemporary Labor Temple but provides no public access permission to any successor property. [2]

**Now:** Occidental Square is a later public space. Seattle Parks dates construction of the present park to **1971**. Its trees, benches and present public use can prompt attention to the labor that keeps a city working, but they are not surviving strike infrastructure. [1]

Use the square as an explicitly modern reading vantage. The interpretive comparison is between routine urban services today and the deliberate choices described in 1919, not a fabricated then-and-now match.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional sequence within open public space, not a route or activated tour:
1. Confirm the park is open and choose a permitted place to pause without blocking others.
2. Read the strike's dates and distinguish this modern vantage from an original event location.
3. Meet May Young through the committee page. Ask whose everyday work makes a collective action possible.
4. Read the food-service passage with its explicit participant perspective and admitted practical difficulty.
5. **Strongest ending:** Look at ordinary city activity and consider the difference between withdrawing labor and abandoning care. The historical account makes that a real organizing problem, not an abstract slogan.

The question is interpretive, not an instruction to photograph workers or question strangers. If access is closed, read the sources remotely. No reenactment, protest organization or special event has been arranged.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Duration:** February 6–11 spans six calendar dates; descriptions calling it five days may count elapsed time or exclude partial days. Preserve the dates rather than manufacture agreement on a duration label. [2–3]
- **Perspective:** The 1919 pamphlet is participant advocacy. Its judgments about success and employers require corroboration, not automatic adoption. [3–4]
- **Named person:** May Young's committee role is primary evidence; a fuller biography, direct interview and identified workplace have not been established here.
- **Late recollection:** UW's oral-history index describes 1977 interviews, including people who participated in or remembered the strike. Those are retrospective sources, not 1919 recordings; no individual full audio was listened to in this pass. [5]
- **Solidarity and exclusion:** A committee pamphlet cannot stand for every resident or worker. The UW research collection identifies separate studies on race, women workers and gender hierarchy; those are research leads, not proof that all unions were inclusive. [2]
- **Place:** No original strike function is assigned to the modern square. No historic photograph matched to today's public vantage.
- **Access:** Published park hours are verified; temporary closures and field conditions remain a visit-day check. [1]
