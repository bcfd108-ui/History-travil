## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** the scenery did not cook breakfast. Tourism history becomes more revealing when the visitor's experience is placed beside the household work that made it possible. A spectacular landscape may attract a guest; food, warmth and somebody's time make the stay work.

**Named primary voice:** Eva Topping's recollection appears in “Voices of JH Women: Highlights from the JHHSM Oral History Collection,” by Marnie Paulus and Nora DeWitt-Hoeger, in the museum's *Chronicle*, Volume 39, Issue 1, April 2019, pages 6–7. The printed record identifies **1983.0001.001, excerpt 16:41–19:15**. This is an institution-published selection from an oral history, not a newly conducted interview or a full recording heard for this draft. [4]

Topping recalls the early Moosehead enterprise and visitors asking to be fed in a small homestead cabin. Her words are: **“So I started in with a coffee pot and a pancake griddle”**. The editors identify her earlier name as Eva Sanford and describe her 1924 arrival to teach, subsequent homesteading, and marriage to Fred Topping. Those biographical details are editorial context, not independently checked land records. [4]

**Original narration for the visitor:** Imagine a brochure with two columns. One lists what a guest expects to enjoy. The other lists what another person must do for that enjoyment to happen. The second column is not a footnote. It is part of the destination's history.

This account makes the theme specific without converting one woman's experience into the experience of every household. It does not establish wages, ownership shares, staffing levels, equitable division of work or whether every service was paid. Do not romanticize the work as effortless hospitality, or invent a conflict the testimony does not describe.

The point is not that visitors were wrong to come. It is that a complete visitor-economy story lets workers and household members remain visible alongside scenery and entrepreneurs. This entry is deliberately not a comprehensive history of settlement or Indigenous land relationships; that larger subject requires appropriate Tribal-published evidence rather than importing a tourism narrative as its substitute.
