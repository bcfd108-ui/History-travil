## 2. The story

### The date on an application and the years behind it

Daniel Freeman's January 1, 1863 application and January 20, 1868 final-proof records bracket the work of turning residence and improvements into a federal title. The National Archives publishes the application, proof and separate eligibility certificate. The Homestead Act required evidence from two credible witnesses for the final step and described the test as having “resided upon or cultivated the same for the term of five years.” Read the archive's documentary sequence in order. Filing an application set the terms. Evidence of work and residence made it possible to claim ownership. The eligibility certificate is a distinct document from both the application and the proof. [3–6]

### Agnes enters the record under her own name

In a letter to Daniel from Iowa, Agnes Suiter wrote, “since I saw you I have taught three months school.” The NPS publishes a transcription of this courtship correspondence. Daniel had gone to Nebraska alone. They married in 1865, after the initial filing and before the final proof, and raised eight children. The letters recover paid work, family obligation and the practical work of keeping in contact. They provide a voice the legal file's claimant-centered structure leaves faint. Neither the letters cited here nor the online proof establishes which member of the household did each field or domestic task. [4,7,8]

### A field, a cabin and a legal boundary

NPS identifies the park as the site of the Freeman claim. It recounts the family's log cabin and later brick house, which burned in 1916. The Palmer-Epard cabin is a different family's building that now helps visitors understand a small dwelling on an early Nebraska claim. The idea of claiming land depends here on a federal statute and its paperwork. It is a legal history of settler acquisition with consequences beyond this family. Keep the displacement reading quiet, on public park ground or at home. This draft makes no Tribally attributed claim about a specific site's history. [2,6,8,9]

## 3. Then / now

The claim was farmed and built on by the Freemans. The park restores tallgrass prairie and interprets homesteading on their former tract. The Palmer-Epard cabin, where available, illustrates an era from a separately identified household. The brick Freeman home burned, and a story about the original cabin is separate from identifying its surviving footprint. [1,8,9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- DocsTeach describes Freeman as the first American to file. NPS says he was one of the first and calls this likely the first claim made and successfully proved up. The midnight story is attributed to legend by NPS. The dated records confirm the filing and proof dates and do not establish nationwide ordering or a minute of filing. [3–5,8]
- The proof's DocsTeach image link resolved to a 298 × 196 derivative in this retrieval. The caption and document sequence were read, but the handwriting was not securely legible at that size. No exact witness testimony or improvement dimensions are transcribed here. Agnes's line is from NPS's published transcription. [4,7]
- The NARA landing page labels the office Brownsfield in its caption. NPS calls it Brownville. Preserve that catalog discrepancy pending comparison with the legible original or fuller case file. [8,10]
