## 2. The story

**Original narration:** A restoration act has a date, but a political movement has people. Begin with a young leader who was also a student, trying to preserve a recollection before other voices were lost. This makes public history more than a federal timeline: it becomes the record of people organizing for their future.

**Travis Parashonts (Benioh), a Cedar Band member**, is the named voice. His *History of the 1980 Restoration Act (Part 1)* is published directly by the **Paiute Indian Tribe of Utah (PITU)**. In its introduction he recalls being 22 in 1975, representing the Cedar Band on the Utah Paiute Tribal Corporation board while attending college. He acknowledges the limits of his memory and the other leaders' perspectives. He explains his purpose as recording **“my experience and knowledge of what happened during the termination and restoration era”**. This is a participant's retrospective account, not an omniscient narrative or an audio recording heard for this research. [4]

The Tribe's institutional history identifies **Cedar, Indian Peaks, Kanosh, Koosharem and Shivwits** as its five constituent Bands. It explains that each has its own organizational government, procedures and responsibilities. Cooperation must not be mistaken for interchangeability. PITU is not a shorthand for every Southern Paiute sovereign community, and Northern Paiute histories must not be substituted. [5][6]

The primary legislative record supplies a precise boundary: **Public Law 96-227, approved April 3, 1980**, restores the federal trust relationship to the Shivwits, Kanosh, Koosharem and Indian Peaks Bands and uses the distinct formulation **“restored or confirmed”** for the Cedar City Band. That difference matters; it is not accurate to say all five had an identical legal history. This is historical-document reading, not present legal advice. [7]

The **Shivwits Band's own published history** separately frames its story around resilience and broken promises. Read it as that Band's account, not as anonymous supporting color for a generic “Paiute story.” Its existence alongside PITU's materials helps keep authorship visible. [8]

**Interpretive payoff:** Federal recognition did not create the people. The strongest end to this visit is not an origin myth about a community beginning in 1980, but the recognition that people already there worked to change how the government treated them. A museum can be an entry point to that history without owning its voice.
