# HT-RES-MT-old-montana-prison — A territorial prison opened in 1871 and emptied in 1979, its wall and cell house still standing on Main Street, Deer Lodge
Division prisons-asylums-tours-001 • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Montana's first prison was a federal answer to a vigilante territory: Congress funded a penitentiary, Deer Lodge was chosen for it in 1867, and on July 2, 1871, the first prisoners were received in a single finished wing of stone. For the next 108 years the institution grew inside walls its own prisoners quarried, cut, and built — a sandstone wall, two cell houses, and a theater paid for by a copper king — while overcrowding was its normal condition. It emptied in 1979, and the complex is now the Old Prison Museum, centerpiece of a five-museum site.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a museum visit: Old Prison Museum, Old Montana Prison Complex, 1106 Main St., Deer Lodge, MT 59722, operated by the Powell County Museum and Arts Foundation (PCMAF), which holds the Old Prison under a 99-year lease from the State of Montana. The Museum's Visitor Center is at the intersection of Main Street and Texas Avenue. Admission and hours print here only as the operator's Plan Your Visit page states them. ## 2. The story
Gold was discovered near Deer Lodge in 1862, and the rush that followed brought thievery, lawlessness, and murder, answered for a time by vigilante groups. In December 1866 Representative John N. Rodgers memorialized Congress for penitentiary funds; funding was approved in January 1867 — $40,000 on the commonly carried figure — and on November 19, 1867, Deer Lodge was chosen as the site, on a 9¾-acre federal land grant. On June 2, 1870, the granite cornerstone was dedicated, its cavity sealed with an 1870 almanac, the local paper, U.S. coins, and eight ounces of Old Crow whiskey, on the nomination's record.

What rose on the grant was smaller than planned. The design called for three tiers of fourteen cells; the building that opened held a fraction of that — the nomination describes a 40-by-80-foot two-story stone building of 28 cells, with no confining wall — and the operator's history describes the finished wing as fourteen brick cells, six by eight feet (see §6). On July 2, 1871, U.S. Marshal William F. Wheeler, the prison's first warden, received the first prisoners. His recollection, quoted in the operator's history, is exact: the prison "opened for the reception of territorial and United States convicts on the 2nd day of July 1871, on which day I received nine convicts." The operator's history names the first prisoner: Samuel E. Hughes, serving a year for armed assault, pardoned after twenty-three days. The nomination gives a different count (see §6).

The young prison changed hands repeatedly. In May 1873 the federal government relinquished it, with twenty-one inmates, to Montana Territory, but operation proved so expensive that the federal government resumed control in August 1874. A fourteen-cell wing was added in 1875 and a south wing in 1885. Women were held here from 1878 — nearly 200 women between 1878 and 1959, on the Historical Society's account — in a prison built exclusively for men: the first, Felicita Sanchez, was confined to her cell for a full year until a second woman arrived, and separate housing came only decades later (see §6).

Statehood came November 8, 1889, and the prison became the state's problem. Unable to fund it, the state contracted the whole operation out: from 1890 the firm of Conley & McTague — Frank Conley, a former prison guard, and Colonel Thomas McTague — fed, clothed, and guarded the prisoners at a per-day rate the nomination gives as 76 cents a convict (see §6). Conley, warden from 1890 to 1921, is the central figure of the prison's history, and his system was convict labor on a large scale. In 1892–1893 the prisoners built the sandstone wall that still encloses the ground, quarrying the stone locally and cutting it inside the walls; they fashioned 1.2 million bricks by hand on the operator's count, and in 1896–1897 completed a 200-man cell house reported then as the most modern of its kind in the country. Conley used the same crews outside the walls — Montana roads, buildings at the state institutions at Warm Springs and Galen, ranch and logging work — and in 1893 he stopped a move of the prison to Billings. Inside the walls he abolished stripes in 1910. The state ended the contract system in 1908 and took direct control, reappointing Conley warden; a 1909 law validated his labor system, and in the 1910s as much as half the population worked on outside projects. In 1911 the prisoners enlarged the wall and built a power plant; the convict-built Conley Street Bridge over the Clark Fork (1912) is documented in the Historic American Engineering Record. In 1912 they built the prison's largest cell house, the 400-man Cell Block 1 by Link & Haire, and in 1918–1919 the prison theater, with convict-made brick, financed by former Senator William A. Clark in appreciation, the nomination records, for the convicts' work on his ranch and for the Anaconda Copper Company — reportedly the first theater within the walls of a prison in the United States.

Conley's end came politically: in 1921 Governor Joseph Dixon's investigation of his alleged use of state money for personal gain forced him out; he was tried for embezzlement and acquitted, but his programs were terminated all the same. Forty years of decline followed. In the 1930s organized labor's protests ended out-of-prison labor, a strict silent system was imposed, and the Depression cut appropriations — the Historical Society's records summary describes a prison with no indoor plumbing, poor wiring, and inmates idle in their cells most of the day. Under Warden Faye O. Burrell (1953–1958), appropriated improvement money went largely unspent — of $105,000 appropriated for a minimum-security building, about $125 was spent — while the population swelled past 600. In 1957 an investigation by Kenyon J. Scudder reported appalling and potentially explosive conditions; that July, prisoners took guards hostage and presented demands — better food and hospital care, an end to the silence system, yard privileges, a federal investigation. The state answered by hiring professional administrators, Warden Floyd Powell and Deputy Warden Theodore Rothe, who began reforms.

On the afternoon of April 16, 1959, the prison rose anyway. The Montana Supreme Court's record in *State v. Alton* fixes the start: at about 3:30 p.m., a guard on the catwalk had a solution thrown in his face and was backed away from a lit torch while inmates took the keys to the gun cage and its rifle; other guards were taken at knifepoint and held. The Historical Society's account names the leaders — Jerry Myles, Lee Smart, and George Alton: Deputy Warden Rothe was killed, Warden Powell was taken hostage and escaped after the prisoner assigned to kill him let him go, and most inmates returned to their cells voluntarily. The operator's account gives the scale: twenty-six staff and inmates taken hostage, the uprising quelled after thirty-six hours by 200 National Guardsmen and Highway Patrol officers. In the final assault early on April 18, a bazooka was fired at the cell house tower; Myles shot Smart and then himself. Three men died: Rothe, Myles, and Smart. The same year, an earthquake damaged the 1896 cell house, and it was torn down.

The riot made the old prison's inadequacy undeniable, but it housed prisoners for twenty more years. It was listed on the National Register on September 3, 1976 (No. 76001126) while still operating — 380 inmates in the nomination's count. In 1979 the last prisoners were moved to a new prison west of town. The Powell County Museum and Arts Foundation took the old prison on under its long lease from the state and opened it as a museum.

One life, carried in the operator's exhibit account, stands for many the records barely name. Paul "Turkey Pete" Eitner, convicted of murder and sentenced to life in 1918, became a model prisoner in charge of the turkey flock; in time, losing touch with reality, he "sold" the flock at twenty-five cents a bird, and the print shop made him checks with which he "paid" the prison's expenses from Cell No. 1. He died in 1967, aged 89, after forty-nine years inside; his cell was retired, and his was the only funeral ever held within the walls, on the operator's account.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a walled compound of 9¾ acres on the federal grant, bounded by Pennsylvania Avenue, Main Street, Conley Avenue, and the high-water mark of the Clark Fork River. **Now:** the same boundaries legible from Main Street — the wall and its towers still closing the south end of the street, the 1912 cell house rising behind it.

**Then:** July 1871 — one finished wing, fourteen to twenty-eight cells depending on the record, no wall at all, and a marshal-warden receiving nine convicts. **Now:** the 1870 building is gone — demolished in 1930–1931 — its site held by its replacement administration building, while the wall, the 1912 cell house, and the theater carry the ground's visible history.


## 4. What's still there
- The prison wall (1892, extended north in 1911): finely dressed random ashlar quarried locally, granite coping from Helena, on the nomination; projecting pilasters, four round towers at the corners, and two square Romanesque towers midway on the east and west walls (on the wall's date and material as variously given, see §6)
- Cell Block 1 (1912): the 400-man cell house by Link & Haire, built with convict labor — red brick, 224 by 80 feet and 80 feet high, four square corner towers; inside, a free-standing cell block of eight tiers of twenty-five cells each, each cell with two bunks, a toilet, and a cold-water sink, and the original Pauly Jail Company locking mechanism
- The Clark Theater (1918–1919): brick, 105 by 65 by 30 feet, inside the south wall; wall murals painted by a prisoner in 1921; built for 1,000 seats, its interior badly damaged by fire on December 9, 1975, on the nomination's record — and today the building in which the operator locates exhibits, including the galloping gallows displayed there (not this entry's photograph)
- The administration building (1930–1931): concrete block, inside the walls on the site of the original 1870 federal prison building
- The Hospital/Segregation Building (1931) and the Maximum Security Building west of the main wall — built as the women's ward (ca. 1918 on the nomination's dating; see §6) — whose slide-bar cells and "black box" the operator's tour describes
- The museum's exhibits inside the walls: the "Prison Life" photo exhibit, restraint and weapons displays, and Cell No. 1, retired after Eitner's death — as the operator's museum page describes them

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Visitor Center, Main Street and Texas Avenue:** Start with the operator's orientation and the complex ticket, then cross to the wall.
2. **At Tower 7 and the main entrance:** The entry as the working prison made it — a small inner room where a guard lowered the key on a rope through a hole in the ceiling, on the operator's account.
3. **Inside Cell Block 1:** The free-standing eight-tier block inside its brick shell, the Pauly locking mechanism, and the cells' fittings — two bunks, a toilet, cold water only.
4. **At the Clark Theater and the center yard:** The convict-built, Clark-financed theater, the murals of 1921, and the yard, where names and prison numbers are scratched into the bricks, on the operator's account.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The first day's numbers do not agree. Wheeler's own recollection is that he received nine convicts on July 2, 1871; the nomination states that the building "received the first of its twelve prisoners" that day. Both print here, and §2 follows Wheeler's first-person count and names Hughes as its first prisoner. The first building's size is likewise two-layered: the nomination describes a two-story stone building of 28 cells, while the operator's history and the museum's federal-building marker describe a finished wing of fourteen cells — most likely a planned building of which one wing opened — an inference, labeled as one here.

Two of the wall's facts are given differently. The nomination dates the wall to 1892 and describes buff-colored stone with granite coping from Helena; the operator's About page dates the wall's construction by convict labor to 1893 and describes locally quarried granite walls, while the state tourism listing calls the walls gray sandstone. Section 4 follows the nomination; the rest prints here, unresolved. The Conley contract rate divides the same way: 76 cents per convict per day in the nomination, 70 cents in later summaries; §2 uses the nomination's.

The women's ward carries three dates: the nomination dates the building that became Maximum Security — originally the Women's Ward — to about 1918; the Montana History Portal's record says prisoners built the women's prison in 1908; the Historical Society's account gives a first separate structure in 1895 and a stone building in 1907. These may describe successive buildings; the records checked do not settle it, and no single date is asserted in §4. Conley's start is given as 1889 by the History Portal record and 1890 by the finding aid, the operator, and the nomination; §2 uses 1890, the contract year. The $40,000 appropriation is dated to January 1867 by the Historical Society and to 1868 in the nomination; §2 follows the Historical Society.

The 1959 riot's hostage count differs by source: the operator gives twenty-six staff and inmates taken hostage; the Associated Press report in the *Oregon Statesman* of April 20, 1959, describes twenty-one hostages rescued in the final assault. Both print, and §2 uses the operator's figure, labeled as the operator's. The 1957 disturbance is framed differently too: a bloodless riot in the nomination, a July 1957 hostage-taking with demands in the Historical Society's summary; §2 follows the Historical Society. The theater fire is dated December 9, 1975, by the nomination; the History Portal record says only that fire left the theater a burned-out shell in the 1970s — and the operator today locates exhibits there, so a restoration intervened that the records checked do not date. The prison's final day is not fixed by the records checked: the operator says it was emptied in the late 1970s, and the Historical Society and the marker record date the move to 1979. A specific September day for the last transfer circulates in secondary accounts; it is not carried in the records checked, and no day prints in §2. Finally, the Plan Your Visit page's winter-closure line still reads "December 5, 2025 – March 12, 2026" — the previous winter's dates — so the current winter's closure, if any, is not stated and none is printed in §1.

## 8. Standing on the ground
What a visitor can check against the record on site, in plain terms:

- Walk the wall's east face and check the nomination's description against the stone: projecting pilasters, four round corner towers, and two square midway towers with crenellated parapets.
- In Cell Block 1, count the tiers and cells against the nomination's inventory — a free-standing block of eight tiers of twenty-five cells — and check a cell's fittings: two bunks, a toilet, a cold-water sink, the Pauly locking mechanism still in place.
- In the Clark Theater, look for the prisoner-painted murals the nomination dates to 1921, and check its two recorded facts in the room itself: a capacity once of 1,000, and an interior badly damaged by the fire of December 9, 1975.
