# Jaketown and the limits of what an archaeological collection can tell us

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP25 · Labeled research draft · NO-ANCHOR · Quiet reflection.*

## 2. Story
People returned to a place in the Yazoo Basin over a long span. The archaeological record indicates separate occupations; it does not make all artifacts from this place contemporary. Geoffrey R. Lehmann’s 1982 state report assembled and compared documented surface collections. It examines small stone artifacts and hundreds of examples of shaped clay objects, among other material. In discussing how the clay objects might have been used, he signals the boundary of a proposed explanation with “Though unsubstantiated by tests”. That qualification matters as much as the proposed use. A recognizable artifact is evidence of work. Its precise use can remain open. [1, 4]

Earlier investigators asked whether distinct clusters of artifacts meant different activities taking place within a community or occupation in different periods. Lehmann records William Haag’s suggestion that the distribution could reflect time and Clarence H. Webb’s preference for an activity-area explanation. The study also reviews how investigators separated later ceramic material from deeper deposits that they understood as earlier. Neither question can be resolved by creating a single occupation date for the whole place. [4]

The 1955 field report records what made part of this work possible and what it cost the site. In 1950 the state highway department relocated a road onto an old railroad bed. Mechanical excavation removed archaeological deposits as well as sand for a causeway. The following year James Ford and Warren Eames worked in the field while Philip Phillips and Mrs. Phillips processed the finds in a rented Belzoni storefront. The authors identify her work without giving her first name in the preface. That temporary workroom is a historical detail. It does not establish a present-day visitor museum. [10, preface and p. 14]

The history of research is also a history of removal. A 2020 Federal Register notice documents Native American ancestral remains removed from Jaketown and the subsequent inventory and consultation process. It distinguishes the laboratory’s determination about shared identity from its determinations about aboriginal land and potential disposition, which named the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians and The Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma. That notice does not establish present exhibition status or authorize visits. A Tribe-published firsthand interpretation of this specific place was not found in this pass. [5]
