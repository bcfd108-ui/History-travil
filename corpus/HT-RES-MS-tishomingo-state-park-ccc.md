# The Civilian Conservation Corps crews who built Tishomingo State Park

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP17*

## 2. Story

In April 1935 Civilian Conservation Corps Company 3497 began turning part of the Bear Creek landscape into a state park. The original historic district nomination dates opening to the public to May 1939. Work crossed that opening threshold. Some crews made the first visitor buildings. Others continued grading and building with stone and timber as the public park took shape. The period named in the nomination, 1936–1948, describes the resources it evaluated. The starting date of the crews’ activity is a different question. [2]

Clifford Green Worsham, a senior engineering foreman, assembled an album of construction work in 1939 and 1940. The state archive preserved the photographs with his typed description list. The department’s teaching guide, which extracts details from his account, names Ernest Clausel as a blacksmith who brought specialized work to the camp. It describes reusing lumber from a closed camp for pool formwork and borrowing equipment when the job required it. Those details give texture to the phrase “constructed by the CCC.” They record an engineering foreman’s perspective, and other men’s first-person testimony remains missing. [3][4]

The park’s name remembers a Chickasaw leader. The Chickasaw Nation writes the name Tishominko and translates it as “speaker for the leader.” Its own account remembers him as a counselor whose role extended into the time of Chickasaw removal. The New Deal work took place on parts of an older homeland, a history that the state park name alone cannot carry. Preserve the history in the Chickasaw Nation’s terms and keep the visit on designated modern park visitor ground. [1][5]

## 3. Then and now

The historic district records the office and lodge, stone steps, walls and culverts, a dam and lake, and the suspended bridge. Some of this material belongs to the original building campaign, and the nomination records later structures and changes separately. The modern state operator offers day use and reserves the lodge for groups. It also runs separate outdoor activities with different limits. Only the office and staff-confirmed upland lodge exterior anchor this account. [1][2]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Staffed day-use anchor.** Start at the public park office and ask where the signed upland viewing area for the CCC lodge is open that day. Use only the office and ordinary public approach identified by staff. [1]
- **Historic fabric with restricted interior.** The nomination records the stone and wood fabric of the historic lodge. Read it from the designated exterior if open. Entrance to the lodge itself requires staff authorization. [1][2]
- **Historical context under other limits.** The bridge, water-control structures, pool and older Indigenous ground remain in the record. No streambank, shore, bridge crossing, archaeological or ceremonial path is recommended here. [1–5]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

At the office obtain the day’s visitor limits. If staff confirm the public upland lodge exterior, use it to see how stone work joins timber and compare the materials with the nomination. Keep a closed lodge or public approach out of the trip. **Strongest story moment — Building after opening day.** The park opened in 1939 while the foreman’s 1939–1940 album was still accumulating. At the office or confirmed lodge exterior, read a park that depended on years of shop work, salvaged material and crews whose names rarely appear on a building label. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The operator calls the bridge a CCC structure built in 1938. The archive catalogs a 1939–1940 album documenting bridge construction among other projects. Completion, afterwork and whether all captions refer to the first span remain unresolved without collation of the individual photographic sequence and engineering logs. [1][3]
- The nomination records April 1935 start, May 1939 opening and a 1936–1948 period of significance. These describe the workforce, public opening and listing scope respectively. [2]
- The archive identifies Worsham as collector and foreman. Authorship, attribution and public-domain basis of the individual album images remain open. The archive’s teacher guide is used as its own research aid and no statement from it is treated as a verbatim transcription of Worsham. [3][4]
