# Genoa Indian School — records, family memory and quiet witness

DRAFTED · Nebraska OP03 · Pending independent Dash QC

## 2. The story

### What the government wrote down

The U.S. Department of the Interior's school profile records a federally operated boarding institution from February 20, 1884 through 1934. The Ponca Tribe of Nebraska's published account gives the same years and identifies the former school as a place for “education, historical research, and remembrance.” The records establish federal administration. The Tribe's wording offers a purpose for reading those records today. [2,3]

A surviving administrative item is the Record of Pupil for Leo Amiotte, dated around 1931 in the project catalog. It is filed with Pine Ridge Agency case records and carries a federal series and box reference. The project record makes the administrative document locatable. It supplies no license to substitute a school record for a person's recollection. The item's public catalog entry is a starting point, with the federal folder and the person to whom it pertains clearly separated in the reading. [4]

### What families chose to remember

The collaborative digital archive catalogs Susana Geliga's October 7, 2024 interview with Wynema Morris. The program identifies Morris's account as remembering Omaha ancestors. The direct interview returned an internal retrieval error. A separately published interview quotes Morris saying of her mother, former student Elsie Gilpin, “She truly saw it through the eyes of a child.” That short statement is a descendant's direct public remark as printed by Nebraska Public Media. It is separate from the missing audio and is not passed off as Gilpin's own words. The archive describes community advisors from the Omaha, Pawnee, Ponca, Santee Sioux and Winnebago Tribes and descendants. [5,6,7]

### Keep the numbers attached to their records

Interior's 2024 profile identifies 261 students and 16 deceased students in a dataset dated January 1, 2024. The project landing page describes thousands of children attending over the institution's existence. These scopes differ. The profile itself cautions that its identified deaths do not necessarily represent people buried at a school. No burial location is part of the itinerary. The point of using names and dated sources is to hold space for people the original administrative categories never fully described. [2,4,7]

## 3. Then / now

The former federal school included a broad campus. The state's account identifies the surviving manual training building, which the Foundation operates as a seasonal interpretive center. The digitization project brings selected historical records and family interviews to readers remotely, which is the current draft visit. [1,5,7,8]

## 5. Public trip-shaped progression

1. Begin remotely with the Ponca Tribe's published interpretation of why remembering matters and the federal profile's dates. [2,3]
2. Read the cataloged pupil record, then the archive's interview entry. Give priority to materials the family and the project make available. [4,5]
3. **Strongest story moment — A mother remembered.** Pause over Morris's public remarks about her mother. Leave the records and names in view, and finish quietly without a task or photograph. In a later verified season this reflection may be done in the staffed interpretive center. [1,5,6]
