# HT-RES-WI-hh-bennett-studio — The storefront that sold the Dells to America, one stereograph at a time
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

A one-story brick storefront at 215 Broadway in Wisconsin Dells holds the working rooms of Henry Hamilton Bennett (1843–1908): the sales gallery, the portrait studio, the darkroom, and the printing rooms of the man whose stereoscopic views made the Dells of the Wisconsin River one of the most widely seen landscapes in America. Built in 1875, the studio ran under Bennett, his wife and partner, and two generations of his successors, and was given by the family to the Wisconsin Historical Society in 1998. It stands today as a Society historic site, with Bennett's negatives, cameras, and inventions still in the building where he worked.

## 1. Trip gate
The operator is the Wisconsin Historical Society, and the stop is the H. H. Bennett Studio & Museum at 215 Broadway, in the middle of downtown Wisconsin Dells (Society listing; the 1976 nomination gives the same address). The Society's own site page, checked November 26, 2026, draws the season's boundary in its own words: "See You in Spring 2027! ... We reopen for visitors in May 2027." The site is seasonal and was closed for the season when checked; no hours and no admission price are printed in this entry, because the Society pages checked state neither in a current, datable form.

The building fronts a public street, so the exterior — including the 1918 facade described in §2 and §4 — is readable from the sidewalk in any season. The interior is the Society's museum, entered through the Society in season. The Dells formations in Bennett's photographs are river ground, reached from the Wisconsin River; the studio is not a landing or trailhead for them. The most famous single frame Bennett made — his son Ashley in mid-air between the mainland cliff and Stand Rock — was a staged demonstration of a shutter, made by a professional with his own son as the subject. It is not a visitor activity.

## 2. The story
Henry Hamilton Bennett was a carpenter by trade before the Civil War (Wisconsin Historical Society site history). The 1976 National Register nomination carries the turn in his life this way: "In 1861 during his Civil War Service, Bennett was wounded in the hand. The injury may have been influential in his decision to purchase a small tin-type studio in Kilburn City, Wisconsin, rather than to follow the family trade of carpentry." The Society's site adds that he served with the 12th Wisconsin Infantry, fought at Vicksburg, and was severely wounded by the accidental discharge of his own gun, and that he learned photography with help from his uncle, George Houghton (the dating tension between the nomination and the site narrative is printed in §6).

In 1865 Bennett purchased a tintype studio business in Kilbourn City — the nomination dates the Broadway studio "ten years after its founder purchased a tintype studio business in Kilbourn City," and the Society's site history says he opened his first studio in 1865. In 1868, per the Society, he took his first stereographic landscape photographs of the rugged Wisconsin River dells. The stereo format put him in the era's mass market: the nomination records that almost every family had a stereographic viewer and that hundreds of thousands of scenic views were sold, as original contact prints mounted on cards. The technical problems were great — wet plates had to be coated, exposed, and developed on the spot — and the Society's site history notes that Bennett traveled with a darkroom tent, developed his plates almost immediately after exposure, and often constructed every part of his cameras except the lenses.

In 1875 he moved the business into the studio he and his friend and mentor William Metcalf designed on Broadway (Society site history). The nomination's description reads the building as a plant, and this entry keeps that reading: "It was built as a commercial photographers studio and its arrangements reflect the phases of a business enterprise, sales, studio portraiture, wet process developing, and contact printing of stereoview negatives." The original facade was red brick, a single story, with a central transomed double door and two-over-two windows on each side.

The views the plant produced — of the Dells of the Wisconsin River and of Devil's Lake, in the nomination's words — "were instrumental in promoting infant tourism." The nomination singles out the work that went furthest beyond scenery: "he pioneered in the development of something very like the modern picture essay — for instance his coverage of a lumber raft trip down the Wisconsin River goes beyond simple documentation to show how all the aspects of raftman's life fit together. In this Bennett was far in advance of his peers who contented themselves with stiffly posed reenactments of 'interesting' incidents." Sara Rath's account in the Society's Wisconsin Magazine of History dates that raft trip to the autumn of 1886, with Bennett's son Ashley signed on as his assistant; the raft photograph in §7 belongs to the series the trip produced. Bennett also covered the Milwaukee waterfront, merchants' and manufacturers' homes, the Winnebago (Ho-Chunk) people, and at least three world fairs — 1876, 1893, and 1904 (nomination). The Society's site history records that he was the first to photograph the Ho-Chunk people, and that the Ho-Chunk Nation uses many of his images today.

The enterprise ran on invention as much as on views. The nomination credits Bennett with "new lens mounts, high speed shutters, a revolving printing house to follow the sun," and with building the stereoview mounters and trimmers "to feed the expanding stereo market." His high-speed shutter made the period's signature Bennett image possible — a man "caught in mid air jumping across chasms," in the nomination's phrase. The famous instance is the leap to Stand Rock: the marker the studio itself erected (WHI 2101) describes "Ashley Bennett, son of photographer H.H. Bennett, jumping to Stand Rock, caught in midair by the instantaneous shutter," and the leap is dated 1886 in the marker's record and in the Society magazine's account (see §6 for the dating basis).

Bennett died in 1908. The business continued under the family — the nomination's significance statement describes the studio as housing "the work of Henry Hamilton Bennett, his wife and partner, and two generations of his successors." The building changed as the business demanded: the gallery was modernized by about 1910 with heavy Arts and Crafts beams while the original display cases were retained, and in 1918 his widow and former partner, Mrs. Evaline Bennett, remodeled the facade in Old English or Tudor style, with a half-timbered stucco gable, a recessed central door, and plate glass windows (nomination). His work was exhibited in the Museum of Modern Art's 1963 exhibition The Photographer in the American Landscape (nomination bibliography). In 1976 the studio was entered in the National Register of Historic Places, with Ruth Bennett Dyer and Jean Dyer Reese named as owners. In 1998 the family donated the property to the Wisconsin Historical Society, and it opened as a Society site in 2000 (Society site history).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** an 1875 red brick storefront with a rotating glass printing house on its roof, selling contact-printed stereoviews of the Dells to a national market of parlor viewers. **Now:** the same address behind a 1918 Tudor facade — the front the street sees is the widow's remodel, while the interior plan recorded in 1976 is still Bennett's arrangement of gallery, studio, and darkroom.

**Then:** a working photographer exposing glass plates in a darkroom tent on the riverbank, developing each plate within minutes of the shot. **Now:** the plates themselves — the nomination records that Bennett's work "is preserved virtually in totality," his negatives held at the studio with his cameras and inventions, "an almost complete collection of the work of a great photographer held in the actual space he worked in"; the Society's finding aid confirms the glass negatives for the early studio photographs are stored at the site today.

**Then:** Kilbourn City, a small lumber town at the Dells. **Now:** Wisconsin Dells, a nationally known tourist destination — the Society credits Bennett's work with helping transform the city from a lumber town into one — and the studio is, per the Society's site history, the oldest known operating business in the city.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The 1875 studio building at 215 Broadway (the Society's Architecture and History Inventory record gives 213–215 Broadway; noted October 2001 as a one-story, light orange brick building) — carrying its 1918 facade (National Register nomination, 1976)
- The original interior layout: the old gallery functioning as exhibit space, the museum occupying the old studio and dark room areas (nomination, 1976)
- The original "Artistic" Eastlake Style display cases, which in 1976 still showed Bennett views (nomination, 1976)
- Bennett's cameras, mounting equipment, and the wet plate process displayed with the props used in portrait sittings (nomination, 1976; the Society presents the studio as its museum today, site checked November 26, 2026)
- The negative collection: a temperature- and humidity-controlled vault finished behind the original studio building by 1976 (nomination), and the early studio series' glass negatives stored at the site (Society finding aid, current)
- A photography business at the address in continuous operation since 1875 (Society site history) — the enterprise itself is part of what survives

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route. The interior stops assume the Society's season; the site was closed for the season when checked and the Society's page gives a May 2027 reopening.

1. **From the Broadway sidewalk:** Read the facade against the record — a half-timbered Tudor front of 1918 over an 1875 brick commercial building. The face is Evaline Bennett's; the enterprise behind it is her husband's.
2. **In the gallery and sales room (in season, through the Society's museum):** Start where the money was made. Stereoviews were a retail product — cards sold by the hundreds of thousands — before they were art, and the Eastlake display cases are the surviving furniture of that trade.
3. **In the studio and darkroom rooms:** The production floor — portraiture, wet process developing, contact printing — the phases the nomination lists as the building's arrangement. Everything Bennett sold passed through these rooms.
4. **At the collection itself:** End with the negatives, stored in the building where the plates were exposed and printed. Then read the §7 photograph — a raft frame from the 1886 river trip — as a product of this plant: made downriver, printed and sold here.


## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The wound that made Bennett a photographer is dated two ways. The 1976 nomination says, "In 1861 during his Civil War Service, Bennett was wounded in the hand." The Society's site narrative has him fight at Vicksburg — 1863 — before being severely wounded by the accidental discharge of his own gun. This entry prints the wound, its mechanism, and its consequence (carpentry closed to him; photography taken up instead) without fixing a year, because the two primary accounts checked do not reconcile to one.

The gift of the studio prints two ways as well. The Society's site history says the family ran the business until 1998, when they donated the property, and that it opened as a Society site in 2000. The marker record for the studio (HMdb) says Oliver and Jean Reese operated the studio until 1999, when they gave it to the Society. A gift in 1998, an operation ending in 1999, and an opening in 2000 could all be true at once, but no source checked says so; the Society's dates are used in §2.

The "oldest" claim comes in three scopes from three sources, and this entry asserts none of them in its own voice: the 1976 nomination calls the studio "the oldest operating photographer's enterprise in the state"; the Society's Architecture and History Inventory note (October 2001) calls it "probably one of the oldest photography studios in the state"; the Society's current site history calls it "the oldest known operating business in Wisconsin Dells" and "believed to be the oldest still operating photography studio in the United States."

The address prints two ways: 215 Broadway in the nomination and the Society's listings, 213–215 Broadway in the Architecture and History Inventory record; the 215 form is used here. The town's name is spelled both "Kilbourn City" and "Kilburn City" inside the nomination form itself; the Society uses Kilbourn City, and Kilbourn is used here.

The revolving printing house is only partly documented in the sources checked. The nomination's description says Bennett "devised a rotating glass printing house that was installed on the roof of the building," its significance text calls it "a revolving printing house to follow the sun," and its 1976 account records that "plans exist for the restoration and preservation of the revolving solar printing house." Secondary accounts say the original device is now at the Smithsonian Institution and that a replica stands at the site; no primary source checked in this pass confirms either statement, so neither prints in §2 or §4.

The 1886 date for the Stand Rock leap rests on the studio-erected marker's record (WHI 2101) and on the Society magazine's account of Bennett's shutter work; the 1976 nomination describes the high-speed shutters and the mid-air jumping views without dating the Stand Rock frame. The year prints in §2 attributed to those sources, not as a nomination fact. The raft trip's autumn 1886 date likewise rests on Rath's account; the nomination describes the raft picture essay without dating the trip, and the §7 photograph's own Society record carries an 1886 creation date independently.
