# Huron suffrage organizers

## 2. The story

### Huron organized before the famous visitor came

On October 9, 1889, a group of eastern South Dakota supporters issued a call to “all friends of equality and justice.” The words are the organizers’ invitation, as quoted in a South Dakota State Historical Society journal study. Their Huron meeting on October 21 formed the state Equal Suffrage Association. Helen M. Barker presided and took on lecturing and organizing. The elected officers connected Huron to Woonsocket and Pukwana, and the association set out to recruit bodies that already knew how to hold meetings and move information across long distances, including farm, labor and temperance groups. [3]

Turn from state officers to the people who put on a meeting. In February 1890, Huron suffrage supporters marked Susan B. Anthony’s birthday at the Grand Army of the Republic hall. Anthony was absent, according to the modern study that cites the *Huron Daily Times*. The paper’s item survives in the Emma Smith DeVoe scrapbook index at Washington State Library. Its direct pages returned an anti-bot screen. In April, Anthony came to South Dakota, set up headquarters in Huron and stayed with Emma Smith DeVoe. DeVoe then organized in the Black Hills as part of the effort. [3–6]

### The people in the same campaign disagreed

Helen Gougar, a visiting national lecturer, said Anthony was “badly out of touch with these home workers.” The same quoted account credited DeVoe with “doing efficient organizing.” Both snippets come through the journal’s reproduction of Gougar’s report. The tension sits in those two judgments. Local organizers already had relationships and knew where people would turn out. The national campaign brought speakers and money, and it pressed to decide how they would be used. Read the account alongside the association’s founding call and ask how much agreement the public language actually represented. [3]

### The first vote

A state historical journal’s table records 22,972 yes and 45,682 no on the 1890 amendment. The proposal lost. These are votes on the amendment, not a count of supporters excluded from voting. The table also shows later campaigns and eventual passage in 1918. Read the 1890 return as a report on one campaign’s result. The local records make visible the meetings, travel and disagreements that the two columns of numbers leave out. [3,7]

## 3. Then / now

The town hosted the 1889 organizational meeting and served as Anthony’s 1890 headquarters. The modern public library is a reading anchor. Its South Dakota Room and digital collection provide a place to look for Huron’s record. No 1889 or 1890 event building is identified here as a publicly visitable survivor. [2–6]
