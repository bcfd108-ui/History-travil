## 2. The story
**Lincoln Ragsdale Jr.** recalls work in his family's Phoenix mortuary business in a published interview-based story by **Summer Cherland**. Its metadata dates the interview **November 10, 2023**, and names **Summer Cherland and Mary Nunn** as faculty interviewers. [1]

He describes early transport work and later management, then adds a personal choice: **“the funeral home business was not my passion.”** The edited text was read directly, not the recording. Do not reenact his driving anecdote. [1]

Interpretive thesis: a community history becomes more legible when visitors notice the work that families performed for each other, not only the buildings associated with prominent people.

### The class of one

Calvin C. Goode’s September 19, 2001 interview gives another dimension to Black Phoenix community work. As a student he wanted to continue mechanical drawing. The segregated school did not have the course, but Goode recalls that a shop teacher “formed a class of one.” He graduated in 1945 and later returned as school accountant. He recalls keeping the accounts from 1949 until the school closed in 1954. This is Goode’s memory in the Historical League’s edited transcript, not independent inspection of his transcript, class register or payroll. [8]

The federal nomination and the city’s property survey place the school in an established industrial district in 1926. The survey records that parents objected to the location. Put Goode’s particular classroom beside that civic record and Ragsdale’s account of family service work. The experiences are years and institutions apart. Together they return labor to a story that can otherwise reduce public life to landmark buildings. [1,8,10–12]

## 3. Then / now
ASU's February 2, 2026 collection announcement identifies **Eleanor and Lincoln Ragsdale Sr.** as business owners and civil-rights advocates. It documents a family archive encompassing photographs, correspondence, interviews and other records, and describes the family's involvement in school desegregation and civic organizing. These are the archive institution's contextual claims, not quotations from Eleanor. [5]

The same article gives an unusually useful present-day bridge: **Shelly Talas**, who helped process the collection, describes recognizing connections with her own insurance work and sharing what she learned with colleagues. She is a firsthand participant in archival work, not an eyewitness to mid-century events. [5]

A working archive is different from a commemorative facade. This dossier's suggested experience is comparing a named participant's recollection with the institution preserving the record. It does not claim that one prosperous family represents every Black household in Phoenix.

- Current Arizona Room access and winter exceptions remain unconfirmed. Building access does not settle archival access.
- The broader community requires additional voices, especially women speaking directly rather than being described by relatives or institutional summaries. This dossier does not claim completeness.
