## 2. The story
Before it was a battlefield, this was **Lahmotta**, a home of the White Bird Band. NPS describes families moving there as the threat of military response increased. Its account connects the June 1877 confrontation to an order requiring people outside the 1863 reservation boundary to relocate within thirty days. The reduction of homeland and the resulting displacement are essential context, not an optional preface to military tactics. [4]

The **Nez Perce Tribe's own June 2019 publication**, in its White Bird Memorial article, recounts the relocation order, the six-person peace party and civilian Arthur Chapman's opening fire on June 17. It records the cavalry retreat and Nimiipuu wounded without Nimiipuu deaths in that battle. The indexed article text was retrieved; the full 26 MB issue exceeded the web reader's limit. This is a Tribal-published remembrance, not an audio testimony or a manuscript examined firsthand. [5]

NPS's historical account names **Wettiwetti Howlis**, rendered there as Vicious Weasel, as leader of the peace party. It says they were instructed not to fire unless fired upon. This puts attempted parley, rather than an inevitable clash, at the center of the stop. A battlefield outcome did not mean that displaced families were now safe. [4]

The NPS trail booklet attributes this short statement to **Tuekakas, Joseph the Elder**: **“I claim the right to live on my land and accord you the privilege to live on yours.”** It is a published attributed historical voice, not a recording; the booklet does not establish the original interview/transcription provenance in the passage inspected. Do not present it as a statement spoken on this battlefield or on June 17. [6]

The Tribe's contemporary account of itself emphasizes continuing connection to lands and waters, language, culture and sovereignty. The Nimiipuu are not confined to an 1877 chapter. [7]

## 3. Then / now
Then: a village landscape, an approaching armed force, a peace party and the beginning of flight. Now: public interpretation makes part of that landscape viewable without licensing a visitor to occupy every place discussed in the account.

Read the hills as context, not as a board on which to replay a battle. The proposed interpretive emphasis is the narrow possibility of peace and the consequences for families, rather than who can locate a firing position most accurately. That is this dossier's ethical framing, not a claim that every on-site panel uses the same language.

## 4. What's still there

**Public access rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance. These access classifications are separate from the locator record.
- **Designated overlook and interpretive shelter:** documented by NPS as public viewing above the battlefield. Stay within the established pullout/viewing surfaces and obey current restrictions. [1]
- **Lower interpretive trail and waysides:** an NPS-managed alternative only when confirmed open and safe. Its existence does not create an obligation to walk it. No off-trail movement is proposed. [1]
- **Landscape:** terrain can help make the history comprehensible, but no present plant, building remnant, battlefield artifact or individual camp position is identified as a find.

The older booklet warns of uneven ground, cliffs, poison ivy and rattlesnakes, and states that there is no water at the trailhead. Its distances are internally inconsistent: it equates 1.2 miles with 2.5 km. Do not use that conversion or treat the booklet as a current accessibility assessment. [6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
A quiet, noncompetitive viewing sequence:
1. Before arrival, read the Tribal remembrance and check NPS restrictions. Bring the material offline; no live connectivity is assumed.
2. Enter only the signed, designated overlook when safely accessible. Orient from its interpretation, not from an archival pin or inferred combat position.
3. Read the account of the peace party. Keep the difference between a published historical account and a participant's original document visible.
4. Look over the landscape from the established viewing area, without trying to locate private, ceremonial, burial or sensitive places.
5. End with Tuekakas's attributed words and a period of silence. **Strongest story moment:** people approaching with a white flag still had a possibility of peace; the subsequent flight was lived by families, not simply maneuvered by armies.

Reflection question: **What changes when I understand this first as a home and an attempted parley, rather than only a battlefield?** No reenactment, posed combat photograph, object search or attendance at a ceremony is part of this stop.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- June 17, 1877 is consistent across the Tribal remembrance and NPS history. Exact casualty terminology varies; no reconciled casualty roll is supplied and no score uses deaths. [4,5]
- The Tribal newsletter was available through indexed text, not full issue inspection. The quote attributed to Tuekakas is mediated by an NPS publication; original-language wording and transmission remain unverified. [5,6]
- The booklet's metric/imperial trail-distance mismatch and a separate NPS outdoor-activities page's approximate 1.5-mile round trip mean this draft assigns no precise walk length. [6,8]
- General public access does not prove present-day winter usability. No claim of independent field inspection, current photographic alignment or step-free access.
- **White Bird Grade and White Bird Battlefield are distinct register entries.** Do not use the historic highway's record as the battlefield's entrance or permissions record. [9,10]
