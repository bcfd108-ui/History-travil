## 2. The story

The useful question in Miles City is not simply who owned the cattle. It is who did the work, who remembered it, and who decided what deserved saving.

The Library of Congress identifies **Paul E. Young**, a retired rancher living in Miles City, as a participant in its Montana Folklife Survey. Fieldworkers **Barre Toelken, Gary Ward Stanton and Paula Johnson** interviewed him over two days about his life as a cowhand, cowboy singer and bronco buster. [3] This is a named occupational voice, not an anonymous frontier character.

The catalog's indexed description of **part 2**, item **afc1981005_afs20407**, describes memories of music, an older sister's sheet music, dances and the people who played for them, alongside ranch work. [4] These are catalog-mediated descriptions: the audio has not been heard here, and a direct page fetch returned HTTP 403. No verbatim quotation, timestamp or listening claim is supplied. The social details matter because an account of stock work need not reduce its worker to a saddle or a job title. Family, recreation and memory belong in the story too.

Miles City's museum supplies a separate, community-authored account of preservation. **Charlie Wiley, Dale Wilder, Montana Bill Roberts, Harry Reed and Sid Vollin** initiated a museum effort in the late 1930s. A January 10, 1939 meeting drew more than thirty people, followed by many small donations. The original log building was dedicated August 22, 1942; the museum identifies it as its present entrance. [5]

The interpretive connection is ours: working lives become public history only through choices about testimony and objects. Young's recollections and the museum's founding story illuminate that process from different sides. They do not prove that his recording plays in the museum or that any particular displayed tool belonged to him.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** occupational knowledge circulated through people, work, music and stories. **Now:** recorded recollection and donated collections offer different windows on those lives. An interview preserves a person's selected memories; a museum label may preserve an object's maker or donor but omit its everyday user.

Compare those forms of evidence rather than claiming a surviving ranch scene. This dossier makes no assertion that a current street, yard or building reproduces the place Young described. It does not identify his former home or propose visiting it.

The museum's public origin account is itself historical interpretation. It is evidence of how the institution describes its creation, not a substitute for reading the original meeting minutes or donation ledger.

## 4. What's still there

The operator describes a **Wagon Depot** with antique vehicles, early office equipment and farm tools, as well as tool displays concerning ranching and other trades. It also lists cabins and a country-school exhibit. [6] These offer a possible material context for labor and community life, once open, but are not verified as Young-associated objects.

The seasonal museum is the lawful physical anchor. The LOC material is the remote research anchor. Neither grants access to a working ranch, auction yard or industrial facility. No live-animal viewing, demonstration or stock-sale attendance is part of this stop.
