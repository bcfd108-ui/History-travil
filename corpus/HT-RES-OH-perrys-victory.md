# HT-RES-OH-perrys-victory — Six officers from two fleets under one stone floor, and a column built for peace
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

On a narrow isthmus on South Bass Island, in Put-in-Bay, a granite Doric column rises 352 feet over Lake Erie — built by a nine-state commission after a battle fought from this harbor in 1813, carrying the names of the American dead and wounded in its rotunda, holding three American and three British officers beneath its floor, and standing, by its founders' stated purpose, to teach international peace by arbitration and disarmament rather than to celebrate a victory alone.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a national memorial on an island, in Put-in-Bay on South Bass Island, Ohio, reached by ferry, plane, or boat. The operator is the National Park Service (NPS), and the terms below are printed only as its own pages state them. The NPS plan-your-visit page states that the park is surrounded by 25 acres of landscaped ground, that the rotunda is free, and that a ticket acquired from the visitor center is required to visit the observation deck; its fees section states that the park grounds and visitor center are free. The NPS fees page, last updated August 6, 2026 as that page carries it, states that an entrance pass is not required and that entrance fees are currently waived due to a construction project affecting visitor access. The NPS operating-hours page states the standard visitor center hours for 2026 as: May 16–September 6, 10 a.m.–6 p.m. daily; September 7–28, 10 a.m.–5 p.m. daily; and September 29–October 26, 10 a.m.–5 p.m. on Fridays, Saturdays, Sundays, and Mondays. The same page states that the observation deck will not be accessible during the 2026 and 2027 seasons due to the Upper Plaza Rehabilitation, and the NPS visit-the-memorial page states that the plazas, rotunda, and observation deck will be inaccessible for the 2026 and 2027 seasons for the same project. No normal-season deck price is printed here because the current fees page states the waiver above; the $10 figure in older NPS pages is treated as a dated, normal-season figure and is discussed in §6. The memorial was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000118, per the NPS's own National Register record).

## 2. The story
The battle this column marks was fought for control of a lake. At the start of the War of 1812 the British held Lake Erie, and with it the supply route from Port Dover, Ontario, to Fort Malden (present-day Amherstburg), supporting their forces along the Detroit River. Command of the American effort was given to 27-year-old Master Commandant Oliver Hazard Perry, who built his squadron at Presque Isle, Pennsylvania through spring 1813 — floating his two big brigs out over a sandbar on pontoons while the British blockade was, for reasons the NPS says historians do not understand, temporarily lifted. In June 1813 the British fleet went to Commander Robert Heriot Barclay, himself only 26. By mid-summer Perry was based at Put-in-Bay, and by **September 9, 1813**, the NPS records, the British supply route had been cut off — which is why Barclay had to fight or abandon Fort Malden.

At dawn on **September 10, 1813**, a lookout aboard *Lawrence* sighted six British vessels northwest of Put-in-Bay, beyond Rattlesnake Island. The NPS battle detail gives the forces precisely: nine American vessels — three brigs (*Lawrence*, *Niagara*, *Caledonia*), five schooners, and one sloop — carrying 54 guns, against six British vessels carrying 63. Range cut the other way: British long guns were accurate to about 900 yards, American carronades threw a heavier charge at about 450, so Perry had to fight close and needed the wind behind him. His squadron sailed at 6 a.m. and struggled for hours; he had ordered a turn back when, at about 10 a.m. on the NPS battle detail's timing, the wind shifted southeast, directly behind the Americans. Before the lines met, Perry brought out his battle flag, crudely inscribed with Captain James Lawrence's dying words — DONT GIVE UP THE SHIP — and, in an account the NPS quotes from Erie dated **October 12, 1813**, mounted a gun slide and asked his men whether he should hoist it, receiving an answering "Aye, Aye" from all quarters. At **11:45 a.m.** *Detroit* fired the first shot at extreme range. For roughly two hours *Lawrence*, helped by only a few smaller vessels, took on the British squadron; by the NPS transcript's account Perry had lost close to 80 percent of his men and all his engaged carronades were disabled. The British were winning. Perry left his wrecked flagship, was rowed through the fire to the largely unharmed *Niagara*, and sailed her straight into the British line. *Detroit* and *Queen Charlotte*, attempting to wear ship, collided and opened the line; every British captain and first officer was by then killed or wounded; and at about half pistol range — some 30 yards, in Perry's own claim as the NPS transcript carries it — *Niagara*'s carronades pounded the British ships for about 15 minutes before they surrendered, vessel by vessel. Barclay, wounded twice, surrendered his entire squadron — a thing, the NPS article notes, that had never happened in British naval history. Perry's dispatch to Major General William Henry Harrison that day is the sentence the lake still repeats: "We have met the enemy and they are ours. Two ships, two brigs, one schooner and one sloop."

The consequences ran on land: Harrison's autumn invasion of Canada, Procter's retreat, and the death of Tecumseh — whose confederacy depended on British supply — in October 1813 at the Battle of the Thames, all on the NPS's account. The war ended with the Treaty of Ghent, ratified in **1815**, which left the boundary unsettled. The peace in the column's second name came with the Rush-Bagot Pact of **1817**, named for Richard Rush and Sir Charles Bagot, under which each side accepted set numbers and types of vessels on each of the Great Lakes — the start of the demilitarized border the memorial overlooks, five miles away (NPS).

The dead were buried before the peace was imagined. At the close of the combat, the NPS construction history records, the slain enlisted men of both fleets were committed to the waters of Lake Erie. On **September 11, 1813**, the crews of both fleets joined in a common ceremony in which six officers — three American and three British — were interred a few yards from the lakeshore, near what is now DeRivera Park. They lay there for a century.

The column came out of the centennial. A commission formed by nine states — Ohio, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, Illinois, New York, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Michigan, and Kentucky (NPS management page) — ran an international design competition, won by Joseph H. Freelander and Alexander D. Seymour Jr. The NPS memorial history, copied from Alfred Mongin's 1961 construction history, gives the sequence: the construction contract was signed on **September 10, 1912**; a 600-foot rail dock was built across the shallow bay to carry granite to the site; foundation excavation began on **December 4, 1912**, and was completed within 20 days; and on **July 4, 1913**, the cornerstone was laid. The shaft that rose on the isthmus — swampland and fill at the start, on the nomination's description — is pink Milford granite, 78 courses, 352 feet high and 45 feet in diameter at its base, capped by a bronze urn 23 feet high and weighing 11 tons (NPS brochure and nomination). The NPS calls it the most massive Doric column ever built, almost 50 feet taller than the Statue of Liberty.

On **September 11, 1913**, exactly a century after the joint burial, a casket bearing the six officers was raised from the shore gravesite and reinterred at the memorial; on completion a marble slab with a bronze-inscribed tribute was set in the rotunda floor above it. An NPS news release names the Americans moved that day as Midshipmen Henry Laub and John Clark and Lieutenant John Brooks. The rotunda's limestone tablets — the names of the American vessels and of the battle's dead and wounded — were hand-engraved intermittently from October 1913 through February 1915; a Tennessee marble floor followed in 1918; and the west doorway carries a bronze tablet with the 150-word Rush-Bagot Agreement of 1817 (nomination). The memorial opened to the public on **June 13, 1915** (nomination). Funding problems left the full complex unrealized; in 1919 the federal government assumed control, and the official dedication was celebrated on **July 31, 1931** — but see §6, where a second dedication year is carried unresolved, as is the conflict in the 1936 establishment dates.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a harbor base from which a nine-vessel American squadron sailed on September 10, 1813, fought a six-vessel British squadron beyond Rattlesnake Island, and returned to bury its officers on the shore. **Now:** a 25-acre landscaped memorial ground on an isthmus, from whose observation platform visitors can see where the battle was fought — in a normal season; for 2026 and 2027 the NPS states the deck, plazas, and rotunda are inaccessible for the Upper Plaza Rehabilitation.

**Then:** six officers of two enemy fleets buried together by the lakeshore on September 11, 1813, and left there for a hundred years. **Now:** the same six beneath the rotunda floor, reinterred September 11, 1913, marked by the marble slab and bronze tribute above them — buried together a second time, under a column built as a peace memorial.

## 4. What's still there
- The memorial column: a 352-foot fluted Greek Doric column of pink Milford granite, 78 courses, 45 feet in diameter at the base, capped by an observation platform and a bronze urn (NPS brochure text and National Register nomination)
- The rotunda in the column's base — Tennessee and Italian marble, Indiana limestone, and granite — its walls hand-engraved with the names of the American vessels and of the battle's dead and wounded (NPS memorial history and nomination)
- The crypt beneath the rotunda floor holding three American and three British officers killed in the battle, marked by the marble slab with its bronze-inscribed tribute (NPS memorial history; nomination)
- The bronze tablet in the west doorway of the rotunda carrying the 150-word Rush-Bagot Agreement of 1817 (National Register nomination)
- The 25 acres of landscaped grounds on the isthmus, bounded north and south by Lake Erie (NPS plan-your-visit page; nomination)
- The visitor center, with exhibits and a 15-minute film on the battle (NPS hours and visitor center pages)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the visitor center:** Start with the 15-minute film and exhibits, and take the fleets' measure as the NPS battle detail gives it — nine American vessels and 54 guns against six British and 63.
2. **On the grounds:** Cross the 25 landscaped acres on the isthmus — fill and lawn where swampland stood, water on both sides. This is Perry's base of action before and during the battle, on the NPS article's statement.
3. **At the column from below:** In the 2026 and 2027 seasons this is the visit the NPS closure notices leave: the shaft and its 78 granite courses read from the ground. Do not plan on the rotunda or deck in those seasons.
4. **In the rotunda, in a season when it is open:** The engraved names, the Rush-Bagot tablet, and the floor slab over the six officers. This is burial ground as well as exhibition space; the stop is a reading stop, not a photo opportunity over the slab.
5. **At the observation deck, in a season when it is open:** The platform at the cap, reached by 37 curving stone steps and the elevator above them (NPS visit page) — the view toward the battle waters, northwest toward Rattlesnake Island.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The dedication year conflicts in print and is carried unresolved. The National Register nomination states that the column was finished in 1915, the terraces completed in 1926, and the memorial dedicated on July 31, 1931; the same date is carried in the standard summaries. A Kenyon College page on the memorial, however, lists "Formal dedication July 31, 1932." Both are printed — July 31, 1931 (National Register nomination) and July 31, 1932 (Kenyon College page) — and neither is resolved.

The construction start is stated several ways. The NPS memorial history, copied from Mongin's 1961 construction history, dates the contract to September 10, 1912, and excavation to December 4, 1912; the NPS article on the memorial states it was "constructed between 1913 and 1915"; the NPS places record states 1912 to 1915; and the American Battlefield Trust's article states construction started in 1915. All are printed as their sources state them. The Mongin sequence is used in §2 because it alone gives the contract, dock, excavation, and cornerstone as separate dated steps, but the disagreement is not resolved.

The federal establishment date also carries two forms. The United States Code text (16 U.S.C. §433a) carries the act date June 2, 1936, while its note records establishment by Presidential Proclamation No. 2182 of July 6, 1936; an NPS brochure text likewise gives July 6, 1936, and October 26, 1972 for the redesignation. Both forms are carried here, unresolved.

The deck fee is a dated figure, not a current one. NPS pages from normal seasons state $10 — a July 19, 2021 news release states the fee remains $10 a person, usable for repeat visits for 7 days, children 15 and under free with an adult, card-only payment; a June 8, 2022 release likewise calls it "the $10 entrance fee to the Memorial's observation deck." The current fees page, last updated August 6, 2026, states instead that no pass is required and fees are currently waived due to construction. Section 1 prints the current page; this section preserves the older figure with its dates.

No coordinates are printed in the locator below. Coordinates circulate in lead-list and places data, but none was verified from a primary record scoped to a defined point: the nomination text consulted describes the location — the eastern edge of Put-in-Bay, on the isthmus, four miles from the mainland — without a coordinate this draft can scope, and the first photograph's NPGallery record carries no latitude or longitude. The fields stay blank rather than borrow a point from a lead list.

A further gap: battle casualty totals are not printed in §2, because the NPS summaries consulted phrase the losses non-identically and no single NPS casualty table was verified. The rotunda's count, as the NPS visit page states it, is 123 Americans killed and wounded, printed here only as that page states it.
