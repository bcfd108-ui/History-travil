## 2. The story
**Barboncito's demand to return home** anchors the reflection. Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian presents his appeal to Sherman: **“I hope to God you will not ask me to go to any other country except my own.”** This is a named historical participant's statement as published in a later institutional educational source, not an original manuscript independently examined here. [6]

The same resource publishes a contemporary Diné perspective from **Ezekiel Argeanas**, emphasizing women's weaving knowledge and sheep as part of ancestors' survival. It also identifies **Pete Price's** transmitted oral account of women's contribution to negotiations. These are distinct attributed voices, not interchangeable transcripts or claims that present-day speakers witnessed nineteenth-century events. [6]

Interpretive center: home was not merely a destination chosen by officials. Families and leaders insisted on belonging to their own homeland. Tell the history without turning suffering into an obstacle course, survival simulation or dramatic reenactment.

## 3. Then / now
The operator identifies forced confinement of **Diné (Navajo)** and **Ndé (Mescalero Apache)** people at Bosque Redondo. These are distinct peoples, not one undifferentiated community. Its state-authored account is context, not authority to speak on behalf of either sovereign nation. [7]

**Sovereign Navajo source:** the Navajo Nation Judicial Branch publishes the Treaty of 1868, headed with conclusion on **June 1**, ratification advised **July 25**, and proclamation **August 12, 1868**. These are different stages, not conflicting dates. Its parties are the United States and Navajo people; do not label it a joint Mescalero treaty or say sovereignty began only because the United States recognized it. This dossier uses the published historical record, not a current legal opinion. [8]

**Sovereign Mescalero source:** an October 17, 2025 post on the Mescalero Apache Tribe's official website describes **Historic Preservation Director Jacob Daukei** and **Cultural Center Director Joey Padilla** sharing Mescalero history during a memorial visit. That is the Tribe's own published account of ongoing historical stewardship, not a transcript of their presentation or a standing public tour invitation. [9]

## 4. What's still there
**Public interpretation:** the current operator identifies a permanent memorial exhibition. Its existence does not establish that every object or program is presently available. [7]

**Published historical and community voices:** the Smithsonian lesson and Navajo Nation treaty transcription can be read remotely. They provide different evidence types: curated quotations and oral-history interpretation versus a published treaty text. Neither authorizes reproduction of culturally sensitive material. [6,8]

**Living stewardship:** the Mescalero Tribe's own report establishes contemporary participation in interpretation. It does not authorize visitors to join ceremonies, seek out speakers personally, visit Tribal offices, or enter a community. [9]

The public sources suffice for a meaningful archive-first reflection. A physical visit may deepen attention, but does not make inaccessible or unverified ground part of this product.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Begin with attribution.** Read the speaker's name and the source type before the short historical appeal. Do not perform it in an invented accent or stage negotiations.
2. **Read the two nations separately.** Put the Navajo treaty record beside the Mescalero Tribe's contemporary stewardship statement without merging their histories.
3. **If a museum opening is independently confirmed, enter only its designated public visitor space.** The remaining sequence can otherwise take place remotely.
4. **Attend to what officials' records leave out.** Give the attributed account of women's contributions room alongside named negotiators, while retaining the limits of the surviving sources.
5. **End with home and continuing peoplehood.** The strongest moment is the insistence that people were not simply a population to be relocated. Pause rather than demand an answer, photograph or completion marker.

This is a reflective reading/visit progression, not a route across the site. Visitors may stop reading or leave emotionally difficult interpretation at any time. There is no reward tied to enduring distress or viewing images of captivity.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Ordinary winter hours remain unresolved: current operator extraction is blank, older DCA listing includes Wednesday, Friends and a dated ticket record exclude it. None is silently promoted to live confirmation. [1–4]
- The quoted historical statement was read in Smithsonian's published presentation, not checked against the original negotiation record or a Diné-language original. Translation and editorial transmission remain relevant limitations.
- **Diné-authored narrative versus sovereign publication:** an individually attributed Diné voice is not automatically a Navajo Nation official statement. Smithsonian is the publishing institution for the educational account; the Judicial Branch separately publishes the treaty. [6,8]
- No directly read Mescalero survivor transcript was obtained. The Tribal stewardship post is valuable primary institutional testimony about 2025, not nineteenth-century eyewitness testimony. [9]
- Counts in institutional summaries may represent different dates, cumulative arrivals or peak confinement. No single precise death or prisoner total is adopted here.
- An indexed older exhibition-script PDF was pursued but downloaded as HTML, not a readable PDF. Its indexed Florence Charley excerpt is not treated as inspected primary text.
- Ceremonies or historical events advertised for past dates are not current visitor permissions. Image rights, same-day weather and specific gallery availability remain open.
