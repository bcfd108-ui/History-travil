# Pullman National Historical Park — the Pullman company town, strike, and porters' ground
HT-RES-IL-pullman-national-historical-park · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

In 1879–80 George M. Pullman bought several thousand acres of prairie and marsh on the western shore of Lake Calumet, about fourteen miles south of downtown Chicago, and built there a factory and a town to go with it. Architect Solon Spencer Beman designed the buildings and landscape architect Nathan F. Barrett laid out the grounds. Construction began in May 1880 and the first residents moved in at the start of 1881. The National Register nomination describes the result, built 1880–84, as the first major effort in the United States to create a completely planned model industrial town in which industrial, cultural, religious, recreational, and residential structures were coordinated under one master plan. The company owned every building and charged rents intended to return a profit on its investment in the town. [3, 4, 6, 7, 9]

That control is the second half of the story. The Presidential proclamation establishing the monument records that Pullman's goal was to cure the social ills of the day, and that the tight control he exercised over his workers helped spark one of the nation's most widespread and consequential labor strikes. When the Panic of 1893 cut orders, the company lowered wages but did not lower the rents it charged for company housing. Workers walked out on May 11, 1894. Eugene V. Debs's American Railway Union took up a boycott of Pullman cars, rail traffic across much of the country was disrupted, federal troops intervened under President Grover Cleveland, and the strike ended violently by mid-July. At its peak, the proclamation records, it affected some 250,000 workers in 27 states. Congress designated Labor Day a federal holiday on June 27–28, 1894, while the strike was growing. [3, 4]

The third thread runs on the cars themselves. Pullman porters — throughout the company's history an exclusively African American workforce — staffed the sleeping cars. By 1937 the Pullman Company had been the nation's largest employer of African Americans for over twenty years. The Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, founded by A. Philip Randolph in 1925, won its contract from the company in 1937. The NPS calls that agreement the first major labor agreement between a union led by African Americans and a corporation, and an important marker since Reconstruction toward African American independence from racist paternalism. The district was designated a National Historic Landmark on December 30, 1970, proclaimed a National Monument on February 19, 2015, and redesignated Pullman National Historical Park in December 2022. [3, 4, 5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The original town site of about 300 acres, bounded in the nomination by E. 103rd St. on the north, the railroad spur on the east, E. 115th St. on the south, and Cottage Grove Ave. on the west. The industrial core stood between 108th and 111th Streets, separating residential districts north and south. [6, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Pullman National Historical Park Visitor Center, Administration-Clock Tower Building, 610 E. 111th St., Chicago. Use the visitor-center parking and public streets and sidewalks for the district walk. The Hotel Florence, Arcade Park, and the Greenstone Church (now Greenstone United Methodist Church) are district landmarks read from public ground unless a separate operator opens them. Private homes are not visitor stops. The Porter Museum at 10406 S. Maryland Ave. is a separate operator inside the district and is closed for construction in this run's check. [1, 2, 10, 11]

## 5. Public progression

Start inside the Clock Tower visitor center for the introductory exhibits, then walk the district on public sidewalks: the industrial core first, then the residential blocks north and south, reading the uniformity of the brick rowhouses as the plan made visible. Pause at the Hotel Florence and Greenstone Church exteriors. **Strongest story moment · Wages cut, rents held.** In 1893–94 the arithmetic of the company town turned against the people who lived in it — pay fell and rent did not — and a local walkout became a national boycott. Keep the porters' 1937 contract in the same walk: the town's labor story does not end in defeat in 1894. End by noting the Porter Museum's closed doors plainly, as a planned return visit, not a stop today. [1–5, 10]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Hours conflict printed in full: the NPS visitor-center page checked this run gives 9 a.m.–5 p.m. every day (winter 9 a.m.–4 p.m., Dec. 15–Mar. 10). Third-party listings retrieved in the same search gave Wed.–Sun. 10 a.m.–4 p.m. and other variants. Follow the NPS page and confirm by phone before a time-critical visit. [1]
- Address forms: the visitor-center address is given as 610 E. 111th St. for directions and parking; the building's Cottage Grove Ave. address (11001 S. Cottage Grove Ave.) appears as a mailing / building address in park materials. Use 610 E. 111th St. for navigation. [1]
- Landmark-date variance in secondary retellings: some secondary accounts give 1969 for the National Historic Landmark District designation. The NPS landmark article gives December 30, 1970. This dossier follows the NPS article; the nomination itself is dated 1969 in the register record (NRIS 69000054, listed Oct. 8, 1969). The two dates describe listing and landmark designation steps and are not reconciled further here. [4, 6]
- The Porter Museum's reopening is anticipated, not scheduled: its site has carried both a 2025 and a 2027 reopening expectation in different page versions retrieved this run. No reopening date is treated as firm here. [10]
- No interior access terms for the Hotel Florence or Greenstone Church were verified this run; neither is promised as an interior stop.
