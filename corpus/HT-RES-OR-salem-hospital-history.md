## 2. The story
Start with a person's agency rather than a diagnosis or a film set. **Shericea Deleon**, a former OSH patient publicly profiled in the September 29, 2023 *Recovery Times*, described becoming an outreach specialist and peer-group facilitator with Dual Diagnosis Anonymous. Her reported words include **“so I understand and can tell them what works for me.”** Her experience becomes a resource she chooses to share, not an exhibit subject outsiders define. [5]

This is a named professional's published account, mediated through an agency newsletter. It is not a full oral-history transcript, proof of every patient's experience, or permission to seek her out. No current patient's story, photograph or medical record is incorporated.

The museum's transcribed 1936 newspaper account offers a very different kind of voice. Its narrator, **“James R. Robblett,” is explicitly a pseudonym**, writing as told to reporter **Paul Hauser**. The introduction says identifying circumstances were altered, anecdotes from other patients were incorporated with permission, and hospital staff reviewed the text. The opening expresses both hope of leaving and ambivalence about institutional life. [6]

Do not treat this as an untouched personal diary or try to identify the narrator. The mediation is part of the history: whose story could reach print, under what editorial and institutional conditions? Its obsolete diagnoses and stigmatizing language are evidence of the period, not this dossier's language or current clinical advice. The original newspaper page was not independently collated.

A family perspective adds a boundary against convenient assumptions. In the same 2023 newsletter, **David Gilliland** explains that his great-great aunt, **Mary Ann “Minnie” Gilliland Smart**, remained present in family letters even though her remains had not been claimed. His account leaves important questions unanswered. Unclaimed does not automatically mean unloved, forgotten or without family. No grave, cremains directory or burial destination is supplied here. [5]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** Oregon State Hospital opened in 1883; its buildings and practices changed over time. The Kirkbride plan associated natural light, air and landscape with treatment, but architectural intention cannot prove humane treatment in every room or decade. [7]

**Now:** The museum describes itself as a private nonprofit interpreting people who lived and worked at the hospital. The operating hospital is separate and still serves people receiving care. A historical museum in one part of an institutional building does not make the larger institution a visitor attraction. [3,8]

The name also needs care: the separate Salem Hospital and the Oregon State Hospital have intersecting local histories but are not interchangeable institutions. Do not label every former North Campus feature an OSH patient ward or turn today's housing into a historical stop. [7]

The interpretive aim is not a simple march from barbarism to perfect modern care. Published survivor, peer, family and institutional accounts can illuminate different experiences without being forced into one reassuring conclusion.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Reflection sequence, not an activated campus route:
1. Read the publicly shared peer-worker account before looking at buildings. Begin with a person contributing knowledge, not an institutional label.
2. Check the museum-only gate. Without confirmed opening and clear museum access, complete the sequence remotely.
3. Compare the 1936 account's pseudonym, reporter collaboration and staff approval with the modern published account. Ask who controlled each publication without presuming the answer is identical.
4. Consider the family's insistence on remembrance without seeking burial locations or intimate records.
5. **Strongest ending:** Leave with the distinction between hearing a story a person chose to share and claiming access to that person's life. Their dignity does not depend on being inspiring, recovered, famous or useful to a visitor.

No role-playing admission, restraint demonstrations, horror framing, diagnostic guessing, “escape” challenge or compulsory emotional response. Content can be skipped; visitors may leave without completing anything.
