# Rosalie Mansion — the work between the kitchen and the table

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP02 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Enter the ticketed house tour at 100 Orleans Street, Natchez. The DAR gives November–February public hours of 10 a.m.–4 p.m. The ticket seller lists a 9 a.m. start and 4 p.m. last tour. Confirm a winter departure with the museum before setting out. The house tour is the public anchor. No stop here extends to the fort, bluff edge, river or cemetery. [1–2]

## 2. Story

The walk from Rosalie’s separate kitchen to its dining room was a regular journey for the enslaved people who prepared and served meals for the household. Nearly every object in the dining room could remain still only because people were moving between buildings. The museum guide describes a cooking hearth, a separate space to keep food warm, a larder for smoked meat and irons heated in rotation. People also slept upstairs in the kitchen building. The surviving layout makes those activities legible even when a visitor can enter only the main house. [3–4]

Peter and Eliza Little built Rosalie in the early 1820s. Eliza’s 1809 needlework sampler and Peter’s 1832 checkbook give two different sorts of dates to the house’s collections. Neither artifact dates construction. The Wilson family later occupied the house. During the Union occupation of Natchez in 1863 the parlors and library became headquarters space. Eliza Wilson and Fannie remained there. Annie and Rebecca Rumble sold Rosalie to the Mississippi State Society DAR in 1938 and stayed to guide visitors. Their interpretation became part of the house’s history in its own right. [1,3–4]

## 3. Then and now

The main house preserves the long hall between its formal rooms. Behind it survive the brick kitchen with living quarters and the smokehouse. The furnishings have a history of their own: the museum records purchases by Eliza Wilson and Fannie and identifies the current parlor carpet as a reproduction. The building, the surviving things and the later arrangement deserve separate dates. [3–4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Docent-led interior — rooms opened for that tour. Exterior — the kitchen and smokehouse within the operator’s public scope, as directed. The guide’s room descriptions are historical documentation, not independent access. [1,3–4]

## 5. Public progression

Begin with the library objects linked to the Littles. In the dining room, trace the meal backward to its warming room and hearth. **Strongest story moment:** a meal could be served at the formal table only after someone crossed the space from the kitchen. If the kitchen is closed, the published floor and room descriptions still support this reading from the house. [3–4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The museum and DAR date the Wilson purchase to 1857. The Society of Architectural Historians gives 1838. The present interpretation follows the museum’s dated family account provisionally; the recorded deed remains the deciding evidence. The tradition that James Shyrach Griffin designed the house lacks corroborating documentation in the independent architectural account. No cook’s name or firsthand account could be attached securely to the kitchen. [1,3–5]
