# HT-RES-VA-appomattox-court-house — The surrender was signed in a parlor, in a village named for a courthouse
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A stage-road village that happened to hold a county courthouse, a brick house borrowed for an afternoon, and the ninety minutes on April 9, 1865, in which the Army of Northern Virginia's war ended — with the paperwork of going home printed overnight in a tavern.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a village, not a single building: Appomattox Court House National Historical Park, the preserved village of Appomattox Court House in Appomattox County, Virginia, east of the modern town of Appomattox. The operator is the National Park Service. The NPS's own fees page states the terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: the park is **free**, and an entrance pass is **not required** to access Appomattox Court House National Historical Park. The NPS operating-hours page states the park is open daily from 9:00 to 5:00, open 9:00–2:00 on December 24, and closed on December 25, January 1, and Thanksgiving Day. The NPS directs visitors to begin at the reconstructed courthouse visitor center, which houses the park's exhibits and its 17-minute orientation film, "With Malice Toward None." The McLean House, where the surrender meeting took place, is open and staffed daily during the warm season and may be open on a limited schedule in winter, per the same page. The park was listed on the National Register of Historic Places under NRHP reference no. 66000827, per the NPS's National Register record.

## 2. The story
Appomattox Court House was a village before it was an event. It was the county seat of Appomattox County, and it took its name the way Virginia county seats did — from the courthouse standing in it. That naming is the source of the most durable confusion in American Civil War memory, and it is stated plainly here, on the NPS's own telling: **the surrender did not happen in the courthouse.** It happened in a private house, the home of Wilmer and Virginia McLean, a short walk away. The courthouse gave the village its name; the McLean parlor gave the village its history.

The McLean House itself had an ordinary beginning. Per the NPS, it was originally constructed in **1848** as the guest house for the Raine Tavern. Wilmer McLean had come to the village from Prince William County, in northern Virginia — where, as Gardner's *Photographic Sketch Book of the War* noted at the time, he had been living on the first Bull Run battlefield when that battle was fought in 1861, and had afterward removed to this quiet place, in the Sketch Book's words, for the purpose of being secure from the visitation of an army. The army arrived anyway, four years later, at the end of a retreat that had begun when Petersburg fell.

On the morning of **April 9, 1865** — Palm Sunday — Lee's Army of Northern Virginia made one last attempted breakthrough against the Federal forces that had surrounded it. The attempt failed. As the smoke of battle cleared, in the NPS's phrasing, Lee asked for terms of surrender. The meeting place chosen was the McLeans' home. The two commanders met in the parlor for about an hour and a half: Lt. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant accepted Gen. Robert E. Lee's surrender on terms the NPS describes as generous, designed to pacify the white southern population. The NPS page adds the fact the parlor photographs never show: nearby, **nine enslaved people** of the McLean household were now free to live independently of the McLean family, with all of freedom's challenges and opportunities.

The surrender of one army was not paperwork that finished itself in an afternoon. On **April 10** Grant and Lee met again, and the McLean House was used that day for the Surrender Commissioners meeting at which the details were finalized. The terms allowed Confederate soldiers to be paroled and return home rather than to prison, and each man was to receive an individual parole pass certifying he would not take up arms against the United States. Per the NPS, approximately **30,000 blank passes were printed at the Clover Hill Tavern in just 26 hours**. The formal surrender of the Confederate infantry — the stacking of arms and flags — followed on **April 12**, opened by the Gordon–Chamberlain salute the NPS's village interpretation describes. In the village's east end, the NPS also interprets what freedom meant on the ground: John Robinson, emancipated at the surrender, helped found the first African American church in Appomattox.

Then the village's second story began, and it is mostly a story of the house being taken apart. The McLeans left in the fall of 1867. When Wilmer McLean defaulted on loans, the "Surrender House" was sold at public auction on **November 29, 1869**; in 1872 Nathaniel H. Ragland purchased the property for $1,250. On **January 1, 1891** the widow Ragland sold it for $10,000 to Captain Myron Dunlap of Niagara Falls, New York, and a syndicate of speculators. Their plans ran from dismantling the house and moving it to Chicago as an exhibit at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, to rebuilding it in Washington, D.C., and charging entrance fees. Measured drawings and materials lists were made, the house was dismantled and packed for shipping — and then, through cash-flow and legal problems, the plan collapsed. The NPS's sentence for what followed is the bleakest in this entry: the home sat dismantled in piles, prey to vandals, collectors, and the environment, for fifty years.

The courthouse outlived the surrender house as a building, but not by long in its working life: the original courthouse **burned in 1892**, and with the county seat's business gone elsewhere the village faded. What stands in its place now is the NPS's reconstruction, rebuilt in **1964** as the park's visitor center.

The federal rescue of the surrender ground came on **April 10, 1940**, when Congress created Appomattox Court House National Historical Monument, including approximately 970 acres. Archaeological work began in February 1941 on a site the NPS describes as overgrown with brush and honeysuckle; historical data was collected and architectural working plans drawn. The whole project stopped on December 7, 1941, with the attack on Pearl Harbor. Bids for reconstructing the McLean House were opened on November 25, 1947. The NPS hours page records what the reconstruction crews found to work with: when the home was rebuilt, only **5,500 bricks** of the original house remained. On **April 9, 1949** — eighty-four years to the day after the meeting — the reconstructed McLean House opened to the public. At the dedication ceremony on **April 16, 1950**, after a speech by historian Douglas Southall Freeman, direct descendants of Grant and Lee cut the ceremonial ribbon together, before an audience the NPS puts at approximately 20,000. The NPS closes its McLean House account with the honest label this entry adopts: although the building has been reconstructed, it provides an excellent representation of the structure that witnessed the surrender meeting.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a working county-seat village on a stage road — courthouse, taverns, stores, law offices, houses — that an army's collapse turned, for one April week in 1865, into the place a war ended. **Now:** the same village kept as a National Historical Park, its lanes walked between restored and reconstructed buildings, administered from the rebuilt courthouse.

**Then:** a brick guest-house-turned-residence, built 1848, whose parlor held Grant and Lee for about ninety minutes on April 9, 1865. **Now:** that house standing again on its ground — dismantled in the 1890s for a showman's scheme that failed, left in piles for fifty years, and rebuilt by the NPS from measured drawings and its own surviving 5,500 bricks, opened April 9, 1949.

**Then:** some 30,000 parole passes run off at the Clover Hill Tavern in 26 hours so surrendered men could walk home as paroled soldiers rather than prisoners. **Now:** in summer, park staff work the tavern press and print passes for visitors, on the NPS's current program schedule — the village's paperwork, still being printed in the village.

## 4. What's still there
- The McLean House (reconstructed, opened April 9, 1949): the surrender parlor, in a house the NPS states was built in 1848 as the Raine Tavern's guest house and rebuilt from 5,500 surviving original bricks and measured drawings
- The reconstructed courthouse visitor center (rebuilt 1964 on the NPS's hours page): exhibits and the 17-minute film "With Malice Toward None," in the building whose 1892 burning ended the original courthouse's life
- The Clover Hill Tavern, where approximately 30,000 parole passes were printed in 26 hours in April 1865, and where summer printing demonstrations continue (NPS hours page)
- The village lanes and the east end of the village: the Kelley/Robinson house, the site marking the Confederate stacking of arms, and the ground of the second Grant–Lee meeting of April 10, 1865 (NPS hours page)
- The village's other restored buildings — tavern guest house, stores, law office, jail, and houses — walked as one 1865 streetscape (NPS park interpretation)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the reconstructed courthouse visitor center:** Start where the NPS directs — exhibits and the 17-minute film — standing in the building that is *not* where the surrender happened, and hearing why.
2. **At the McLean House:** The parlor, ninety minutes on April 9, 1865 — and the house's own stranger story: built 1848, dismantled 1893, fifty years in piles, rebuilt and opened April 9, 1949.
3. **At the Clover Hill Tavern:** The paperwork of peace — 30,000 parole passes in 26 hours — and, in summer, the press still printing them.
4. **On the village lanes east:** The stacking-of-arms ground, the April 10 meeting site, and John Robinson's story — emancipation made local, in the village where it arrived with the surrender.
5. **Back at the courthouse steps:** The naming lesson to carry out: the village is called Court House, the courthouse burned in 1892 and was rebuilt in 1964, and the surrender was in the house down the lane the whole time.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The surrender's location confusion is not a gap but a standing error in popular memory, printed here as the NPS carries the correction: the village is named Appomattox Court House for its courthouse, and the surrender meeting of April 9, 1865, was held in the McLean House parlor. Any account placing Grant and Lee in the courthouse is wrong, and this entry's stop progression is built to make the distinction on the ground.

The McLean House's rebuilding year is carried two ways by the NPS's own pages. The park's operating-hours page states, "When the home was rebuilt in **1948**, only 5,500 bricks remained," while the NPS McLean House history dates the opening of the reconstructed house to **April 9, 1949**, with bids opened November 25, 1947. Read together, the pages describe a reconstruction carried out across 1948 and opened in 1949; neither page says that in so many words, so both years are printed as their pages carry them and the join is labeled as this draft's reading, not the NPS's statement. The dismantling year is likewise carried as 1893 on the hours page and described without a single year on the history page (sold January 1, 1891; dismantled after measured drawings were made); 1893 is used in §2 as the hours page carries it.

The NPS National Register record consulted for this entry (NRIS 66000827) was identified by its record number and title; its full record text, including any published listing date, could not be re-read in this session after a rate-limit failure on the NPGallery service, so no listing date is printed for it here rather than borrowed from a lead list. The park's size is carried by the NPS McLean House history as approximately 970 acres at the monument's creation on April 10, 1940. Later park totals in other NPS materials were not reconciled against that figure in this session, and no current acreage is printed.
