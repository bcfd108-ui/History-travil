## 2. The story

A newspaper reaches a reader as a seemingly finished object: a masthead, a headline, a sheet folded at the door. The labor story begins by taking that object apart. Reporting, composition, plate-making, paper handling, press operation and distribution are different jobs. Their cooperation can disappear from the reader's view precisely because the daily production sequence works.

**Named participant voice:** Gene Klare wrote in his 19 January 2001 *Northwest Labor Press* column, **“I worked as a union reporter at the Oregonian until 5 a.m. Tuesday, Nov. 10, 1959”**. He described honoring the stereotypers' picket lines and later working for the union-supported *Portland Reporter*. This is his own published recollection, written decades after the event and openly from a union perspective. It is not a neutral court finding or a recording newly transcribed for this dossier. [3]

Klare's 19 November 1999 column reproduces an earlier account by **George Roe**, an Oregonian printer and member of Multnomah Typographical Union No. 58. Roe described disagreement over staffing a plate-making machine, and the solidarity of other trades whose contracts were still in effect. That distinction matters: the people who made a paper were not interchangeable, yet their collective action could connect the composing room to the mailroom. Roe's 1980 original was not independently retrieved; this portion is a participant account carried within another participant's later column. [4]

Independent archival context confirms the underlying rupture without requiring acceptance of every allegation in those columns. OHS's collection history records the 1959 strike at both the *Oregonian* and *Oregon Journal*, Newhouse's 1961 purchase of the *Journal*, and the latter paper's final closure in 1982. The guide describes a four-year dispute, a chronology that needs qualification below rather than silent harmonization. [5]

The labor response was also productive work, not just withdrawal. The Labor Press's institutional archive records that Klare helped establish the *Portland Reporter*, published February 1960–September 1964, and worked in reporting, advertising and promotion. This makes the most compelling story moment a competing newspaper: workers attempting to preserve both a livelihood and a public voice. [6]

## 3. Then / now

Then, the disputed work was tied to newspaper production plants and editorial offices. Now, the public reading room allows a visitor to compare traces of that system without entering an industrial workplace. **OHS is an interpretive/research anchor, not the original strike site.**

An issue produced during a dispute is not simply a container of information about the dispute. It is also a result of a labor arrangement. Whose account reached print, whose skills made that possible, and what publication could a reader choose instead? Those questions are the interpretive comparison; no current staffing structure or present employer practice is inferred from the historical conflict.

## 4. What's still there

OHS preserves the **Oregonian news negatives and photographs, Org. Lot 1445**, dating 1980–2013. The collection includes assignment folders and production-related information, but it is incomplete and much later than the 1959 strike. It must not be presented as a strike-photo collection. Its finding aid identifies related earlier and news-production collections, useful leads rather than already-examined boxes. The collection is open for research, with possible preservation limits on negatives and digital media. [5]

Separately, Multnomah County Library's operator-authored newspaper guide lists print holdings of the **Portland Reporter, 1960–1964**, identifying it as a union-published alternative. This corroborates the survival of the rival paper as a research object. It does not establish that those issues are physically available at OHS, or add a second library visit without a separate location and retrieval check. [7]

The public online Klare columns survive as readable testimony. No surviving press machinery, strike placard or labor-history exhibit is promised on the museum floor.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Before departure:** Read Klare's brief first-person employment statement and distinguish it from his broader interpretation. Check the library gate, not museum hours. If the purpose depends on originals, keep the visit conditional until their availability is separately established.
2. **At the public lobby:** Follow the research-library check-in process. Do not treat being inside OHS as permission to enter storage or staff areas.
3. **At a reading table:** Compare the dated participant account with the archive's institutional chronology. Record differences rather than editing them out. If originals are unavailable, this remains a valid online/document-reading stop, not a failed attempt at a factory tour.
4. **End with the strongest story moment:** Put the idea of the *Portland Reporter* beside the dominant papers. The people who stopped producing one newspaper also attempted to produce another. Ask: **When newspaper workers become news, who has the machinery, money and distribution to tell their story?**

This is a proposed interpretive sequence, not a booked program, verified exhibit order or claim that staff will provide a guided presentation.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Strike end:** Klare's 2001 column gives 4 April 1965; the Labor Press archive gives 5 April 1965. OHS's historical note calls the strike four years long, while Multnomah's guide calls it three years. These descriptions are not reconciled by inventing a common endpoint. The beginning in November 1959 is consistently supported; later stages and formal termination need original union records. [3][5][6][7]
- **Local number:** Klare/Roe print Stereotypers Local 49; a retrieved contemporary congressional search excerpt uses Local 48. The dossier does not resolve this discrepancy or use an unverified number as a locator.
- **Perspective:** Union accounts contain pointed claims about management motives and violence. Those are not adopted here as independently proven facts. No private residential location or allegation is made a visitor target.
- **Originals:** Roe's full 1980 account, original labor agreements and a matched pair of newspaper issues were not inspected. Klare's published column was read; no oral-history audio was listened to.
- **Access:** Library opening does not guarantee a requested box, microfilm reader, appointment slot or individual accommodation. No retrieval or travel-condition confirmation was obtained.
