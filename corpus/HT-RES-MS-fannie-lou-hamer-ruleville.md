# Fannie Lou Hamer, the timekeeper who challenged a delegation

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP20*

## 2. Story

Fannie Lou Hamer’s address at the 1964 Democratic National Convention was also a description of work. In her testimony before the Credentials Committee she described years as a timekeeper and sharecropper, traveling from Ruleville to try to register, and the demand that she withdraw her attempt or leave the plantation. The archived hearing transcript records the names of neighbors, the trip and her decision in her own account. Her political work demanded time, income and protection for people whose control over those things was already limited. [3]

Hamer told the Credentials Committee why she was speaking: “we want to register, to become first-class citizens.” Those words are from the National Archives’ printed copy of the hearing transcript. She spoke on behalf of a Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party challenge to a delegation she argued did not represent Black Mississippians excluded from voting. She described violence and intimidation as experiences she and her neighbors carried into the hearing. The printed transcript identifies where and when she made her case. It is not a photograph or original audio of the moments she described. [3][4]

Later in Ruleville her work included helping people obtain food and services. The family’s interpretation records her Freedom Farm efforts, the Pig Bank and a local museum maintained by movement participants, including the late Hattie Jordan. It identifies a typewriter used by SNCC worker Charles Maclaurin to prepare letters and reports. This part of the historical record puts campaigning beside practical office and community work. The museum and garden are separate projects that preserve different pieces of it. [1][4]

## 3. Then and now

The Ruleville organizing community, plantation work and the hearing in Atlantic City were distinct historic settings. The local memorial garden was dedicated in 2002, the family project says. The garden's statue and interpretation commemorate Hamer. At the nearby museum, documented objects are part of a separately staffed, appointment-listed operation. The modern reflection ground does not stand for every location named in her testimony. [1–4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Public memorial exterior.** The officially identified memorial garden around the public entrance and visible interpretation, subject to posted gates and conditions. Keep the solemn parts of the garden free of visitor activity prompts. [1][2]
- **Separate museum interior.** The Ruleville family resource identifies the museum in the park and multipurpose complex. Its site lists appointment-only access. This guide sends no walk-up visitor there. [1]
- **Historic work and family ground.** The hearing records locations that explain the story. No plantation, home or burial spot is treated as a route target. [1][3]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

If the public signing and gate allow a visit, read the exterior interpretation quietly without adding a grave stop. **Strongest story moment — First-class citizens.** Place the exact line from the committee hearing alongside the account of leaving a job she had held for years. The language on the public memorial can help a reader understand how much was put at risk by attempting to register. [1–3]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The project run by Hamer’s family gives a Byron Street number of 726, while Visit Mississippi links the garden at 929 Byron Street. Both name the same memorial. The conflicting address and outdoor gate hours remain open. Use the named signed public memorial rather than a numeric locator. [1][2]
- The Ruleville museum is separate from a similarly named civil rights museum in Belzoni. The Belzoni operator’s page refers to its own appointment arrangements and cannot carry Ruleville access. Current walk-up indoor access is not verified at either site in this dossier. [1][5]
- The archived statement carries the formal hearing wording. The original recording was not independently synchronized against this transcript. No historical frame of the garden with verified rights was retrieved. [3][6]
