## 2. Story

The people who became the Rodney Presbyterian congregation had been part of Bethel across the county line. A state charter in 1828 recognized worshippers at Petit Gulf, the name used before that year’s act incorporating Rodney. The state nomination distinguishes the civil charter from church governance. Rodney worshippers petitioned the presbytery in 1852 to become a separate congregation. Their surviving record book opens in February of that year. That means the paper trail for the new congregation starts two decades after the church building’s dedication. [1–4]

John Russell Hutchison knew the congregation as a minister. In 1874 he recalled an institution that grew from improvised worship into a building project. On p. 33 of his printed recollections he writes, “Early in 1829 steps were taken to erect the present brick church at Rodney.” He names Jeremiah Chamberlain as the minister who preached the dedication sermon on January 1, 1832. He remembers a final $1,500 claim by the builder and says David Hunt paid it. The minister names the person who paid, and the builder whose money was still owed remains unnamed in this passage. The account is a recollection published more than forty years later. The society repeats the dedication date. The original construction contract and any payment records are still open. [1,3,5]

Hutchison also records how members pledged money for nearby Oakland College. Chamberlain had duties at Rodney, Bethel and Oakland, and Hutchison later taught ancient languages while serving as a pastor. Those linked assignments help explain how a small congregation could sustain a building and participate in a larger educational project. Oakland’s campus subsequently became Alcorn University. Put both institutional histories beside one another while keeping the two sites and their access boundaries distinct. [3,5–6]

## 3. Then and now

The nomination calls Rodney a former river settlement and explains how its original place-name related to an eddy in the Mississippi. The brick church later lost the congregation that had built and maintained it. The current private preservation society acquired it in 2018 and has requested support for stabilization. Its ability to survive as a structure is a different matter from its usability as a public destination. [1–3]

## 5. Reading progression

Read Hutchison’s few lines on the construction debt beside the nomination’s account of the separate church petition. **Strongest story moment — The bill after the dedication.** The building people remember for its front and its military associations was also a work site where a builder was still owed money after worship began. ## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The 1828 charter, 1832 dedication and 1852 organization are different events. The current society dates the church building and dedication consistently with Hutchison’s account. The federal listing treats 1863 as significant. A visual feature widely taken as physical evidence of that incident needs provenance before it can be described as surviving shot. The dossier holds the military incident’s detail until original correspondence establishes which claims can be supported. [1,3,5,7]

Mississippi’s archive describes collection Z/0828 as microfilm only and gives roll 36073. The manuscript images were not available from the finding aid. Original building contracts, masons and joiners, a parcel-specific public viewpoint, and the latest structural report remain open. [1,3–4]
