# HT-RES-PA-johnstown-flood-national-memorial — The failure of the South Fork Dam, May 31, 1889
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A canal reservoir, finished in 1852 and later held by a private fishing and hunting club, whose dam failed on the afternoon of May 31, 1889 and sent the water of Lake Conemaugh down the Little Conemaugh valley into Johnstown. The National Park Service preserves what is left of the dam, part of the empty lakebed, and buildings of the club that owned both.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is to Johnstown Flood National Memorial, a unit of the National Park Service in Cambria County, about ten miles northeast of Johnstown. The park preserves the ruins of the South Fork Dam, part of the old lakebed, and some of the buildings of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. The visitor center is at **733 Lake Road, South Fork, PA 15956**. [1][2]

The Visitor Center is open Fridays through Tuesdays from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., and is closed on Wednesdays and Thursdays. It is also closed on Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Day, and New Year's Day. Park grounds and trails are open daily, year round, from sunrise to sunset, weather permitting. There is no entrance fee. [3]

In the visitor center, the National Park Service shows a park-produced film, "Black Friday," which attempts to recreate the flood; the visitor center holds displays on the history of the South Fork Dam and the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. Directions as the National Park Service gives them: take US Route 219 to the St. Michael / Sidman exit, head east on PA 869 about 1.5 miles, turn left onto Lake Road, and follow it about 1.5 miles to the visitor center. [2][4]

The memorial was authorized as a National Park Service unit on August 31, 1964, and is dedicated to the lives lost in the flood of May 31, 1889. The National Park Service gives the park at 178 acres (72.0 hectares). [5]

## 2. The story
The dam was built for a canal. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania envisioned the South Fork Dam as a reservoir for the Western Division of the Pennsylvania Main Line Canal, to supply the canal in times of low water; Sylvester Welch, principal engineer of the Allegheny Portage Railroad, was its principal engineer. From design to finish the dam took well over a decade, and it was finished in **1852**, when canals were already on their way out. The Commonwealth sold the property; it was owned in turn by the Pennsylvania Railroad, by a local businessman and one-time congressman named John Reilly, and finally by the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. [4]

The club was the idea of Benjamin F. Ruff, a tunnel contractor and sometime real-estate salesman from the Pittsburgh area. It was legally created as a nonprofit corporation in **1879**, with Ruff as a chief stockholder and, it is believed, its president until his death in March 1887. Over its ten years the club grew from 16 members to, it is believed, 61 in 1889. Its lake — Lake Conemaugh — covered about 450 acres and provided fishing, hunting, and boating for the members. Colonel Elias Unger was president of the club in 1889; his farm stood above the dam. [4][6]

On **May 31, 1889**, after heavy rain, men worked on the dam through the morning as the lake rose. John Parke, a young engineer recently arrived to supervise installation of a sewer system at the club, rode to South Fork at about 11:00 a.m. to spread word of the dam's condition; at least three warnings went out from South Fork down the valley that day, the last believed to have reached Johnstown at just about 3:00 p.m. The National Park Service account of the day at the dam is drawn from first-person accounts by Unger, Parke, William Y. Boyer, the club's Superintendent of Lake and Grounds, and several others. [4]

The dam failed after 3:00 p.m. — most accounts say either **3:10 or 3:15**. It is accepted that the flood struck Johnstown proper at **4:07 p.m.** The lake emptied down the valley. In Johnstown, the wreckage the water carried collected against the arches of the Pennsylvania Railroad's stone bridge (see the photo record, §7). [4]

The count of the dead, as the National Park Service prints it: a list published about fourteen months after the flood set the death toll at **2,209**. The Service notes that some victims were never included in that count, and that the actual toll was probably well over 3,000. Section 6 prints that as the open question it is. [4]

Clara Barton arrived with a team on Wednesday, **June 5, 1889**. Her branch of the American Red Cross sheltered survivors in large buildings known as "Red Cross Hotels," some standing into early 1890, and provided meals, provisions, and medical care. The Johnstown Flood is considered the first major civilian disaster relief effort of the American Red Cross, then less than ten years old. A separate Philadelphia branch of the Red Cross, not associated with Barton, also came. [4]

The South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club was never held legally responsible for the flood, though it was held responsible in public opinion. The only successful cases arising from the flood were against the Pennsylvania Railroad Company, which lost two cases based on loss of property. It is not clear what became of the club's papers; the National Park Service notes a suspicion that much was thrown out in 1917 when the law firm that had represented the club moved buildings. [4]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** an earthen dam finished in 1852 to feed a canal, holding back a 450-acre lake stocked and kept for a private club, with cottages and a clubhouse on its shore and a working-class city in the valley below it. **Now:** the dam stands as two remaining abutments with the middle gone. Visitors can stand on either abutment and look out over the empty lakebed to gauge how much water the lake held. [6]

**Then:** Lake Conemaugh, two miles of held water above the Little Conemaugh valley. **Now:** the lakebed is managed as a wet meadow, with plant, tree, and shrub species on both sides of the South Fork of the Little Conemaugh River, which runs through it again. The memorial also holds the farm of Elias Unger, the club's president in 1889, and the clubhouse of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club. [4][6]

## 4. What's still there
- The remains of the South Fork Dam — both abutments, with the breach between them (see the National Park Service photograph record for the dam remains in the source list)
- The former Lake Conemaugh bed, managed as a wet meadow, with the South Fork of the Little Conemaugh River running through it
- The Unger House and the farm of Elias Unger, president of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club in 1889
- The clubhouse and other buildings of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club preserved by the park
- The visitor center at 733 Lake Road: displays on the dam and the club, and the National Park Service film "Black Friday"
- Park grounds and trails, open daily from sunrise to sunset, including the ground at the dam itself

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The death toll is not a settled number, and this entry does not settle it. The National Park Service prints the figure from the list published about fourteen months after the flood — 2,209 — and states in the same answer that some victims were never included and the actual toll was probably well over 3,000. Contemporary print ran far higher still: the 1889 photograph in §7 carries its publisher's line "Loss from 10,000 to 12,000 lives" on the print itself. That line is evidence of what was claimed in 1889, not a count this entry adopts. [4]

The dam's failure time is given by most accounts as either 3:10 or 3:15 p.m.; the National Park Service prints both and fixes only the arrival at Johnstown, 4:07 p.m. This entry does the same. [4]

The dam's construction start is not fixed in the National Park Service sources consulted here: the Service's account gives the design-to-finish span as "well over a decade" and the finish as 1852, without a start year this entry can print. The club's membership in 1889 (61) and Ruff's presidency are printed by the Service with its own qualifiers ("it is believed"), and are carried here with those qualifiers. [4]

Interior access, tour schedules, and opening arrangements for the Unger House and the clubhouse were not established from National Park Service sources in this pass. Accessibility specifics for the trails at the dam and lakebed are likewise unverified here. No booking, permission request, or contact occurred.
