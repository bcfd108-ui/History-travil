## 2. The story

**Pauline Olson's own accounting.** The State Historical Society publishes a transcription of her diary entry marking **September 2, 1949**, when the household changed from its earlier electrical supply to line service. She recorded a twelve-year wait and wrote: **“Pretty Late in life to get this”**. The displayed transcription was actually read; the manuscript image and complete volume were not examined. [3]

**Convenience carried a price.** Her entry itemizes **$316.98** for house-and-barn wiring, **$100** for the replacement cream separator and a refrigerator calculation of **$330 minus $25 = $305**. The remaining arithmetic is not cleanly explained in the transcription. It should not be converted into a confident total household investment. [3]

**A person, not an anonymous farm statistic.** The institutional introduction identifies Olson as **1885–1969**, married to John Jacob Olson, raising four children in Bottineau County. Her diary began in 1926 and covered weather, family life and farm business as well as technological change. Its six volumes are identified as **Pauline Olson Diaries, 1926–1969, Mss 10140**. Those details establish a North Dakota household nexus without turning the family's land into a destination. [4]

**Original interpretation.** The strongest story is not a glowing bulb. It is the collision of promised relief, money spent and time already lived. A household ledger can register the meaning of public infrastructure more sharply than a list of institutional founding dates. This is interpretation of the cited evidence, not a claim that every household shared her circumstances.

## 3. Then / now

**Two federal dates, not one.** USDA's historical chronology distinguishes the administration's creation by **Executive Order 7037, May 11, 1935**, from statutory provision in the **Rural Electrification Act approved May 20, 1936**. The chronology on printed page iii was read. This is historical context, not current financing or electrical advice. [5]

**Local organization mattered.** ND Studies describes a partnership of federal lending and locally organized cooperatives, against the difficulty of serving widely separated farms. That account helps explain why an announcement in Washington and household service were different events. Its broad percentages are not used here as a measure of the Olson family's circumstances. [6]

**The present encounter is documentary.** A visitor can approach the surviving record in a public research setting. No claim is made that the historical home, appliances or electrical installation survive, are publicly accessible, or appear in a current museum exhibition.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Before leaving:** read the brief household entry online. Decide whether the archive offers a specific source that makes a visit worthwhile, rather than building a drive around the abstract subject of electricity.

**At a confirmed public research appointment:** begin with the collection identity and the distinction between transcription and original. Follow staff rules; request only the material actually needed. No appointment or retrieval has occurred in this research.

**Build the story in layers:** place the national program chronology beside the local institutional context, then return to the household record. A policy date, a service date and a personal interpretation are three different kinds of evidence.

**End at the strongest human moment:** leave space for the writer's sense of arriving late to a change. The close is quiet recognition of the years behind a convenience, not a staged switch-on, equipment demonstration or search for the family's property.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**An error in the interpretive wrapper matters.** The ND Studies REA page first calls 1935 the act year, then describes congressional passage in 1936. The older modernization introduction also compresses the early legislative history and says cooperatives began organizing in 1949. Do not generalize that latter date to the entire state. USDA's separate 1935/1936 chronology governs the federal dates here. [4][5][6]

**Primary-source boundary:** the household voice is a published diary transcription, not a modern oral-history recording. No narrator audio, original handwriting verification or complete diary reading is claimed. The missing original-page check remains important for ambiguous figures.

**Representation limit:** this household cannot stand in for all farmers, tenants, hired workers or Tribal communities. No Tribal account has been examined for this unit, and none is manufactured. Worker organization and household purchasing deserve separate corroboration before a broader interpretation.

**Travel gap:** current public archival rules were read, but no specific visit date, retrieval availability or seasonal exception has been confirmed. This is a research draft, not a cleared itinerary or acceptance decision.
