## 2. The story

Begin with a letter trying to find its readers, rather than a company map. In April 1863 William Kaulehelehe, formerly a Hawaiian teacher and religious worker at Fort Vancouver, compiled addresses for Hawaiians scattered around the Northwest. The letter was published in *Ka Nupepa Kuokoa*, June 6, 1863, page 4; Bishop Museum publishes a translation. His concern was practical: confused names and place names meant letters failed to reach people. He urged senders to get the address right so recipients could receive **“the loving sentiments of their friends of their land of birth.”** This is his published written voice in translation, not an audio recording. [5]

NPS historian Marie Hashimoto describes the tension in his earlier position: company leaders wanted religious instruction and discipline, while Hawaiian workers wanted an advocate. William and Mary Kaai arrived in 1845. Mary is not merely a footnote to an employee list: their household moved between the Village and stockade, and remained after the company's withdrawal. NPS records the Army's forced removal and burning of their home in 1860, followed by their move to Victoria. Their house is not one of today's reconstructed cabins. [6]

A contemporary Hawaiian research voice matters too. University of Hawaiʻi Native Hawaiian Student Services' 2026 account, by Kaʻuikikaikaimālie Kalama and Kanoniakapueo Kane, reads Mary's relationships through the funeral chants published after her death. It identifies women who described her as an adoptive mother or elder sister. That reading brings household and kinship back into a landscape often explained through corporate employment. It also warns how little of Mary's experience survives in her own words. Do not manufacture dialogue for her. [7]

The letter is a powerful ending because its author and Mary appear in its Victoria list, while it reports no Hawaiians at Fort Vancouver. That is one correspondent's 1863 statement, not proof of the disappearance of Hawaiian people or culture throughout the region. [5]

## 3. Then / now

The Village housed employees, families and returning brigades outside the company's stockade. NPS describes a peak population above 600 and explains that Hawaiian workers helped give it the historical name “Kanaka Village.” That label does not mean everyone there was Hawaiian. [8]

Today two reconstructed houses and outdoor interpretation stand along the Land Bridge Trail. Their interiors are generally closed except for special events. They are interpretive reconstructions, not surviving family homes. [8] The reconstructed fort represents the 1845 setting; it must not be treated as untouched nineteenth-century fabric. [6]

The useful comparison is between what the stockade makes visually prominent and the household work beyond it. This dossier does not identify an exact lost-house footprint, an archaeological digging location, or a modern photograph aligned to a historical view.

2. **Read the stockade as a workplace.** Use available interpretation to ask who supplied labor and whose family life lies outside the principal buildings. Do not promise a live demonstration.

4. **Finish with the letter, not another building.** At a safe public stopping place, read the short passage in §2. Ask what it meant for a displaced household to keep correspondence moving across the Pacific. The strongest moment is realizing that a directory of addresses is also a record of care. This is reflection, not a reenactment of eviction.
