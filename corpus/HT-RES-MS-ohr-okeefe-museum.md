# George Ohr’s crowded pottery and Biloxi’s museum of his work

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP22 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
In a photograph from 1901, George Ohr sits in a workroom where some vessels line the shelves, some stand on the floor and others hang above his head. A sign he allowed into the photograph announces, “CANNOT BE DUPLICATED.” That short line is evidence from the original image. The rest of the room shows the stock a working potter made and stored. Some of it looks like goods one could use every day. The photograph gives room for the sales pitch and the trade at once. [2]

Ohr learned from Joseph Fortune Meyer and developed a livelihood in earthenware, drawing on local clay. The museum identifies Henry Portman as an apprentice who helped with the clay work. Its account distinguishes the early need to sell useful ware from later experiments with very thin walls, moving or pinching a rim after throwing and using striking glazes. The Met’s vessel record for an Ohr vase places the surviving object in Biloxi Art Pottery and dates it 1894–1906. A pottery’s advertisement can overstate uniqueness. An attributed surviving vessel gives the reader a separate opportunity to see what Ohr did with the clay. [3, 4]

A fire broke the workshop’s continuity. The museum dates the destruction of Ohr’s studio to 1894 and records that he kept fire-damaged pots. The Biloxi Historical Society’s account of the October 12, 1894 fire names Ohr among the commercial losses, while a modern ceramics essay puts the pottery fire in 1893. The original newspaper image has not been checked. In the museum’s chronology Ohr eventually stopped glazing much of his new work and kept a large body of unsold pottery together. [3, 5, 9]

At the present campus there is another maker’s story. The original nomination identifies Pleasant Reed as the builder of his family’s wood house on Elmer Street. In James D. Dodds’s account, lumber receipts suggest construction began around 1887. His essay also records Reed family memories of Creole speech and the boys’ sleeping loft. Reed’s surviving original building was moved in 2002 and lost in Katrina. The present reconstruction lets the museum teach a history of building and family life distinct from Ohr’s commercial pottery. [6, 7, 10]

## 3. Then and now
The 1901 image is the original Biloxi Art Pottery workroom. The present Ohr-O’Keefe campus houses exhibitions in new buildings. Its operator traces museum development through the damage from Hurricane Katrina in 2005, the 2010 first-phase opening and the opening of a first stainless-steel pod in 2014. Confirm where Ohr’s pottery is showing on a given day. The Pleasant Reed Interpretive Center at the museum is reconstructed. The reproduction architect says its exhibition interior was adapted. ## 5. Public progression
Begin at admissions and ask which public gallery is displaying Ohr. Start with a single vessel. Follow its rim, wall and base, then compare the scale and decoration of the pots in the historical workroom photograph. If open, make a separate short visit to the Reed exhibit as a second example of how a Biloxi maker worked with local materials. End on **The Workroom’s Promise**, reading the words painted over Ohr’s shelves and putting the individual vessel back into a room full of goods. [1–4, 6–8, 10]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The museum’s account dates the major pottery fire to 1894. A later essay gives 1893; the society’s 1894 fire account supports the later year but is secondary. The December 1901 *Crockery and Glass Journal* autobiographical pages remain unverified as an original scan. Ohr’s working voice in this draft comes from his shop’s photographed sign. [2, 3, 5, 9]
- The museum dates Ohr’s closure to 1910, while the Met uses 1883–1906 for its manufacturer chronology. These scopes may differ. A last production date and the date of closing or packing the business are separate research questions. [3, 4]
- The loc.gov image presentation returned HTTP 403 through the search reader. The LOC item JSON provided the original item and the image pixels were inspected directly. The neighborhood workroom’s current parcel and a modern matched image remain unverified. [2, 11]
- The original Reed nomination registers the historic house, whose fabric was lost. Its reference has not been assigned to the current Ohr museum or Reed reconstruction. [6, 7, 10]
