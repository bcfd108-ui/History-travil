# Farish Street Historic District — the record and the contract behind Trumpet

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP11 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Use the public sidewalk at the Trumpet Records interpretive marker, 309 North Farish Street, Jackson. The marker program identifies this as the former business site. The street is the public anchor. A shuttered storefront or altered frontage supplies no invitation to enter. The marker was documented at dedication; its present condition has no dated field check in this dossier. [1–3]

## 2. Story

People on Farish Street were already buying the music before there was a record label. In 1949 furniture dealers Lillian and Willard McMurry acquired a hardware store’s stock, including 78 rpm records. Local demand for blues and gospel helped turn the address into the Record Mart. The business became home to the Diamond Record Company and its Trumpet label. Its first recording session took the St. Andrews Gospelaires to Jackson radio station WRBC on April 3, 1950. The name on the record and the room where the musicians actually played could be miles apart. [1,4]

One of Trumpet’s records carried Elmore James’s slide guitar on “Dust My Broom.” Sonny Boy Williamson II played harmonica, with Leonard Ware on bass and Frock O’Dell on drums. The reverse side carried Bobo Thomas’s “Catfish Blues.” That pairing put two musicians’ work into the hands of someone buying one disc. Farish Street supplied the link between that disc, a paying audience and a business able to send it beyond Jackson. [1,4]

The paperwork shows the limits of that bargain. The University of Mississippi holds the Diamond agreement with James and McMurry’s later correspondence. James had obligations to report his whereabouts and make himself available. In May 1952 McMurry telegraphed him about recording for others. In August she asserted a six-month extension for time she considered him unavailable. The letters preserve the label owner’s position. A matching reply by James has not been established here. [5–6]

## 3. Then and now

The Record Mart made sense on a street of Black-owned businesses, customers and places to hear live music. At the Alamo Theatre farther along Farish, singer Dorothy Moore took part in talent contests. At the store, music could become a commodity that left the neighborhood in a box. When the label closed in the 1950s, the records continued to circulate and the administration of rights continued. [1–2]

## 4. Surviving features and access

The verified physical interpretation is the marker in front of the former Trumpet site. At its dedication on November 17, 2007, Vitrice McMurry spoke about her mother. Roscoe Robinson recalled recording with the Southern Sons, and Jerry McCain attended. Their presence ties the street memorial to people who actually made the music. The surviving label archive is at the University of Mississippi. Its collection guide is a source for this story. [2,5]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

**Read the street number twice.** It identifies where the McMurrys sold records and eventually operated a studio. The Blues Trail documents the company’s use of numerous other studios before in-house recording. Hearing a record made for Trumpet does not place its microphone at 309. The agreement and the address establish a business relationship much more securely than an imagined room. [1,4–6]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

Accounts place the national chart appearance in March or April 1952. Both support a national hit that spring. The legal study uses August 4 and August 5 for James’s agreement in different passages, so this dossier retains the month. It identifies the surviving contract and telegram by folder, with their contents available here through a published archival analysis. No claim of interior survival, original furnishings, or an exact recording-room pin. [1,4–6]

## 7. Historical photograph

No historical storefront image has been matched to number 309 with both visual and reuse checks. A modern marker photograph would illustrate the later dedication. The record label and the recorded performance each carry separate rights questions. Image and audio slots empty.

## 8. Optional visitor observations

From the public sidewalk, an optional dated report could establish whether the Trumpet marker is still in place and legible. A photograph is optional where permitted. Permission and credit preference would be obtained before reuse.
