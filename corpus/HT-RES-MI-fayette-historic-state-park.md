# HT-RES-MI-fayette-historic-state-park — Two limestone furnaces on a deep harbor, and the town that burned its forest
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

A company town was built in 1867 on a 40-foot-deep harbor in Lake Michigan's Big Bay de Noc to turn iron ore into charcoal pig iron, and for twenty-four years its two limestone blast furnaces ran on the hardwood forest around it — some 16,000 acres of company woodland, cut and charred to feed the stacks. The town housed about 500 people, raised an opera house and a hotel alongside its kilns and machine shop, produced 229,288 tons of iron, and then closed when the forest was gone and coke-fired smelting underpriced charcoal iron. What remains is the furnace complex, the townsite's buildings, and the harbor, kept as a Michigan state park since 1959.

## 1. Trip gate
Fayette Historic State Park is a Michigan state park, operated by the Michigan Department of Natural Resources (DNR). The DNR's General Management Plan for the park describes a park of approximately 724 acres on the Garden Peninsula in Delta County, on the east shore of Big Bay de Noc, preserving over 20 historic buildings of the iron-smelting townsite, with a modern campground, harbor, boat launch, swimming beach, and five miles of trails. Vehicle entry is under the statewide Michigan state parks rule: a Recreation Passport is required for a motor vehicle to enter. No dollar figure for the Passport is printed in this entry, and no park hours are printed, because the DNR's own park page could not be read in this pass and no primary source checked states them (see §6).

The townsite is a seasonal visit. The DNR's centennial account of the park states: "The park offers guided tours during the summertime and self-guided tours the balance of the season." The approach is part of the record: the same DNR account directs the traveler off U.S. 2 at Garden Corners in Delta County, south along M-183 down the Garden Peninsula — the closer you get to Fayette, in the DNR writer's phrase, the further back in time you go. The historic core is the townsite on Snail Shell Harbor: the furnace complex first, then the town's own buildings, then the harbor that explains both. Nothing in this entry directs a visitor into a closed building, onto the furnace masonry, or anywhere the park itself closes; the townsite is walked as the park lays it out.

## 2. The story
The town began in 1867, when the Jackson Iron Company built a charcoal-fired iron-smelting works on Snail Shell Harbor. The National Register nomination for Fayette records that construction began that year, that the first furnace — built under the management of Joseph H. Harris — went into operation in December 1867, and that the town was named after Fayette Brown, general agent for the Jackson Iron Company. The DNR's account adds that Brown was the company agent who chose the site. The choice was arithmetic: the Garden Peninsula's limestone underlay the ground, so the bluffs could supply flux for the furnaces and cheap building stone besides; the company owned some 16,000 acres of hardwood land around the town, to be made into the charcoal the furnaces were fired with; and Snail Shell Harbor, 40 feet deep, suited the shipment of ore in and pig iron out.

The works grew to the nomination's inventory. The nucleus was two limestone furnaces — the first built in 1867, the second, with the casting house, completed in 1870. The stacks stood 42 feet high inside; the casting house measured 45 by 60 feet, the boiler room 40 by 50, the engine room 20 by 26, all of locally quarried limestone under iron roofs, and behind the complex ran a solid masonry wall 300 feet long and 14 feet high. Around the furnaces stood eleven charcoal kilns, a machine shop, a blacksmith shop, a dock, and a sawmill; around the works stood a town — a store, an ice house, a grain elevator and granaries, a jail, an opera house, and a hotel, with a two-story stone warehouse begun in 1870. The private houses were commonly small, plain, frame buildings of a story and a half, and the nomination counts living accommodations for a population of 500 in 1870. A railroad ran south from the town about six miles into the company's wooded tract, hauling the raw material of charcoal to the kilns.

The supply line ran across the water. The ore came from the Jackson Iron Company's mine near Negaunee, shipped by rail to Escanaba and then taken across Big Bay de Noc to Fayette in scows of 200-ton capacity; the finished pigs of iron left by water for ports farther down the Great Lakes. The product justified the logistics: the nomination calls it a very high grade of charcoal pig iron, and the DNR's management plan records that Fayette's furnaces produced 229,288 tons of charcoal iron across the operating years, that most of it was converted to steel and used in railroad rails, and that Fayette was the second-largest charcoal iron smelting operation in Michigan during the 1800s. An 1870 visitor saw the transformation while it was still new: James Hibbert Langille described, in the DNR's quotation of him, "great changes" around Snail Shell Harbor — a long dock built, schooners landing building material, houses and barns rising, "an immense smelting stack" going up, coal pits beginning to smoke on the hillside, and "the whole region echo[ing] with the sound of axes and hammer."

The boom ran through the 1870s and into the early 1880s. In May 1883 a disastrous fire heavily damaged the furnaces; they were rebuilt, but, in the nomination's words, production was not the same as formerly. The end came from the two directions the nomination names: the depletion of the hardwood forests made it necessary to ship wood in, raising the cost of production, and the coke-fired method of smelting could make better iron at a cheaper cost. The DNR carries the closing in 1891 — its management plan runs the production span 1867 to 1891, and its park account states flatly that when the charcoal iron market declined, "the Jackson Iron Co. closed its Fayette smelting operation in 1891." (The 1970 Register nomination gives 1892; the conflict prints in §6 and is not reconciled there or here.)

What followed was a long quiet. In 1905 the Jackson Iron Company sold Fayette to the Cleveland-Cliffs Iron Company. By the 1920s, the nomination records, the town "had the air of a ghost town, though it was a popular resort." In 1959 the State of Michigan acquired the townsite and surrounding acreage — the management plan specifies a land exchange, the Mead Paper Company trading the property for other state land — and Fayette became a state park. The Register nomination, written in 1970 with restoration then underway, found the smelting complex's ruins stabilized and a charcoal kiln rebuilt, the opera house and hotel standing, and eleven private residences surviving, including the superintendent's and doctor's houses, with foundations, basement pits, dock pilings, and the railroad grade still evident. In 1974 the DNR and the Michigan History Center joined in an archaeological and cultural-resources study of the park and entered a memorandum of understanding to stabilize, renovate, and interpret the townsite — the arrangement under which the townsite is kept today. Fayette was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on February 16, 1970.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a working company town of about 500 people in 1870 (Register nomination) — two blast furnaces, eleven charcoal kilns, a dock and sawmill, an opera house and a hotel, all of it existing to make pig iron. **Now:** a state park townsite of over 20 preserved historic buildings (DNR management plan), walked as a museum, with the furnace complex standing over Snail Shell Harbor.

**Then:** 229,288 tons of charcoal iron produced across the operating years, most of it converted to steel for railroad rails (DNR management plan). **Now:** the production is the interpretation — the stacks, casting house, and kilns stand as the record of how the iron was made and what it cost the forest.

**Then:** a railroad running six miles south into 16,000 acres of company hardwood, feeding the kilns (Register nomination). **Now:** the woodland is the park's quiet — trails through hardwood forest where the charcoal supply grew, and the railroad grade among the surviving traces the 1970 nomination already counted.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The furnace complex — the two limestone stacks, casting house, boiler and engine rooms, and the 300-foot masonry wall — stabilized as ruins by the time of the 1970 nomination and preserved in the townsite today (Register nomination; DNR management plan's count of over 20 preserved historic buildings)
- A rebuilt charcoal kiln, standing by 1970 (Register nomination), among the kilns' sites
- The opera house and the hotel, extant in 1970 (Register nomination) and among the townsite's preserved buildings today
- Eleven private residences counted in 1970, including the superintendent's house and the doctor's house (Register nomination)
- Foundations, basement pits, dock pilings in Snail Shell Harbor, and the railroad grade south of town (Register nomination)
- The A. Gene Gazlay Visitor Center, carrying the townsite's interpretation (DNR management plan)
- Snail Shell Harbor itself — the 40-foot-deep inlet of Big Bay de Noc, with high limestone cliffs rising from the water's edge across the harbor from the townsite (Register nomination)
- The modern park around the townsite: campground, harbor and boat launch, swimming beach, and five miles of trails (DNR management plan)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the townsite is walked as the park lays it out, in the park's season:

1. **At the visitor center:** Start with the A. Gene Gazlay Visitor Center's overview of the townsite (DNR management plan) — the plan of the works before the works themselves: furnaces at the harbor, town behind them, forest behind the town.
2. **At the furnace complex:** Read the plant at its harbor edge — two stacks 42 feet high inside, the casting house where the pigs were cast, the masonry wall behind. This is the nucleus everything else served (Register nomination).
3. **At the kilns and the railroad grade:** Do the forest arithmetic. Eleven kilns and a six-mile railroad into 16,000 acres of hardwood made the charcoal; when the near forest was gone, the economics went with it (Register nomination).
4. **On the townsite street:** The company town whole — store, hotel, opera house, the superintendent's and doctor's houses among the workers' frame houses. A smelting works with an opera house is the point: this was a town, not a camp (Register nomination).
5. **At the harbor shore:** The dock pilings and the deep water. Ore came across Big Bay de Noc by scow from Escanaba; iron left down the lakes. The cliffs across the harbor supplied the flux and the building stone (Register nomination).

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The closing year conflicts between the two best sources. The DNR's current record — its management plan's production span of 1867 to 1891, its park account's statement that the Jackson Iron Company "closed its Fayette smelting operation in 1891," and the Wisconsin Historical Society's 1925 photograph record describing the company as having "closed in 1891" — carries 1891. The 1970 National Register nomination states that "the furnace's ceased operation in 1892." Both forms print here; §2 uses the DNR's 1891 with its sources named, and the nomination's 1892 is neither corrected nor reconciled.

The park's acreage conflicts inside the DNR's own publications: the General Management Plan calls Fayette "a relatively small park of approximately 724 acres," while the DNR's centennial account calls it "the 711-acre park." Neither figure is reconciled here; §2 uses the management plan's approximately 724. Larger figures in circulation (850, 859 acres) rest on secondary listings and are not printed.

The ownership steps between 1905 and 1959 print only as the primary sources carry them. The nomination establishes the 1905 sale to Cleveland-Cliffs and the town's resort character by the 1920s; the management plan establishes the 1959 state acquisition by exchange with the Mead Paper Company. The fuller chain repeated in secondary accounts — a 1916 purchase and summer-resort era to 1946, a second private owner, a tax default, an Escanaba Paper Company holding — was not verified in a primary source in this pass and is not carried in §2.

Visitor specifics are thinner in this entry than the place deserves, for a stated reason: the DNR's own park web page could not be read in this pass (the fetch was refused), so no park hours and no Recreation Passport prices are printed. The season's shape — guided tours in summertime, self-guided tours the balance of the season — is the DNR centennial account's own statement. A street address for the park (4785 II Road, Garden) circulates in local visitor guides that reproduce the park's published information; no primary source read in this pass prints it, so it is not carried in §10.
