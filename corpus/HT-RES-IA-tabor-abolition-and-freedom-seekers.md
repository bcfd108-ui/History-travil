# Tabor — The room a town made for freedom

Iowa OP05 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

**A house built in a hurry.** Samuel Adams, a cabinetmaker and carpenter, supervised village work on John and Martha Todd’s house. The historical society’s account says Adams made walnut doors and sashes, and that the Todds moved into the unfinished house in August 1853. Martha wrote to her father, “We have been in our new home nearly four weeks, but no part of it is finished yet.” The operator transcribes the letter. The manuscript was not read. The modest house was both a family's home and part of a town whose residents would be called upon to help people escaping slavery. [2, 3, 5]

**Follow the people who needed shelter.** The National Park Service study follows Jim Daniels and his family and a woman named Jane from Missouri. Daniels sought help after learning that his household faced sale. The travelers reached southwestern Iowa after a journey broken by waiting and danger. A son born to the Daniels family in Kansas is named in the federal study. The society’s account gives 11 people arriving with Brown in February 1859. Iowa Heritage Illustrated gives 11 and separately notes the birth of a child in the traveling party. The local date is February 4. These numbers have different scopes and the count standing at the Tabor meeting is unverified. [6–8, 10]

**A divided welcome.** J. E. Todd’s 1898 account recalls a dispute over allowing a Missouri visitor to hear Brown. Brown withdrew and others continued debating. J. E. Todd says he did not recall formal action after the dispute. The 2009 account prints a resolution as having been adopted after Brown left. Preserve the difference. The difference matters because the published records give a less uniform picture of the town than a simple story of unanimous welcome. The named freedom seekers remain at the center of this stop. [7, 8]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic place.** The Todd House stands at 705 Park Street, which the city identifies as its original site. The house and public square belong to the Tabor Antislavery Historic District. The square is a public place from which to reflect on the documented town. The exact room or overnight placement of every member of the party is unresolved. [1–4]

**Lawful public anchor.** Tabor City Park / Public Square at Park Street, Tabor. Drive up and walk from legal parking onto an open public sidewalk or park path. Use the public square edge for orientation and the public edge of Park Street for an exterior view of the house if clearly visible. House grounds, porch and doors remain outside the public-square route. Park restrictions and weather control. [1–4]

## 5. A short visit

From the public square read Martha’s sentence about an unfinished house, then the Daniels family and Jane account. Finish quietly with **The arrival came before the argument**, considering the needs of people arriving after weeks on the move. The published records are the entire fallback if winter conditions keep the square inaccessible. [2, 6–8]
