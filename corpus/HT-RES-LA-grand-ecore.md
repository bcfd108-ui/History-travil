# Grand Ecore — Red River bluff, landing and Civil War ground
HT-RES-LA-grand-ecore · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The bluff is the fact everything else hangs on. At Grand Ecore the Red River bends below ground some eighty feet above the water, in a parish where most river land is low. The Corps' visitor center stands on that bluff today, and its exhibits take up the river itself — the Corps' role in developing the waterway, and the geology, paleontology and Native American cultures of the region. The Indigenous history of this river ground is far older than any document in this pass; this dossier records the center's own framing of it and holds any more specific account of Caddo and Natchitoches peoples for Tribal and archaeological evidence rather than retelling it from tourism copy. [1, 2]

The Corps gives the colonial layer in one compressed line: Grand Ecore, it says, once belonged to a Frenchman who served as commandant of the garrison at Natchitoches. No name, grant or date is attached to that statement on the page inspected, and no colonial land record for the bluff was retrieved in this pass. What can be documented is the landing's work in the nineteenth century, and the first full episode is a failure. In February 1834 the followers of Count Leon — German religious separatists breaking from the Harmony Society — came up the Red River and landed at Grand Ecore, the state encyclopedia records. They named their first Louisiana settlement Gethsemane. Yellow fever broke out; Leon himself died on August 29, 1834. Flooding destroyed much of what they had built, including their cemetery, and in 1835 the survivors left the river bottom for high ground in Claiborne Parish, where their colony became Germantown. Grand Ecore's low ground gave the colony its landing and then took the settlement back. [1, 7]

The river itself was barely a road. The Great Raft — a vast accumulation of drift timber — choked the Red River, and an 1879 national reference work, describing the river as the United States first found it, places the raft as extending from near Grand Ecore to a little below Shreveport. Captain Henry Shreve's snag-boat work opened a navigable channel through it in 1835–1839. A local account drawn from the Cammie G. Henry Research Center's collections describes what the clearing did to the geography of trade: as the cleared channel deepened, the river cut a new main channel across a bend some four miles upstream at Grand Ecore, steamboat access to Natchitoches itself became sporadic and then possible only at high water, and Grand Ecore grew into a significant settlement with at least two hotels. The bluff landing, not the old town landing, was where the river now ran. [10, 11]

War made the bluff a gun position. In his official report, printed in the *Official Records*, Confederate General Richard Taylor describes the first works: when General Banks occupied Alexandria in the spring of 1863, Taylor caused guns to be placed in position at Grand Ecore to keep Union gunboats from ascending the river, and a company under Captain Hutton was left in charge of them. What followed was an engineers' quarrel conducted by letter — Taylor received orders to push the works, sent Major Levy to inspect, and reported back that Grand Ecore was not, in his judgment, a suitable point for defensive works, proposing a position below the mouth of Cane River near Plaisance instead; the department's chief engineer inspected and reported against continuing the work as well. The paper trail is the surviving monument of the 1863 battery: a position chosen, garrisoned, argued over and doubted by the very men ordered to build it. Who performed the labor on those works is not stated in the records inspected here, and this dossier makes no claim for it. [8]

In the spring of 1864 the bluff became the hinge of the largest campaign fought in Louisiana. Under the Union plan, Major General Nathaniel P. Banks marched overland to join Rear Admiral David Dixon Porter's Mississippi Squadron and troops under A. J. Smith on the Red River, while Frederick Steele pushed south from Little Rock — the objectives, as the Arkansas state encyclopedia summarizes them, being Texas, the Confederate forces west of the Mississippi, and the cotton country. The state tourism authority's Civil War survey states Grand Ecore's part plainly: it served as an important staging point and supply depot for the Union Army and Navy during the campaign, and Confederate forces used the position both before and after the spring 1864 invasion. Porter forced his vessels over the falls at Alexandria, and Banks moved forward to Grand Ecore. [4, 5]

At Grand Ecore the campaign came apart on a choice of roads. The river and the road parted there, and Banks — under pressure of time, with Smith's borrowed troops due back east by April 10 — took a route that swung away from the river and away from Porter's gunboats. The state encyclopedia of Louisiana notes that the reasons have never been satisfactorily explained. Taylor struck the strung-out column at Mansfield on April 8; the Union army recoiled to Pleasant Hill and beat off Taylor's assault there on April 9 — and Banks, his confidence gone, ordered retreat anyway, first to Grand Ecore. Porter, fighting low water and obstructions, likewise fell back on Grand Ecore. [5, 6]

What the retreating army did on the bluff is recorded by the men who dug it. A soldier of the 47th Pennsylvania, Henry D. Wharton, wrote home that the regiment's sojourn at Grand Ecore lasted eleven days, in which the position was fortified with entrenchments built of heavy logs five feet high and six feet wide, filled with dirt, with trees felled for two hundred yards in front to clear a field of fire. A modern Army historian's summary, quoted by the regiment's history project, gives the engineers' version: on April 11 Banks' engineer officers laid out a semicircular line of entrenchments around the hamlet — three miles in that account, against Wharton's five — using in part works Taylor's men had prepared earlier, with felled-tree obstacles and artillery positions sited on the high ground. The works were never seriously challenged; Taylor did not have the men to assault entrenched troops. On April 22 the army abandoned the position it had spent eleven days building and marched for Alexandria, fighting at Cane River Crossing the next day. The campaign ended in failure and in the burning of Alexandria on May 13, 1864, as the Union army pulled out — an ending the state's encyclopedia treats at length and this dossier does not soften: the bluff's campaign is a story of a supply base, a wrong road, and a retreat, not of a victory by either army on this ground. [6, 9]

The modern river is a managed one. The J. Bennett Johnston Waterway runs 236 miles from Shreveport to the Mississippi River, providing navigation, flood-damage reduction and ecosystem restoration; the Corps and the Red River Waterway Commission have partnered on the project since the mid-1960s. The Grand Ecore Visitor Center opened in May 2003 as part of the waterway's recreation development, and on November 9, 2015 the Cane River National Heritage Area signed a cooperative agreement with the Corps to assist in the center's daily operations, staffing, interpretation and outreach. The Corps' Louisiana field office describes the center as standing atop hills within a historic Civil War battlefield, overlooking the confluence of Bayou Pierre with the Red River, with a gazebo for the panoramic view. Inside are nine exhibits on the history, present and future of the waterway, and a walking trail gives visitors a first-hand look at Civil War entrenchments — the dug ground of April 1864, read from the bluff where it happened. [1, 2, 3, 12]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The bluff, landing and hamlet of Grand Ecore on the Red River, about four miles north of Natchitoches, Natchitoches Parish — a river landing made significant when the post-Raft channel shifted to this bend, a Confederate gun position from 1863, and the Union staging point, supply depot and entrenched fallback of April 1864. The exact footprints of the 1834 Gethsemane settlement, the steamboat landing and warehouses, and the full extent of the 1863–1864 earthworks have not been established in this pass. [1, 4, 7, 8, 9, 11]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Grand Ecore Visitor Center (J. Bennett Johnston Waterway), 106 Tauzin Island Road, Natchitoches, LA 71457, operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers with Cane River National Heritage Area assistance under the 2015 cooperative agreement. Use the visitor center, its signed grounds, the overlook/gazebo and the marked trail to the entrenchments during confirmed opening hours. The riverbank below the bluff, any boat-launch or port area, and unmarked ground off the trail are separate ground and are not part of this progression. [1, 2, 3, 12]

## 5. Public progression

Start inside if the center is open: take the nine exhibits in order, so the managed river of locks and pools is in mind before the unmanaged one is. Then walk out to the overlook and read the bend — the reason a landing, a battery and an army all wanted this one piece of high ground. Take the trail to the entrenchments last and read them at Wharton's scale: logs five feet high, dirt-filled, a cleared field two hundred yards deep, eleven days of digging for a position abandoned without a battle. **Strongest story moment · The empty works.** In April 1864 two armies in turn fortified this bluff, and the heaviest labor of the campaign here was digging that no assault ever tested; the captain left in charge of the first guns, the engineers who argued against the position, and the regiments who felled the trees all left paper and earth, but no battle — the ground's significance is the campaign's turning, not a fight. Keep the visit quiet on the trail; this is battlefield ground. [1–12]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Hours conflict, printed in full.** The Cane River National Heritage Area partnership page (agreement of November 9, 2015) lists Thursday–Sunday 10:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m., closed Monday–Wednesday, free admission, phone 318-354-8770. The Natchitoches tourism listing inspected this run lists Tuesday–Saturday 10:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m., changing to Wednesday–Sunday 10:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m. starting in April. The Corps' own Grand Ecore page printed no hours at all in this pass. The trip gate therefore instructs confirmation by phone and follows no single listing. A seasonal pattern may reconcile the two listings, but that reconciliation is not documented and is not assumed. [1, 2, 3]
- **Entrenchment length conflict.** Wharton's contemporary letter gives entrenchments "for a length of five miles"; the modern Army historian's summary quoted by the regimental history project gives a "three-mile, semicircular line." Both are printed; the difference may reflect the full fortified perimeter against the engineers' main line, but no source inspected resolves it. [9]
- **No National Register listing was located** for Grand Ecore, the visitor center or the battlefield in this pass, and no register reference number is claimed. The visitor center building dates to 2003. Any parish or state marker program for the bluff was not verified. [1, 2]
- **Colonial land history is a single unattributed line.** The Corps states the ground "once belonging to a Frenchman who served as commandant of the garrison at Natchitoches" without a name or date. Colonial grant and plantation histories of the bluff circulating in secondary summaries were not verified against land records in this pass and are not stated. [1]
- **The steamboat-era settlement rests on one local account.** The channel-shift narrative and the "at least two hotels" detail come from a local history piece drawing on the Cammie G. Henry Research Center's collections; no steamboat register, newspaper or directory corroboration was retrieved in this pass. The Great Raft's extent "from near Grand Ecore" rests on an 1879 reference work describing conditions decades earlier; it is used as a period reference, labeled as such. [10, 11]
- **Post-April 1864 reoccupation claims are unverified.** Secondary fort-listing summaries describe Confederate reoccupation of the battery after April 1864 and a Union occupation in June 1865 with surrendered guns on site. No primary or agency source for those claims was retrieved in this pass, and they are not stated as fact in the story above. [8, 9]
- **Labor on the 1863 Confederate works is undocumented here.** Taylor's report names the officer left in charge and the engineers who disputed the position; it does not state who built the works. In a slaveholding war economy that silence matters, and it is printed rather than filled. [8]
- **Overlook access outside center hours, trail condition and length, and current staffing** were not verified beyond the listings cited. The 2015 partnership page's holiday closures are dated 2014–2015 and are not current guidance. [2, 3]
