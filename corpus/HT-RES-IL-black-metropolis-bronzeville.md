# Black Metropolis–Bronzeville — the Great Migration's city within a city, and the Bronzeville Walk of Fame
HT-RES-IL-black-metropolis-bronzeville · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

In the early twentieth century, tens of thousands of Black Southerners came north to Chicago in the Great Migration, and restrictive covenants and discrimination confined most of them to a narrow South Side corridor — the "Black Belt." What they built inside that confinement was, in the National Park Service's Teaching with Historic Places account, a place literally built from the ground up with its own economic, social, and political establishment, directly supported by Black enterprise and capital: by 1920 the Black Metropolis held a population of 109,548 and stood as a virtually self-contained metropolitan development. The corner of State and 35th Streets became its Wall Street: Jesse Binga opened the Black-owned Binga Bank at 3633 S. State St. in 1908; Anthony Overton commissioned the Overton Hygienic Building in 1922 and the Chicago Bee Building in 1929; Robert S. Abbott's Chicago Defender, housed from 1920 at 3435 S. Indiana Ave., exhorted Southern readers northward and published Langston Hughes and Gwendolyn Brooks. Oscar De Priest's election in 1915 as the city's first Black alderman carried the district's organized political life into city government. Drake and Cayton's 1945 study gave the phenomenon its enduring scholarly name, Black Metropolis; the neighborhood's own name, Bronzeville, celebrated the community its residents had made. [2, 8]

The district's military thread runs through the same streets. The Eighth Regiment of the Illinois National Guard — a Black regiment that served in France in World War I as the 370th U.S. Infantry — received the first armory built for a Black regiment, at 3533 S. Giles Ave., and its Victory Monument, sculpted by Leonard Crunelle and dedicated in 1927 (a Black doughboy figure was added atop it in 1936), stands at 35th Street and King Drive. The nine surviving structures of the Black Metropolis Thematic Nomination — eight buildings and that monument, centered near State and 35th — are the tangible remains the NPS lesson is built on: the Overton Hygienic Building, Chicago Bee Building, Wabash Avenue YMCA, Chicago Defender Building, Unity Hall, Eighth Regiment Armory, Sunset Cafe, Supreme Life Building, and Victory Monument. Six of the nine were individually listed on the National Register of Historic Places on April 30, 1986, under the thematic nomination; the same nine were designated a Chicago Landmark District on September 9, 1998. The district is not a National Historic Landmark district and not a National Register-listed district — a correction to looser descriptions, printed in Gaps. [1, 2, 3]

The commemoration layer came two generations later, on King Drive itself. In 1996 the City of Chicago erected Alison Saar's Monument to the Great Migration — a fifteen-foot bronze traveler, his suit woven from worn shoe soles, standing on a mound of soles, suitcase in hand, facing north — at the boulevard's north end near 25th Street. Between that monument and the Victory Monument at 35th, the Bronzeville Walk of Fame set bronze plaques for the neighborhood's own into the sidewalks, medians, and crosswalks of ten blocks: ninety-one plaques in the count the state tourism office publishes, honoring residents including Ida B. Wells, Gwendolyn Brooks, and astronaut Robert H. Lawrence Jr., with Gregg LeFevre's large bronze street map of Bronzeville fixed in the meridian sidewalk just north of 35th. In January 2023 Congress designated the Bronzeville-Black Metropolis National Heritage Area — the first National Heritage Area dedicated to the story of the Great Migration — running from 18th Street to 71st Street, Lake Michigan to Canal Street, with the Black Metropolis National Heritage Area Commission as its local coordinating entity. The heritage area is a landscape designation, not a park unit: the ground remains a living neighborhood. [1, 4, 5, 6, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The Black Metropolis core sits in the Douglas and Grand Boulevard community areas, centered near State and 35th Streets; the landmark district's nine structures stand between the Dan Ryan Expressway and King Drive, 31st Street and Pershing Road (39th). The Walk of Fame runs on King Drive between 25th and 35th Streets. The 2023 heritage area is far larger: 18th to 71st Streets, Lake Michigan to Canal Street. The three geographies are related and are not interchangeable. [1, 2, 3]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The public sidewalks, medians, and crosswalks of Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. Drive between 25th and 35th Streets, Chicago — the Walk of Fame itself, bookended by the Monument to the Great Migration (north, near 25th St.) and the Victory Monument and bronze map (south, at 35th St.). Read landmark buildings from public ground only unless a building's own operator opens it: the Chicago Bee Building now houses a public library branch, the Eighth Regiment Armory is a working school, and the Wabash Avenue YMCA, Unity Hall, and Supreme Life Building are separate institutions with their own terms, none verified as visitor interiors this run. [1, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Walk it north to south, the direction of arrival reversed: stand first with Saar's traveler at 25th Street, who faces north because the people he stands for were arriving, then walk the ten blocks reading plaques as a neighborhood reading its own roll — a journalist, a poet, an astronaut — and end at 35th Street, where the Victory Monument and LeFevre's bronze map let you look back at the whole ground at once: the regiment, the map, and, a block west, the State Street corridor where Binga, Overton, and Abbott built the Black Metropolis the plaques' honorees inherited. **Strongest story moment · A city within a city, built under confinement.** The covenants drew the boundary; the people inside it built a bank, two newspapers, an insurance company, a regiment's armory, and a musical culture that remade the country — and then, seventy years on, set their own names in bronze in the boulevard. Keep the posture reflective: this is a living neighborhood and other people's home ground, read from its sidewalks. [1–7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Status correction: the sweep list described this ground as an "NHL district." It is not. The Black Metropolis–Bronzeville District is a Chicago Landmark District (designated September 9, 1998); six of its nine structures are individually listed on the National Register (April 30, 1986, Black Metropolis Thematic Nomination), and the district itself is neither a National Historic Landmark district nor a National Register-listed district. The 2023 National Heritage Area is a separate, larger designation. [1, 2, 3]
- Plaque-count conflict printed in full: Enjoy Illinois' heritage account counts ninety-one Walk of Fame plaques; the same office's National Heritage Areas page describes "more than 100" honorees, and some accounts extend the Walk's plaques toward 47th Street or count over 120 mapped sites on the LeFevre map. The ninety-one figure describes the original 25th–35th installation as published; no reconciled current count was verified this run. [4, 5]
- Victory Monument date variance: the dedication is given as 1927 in the landmark literature (Crunelle; doughboy added 1936) and as 1928 in some visitor listings. This dossier follows 1927 and prints the variance. [2, 3]
- Monument name variance: the Saar sculpture appears as both "Monument to the Great Migration" (NPS, Enjoy Illinois) and "Monument to the Great Northern Migration" (the City of Chicago marker inscription as transcribed by HMdb). Both names are printed; the marker's own wording is "Great Northern Migration." [1, 4, 7]
- No staffed visitor gate verified: older listings for a Bronzeville Visitor Information Center in the Supreme Life Building (3501 S. King Dr.) circulate in third-party guides with hours, but no current operator page confirming those hours was retrieved this run. The trip gate therefore rests on the public streetscape alone, and no interior stop is promised.
- Interior access: no visitor terms for the Wabash Avenue YMCA, Unity Hall, Supreme Life Building, Sunset Cafe building, or Eighth Regiment Armory (a working school) were verified this run; the Chicago Bee Branch Library in the Chicago Bee Building operates on Chicago Public Library hours, not verified here.
- Heritage-area implementation is young: Congress designated the area in the National Heritage Area Act signed January 5, 2023 (Public Law 117-339), with up to $10 million authorized over fifteen years in the legislative accounts; the NPS page lists the Commission as coordinating entity but publishes no visitor-center address or hours. Management-plan status was not verified this run. [1, 6]
