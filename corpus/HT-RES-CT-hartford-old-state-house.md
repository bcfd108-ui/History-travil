# HT-RES-CT-hartford-old-state-house — Hartford: where Connecticut helped shape the republic
Division CT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

Completed in 1796 and designed by Charles Bulfinch, Connecticut's Old State House is one of the young republic's own buildings — and it stayed at the center of the argument. The Hartford Convention met here in 1814, and early proceedings in the Amistad case were heard here in 1839. It still stands on Main Street, ringed by the modern city that grew up around it.

Connecticut's Old State House, 800 Main Street, Hartford, Hartford County, Connecticut

**Public access:** Exterior interpretation from public sidewalks at 800 Main Street; no admission is required for this entry. The building operates as a museum with its own hours if you choose to go inside — confirm with the Connecticut Old State House before visiting.

## The story

The Old State House connects three strands of American history: the development of representative government, constitutional disputes between the states and the federal government, and the struggle against slavery.

Its Federal-style architecture — brick, a columned portico, and the cupola above — is the architectural language the young republic adopted for itself, from one of its most influential early architects, Charles Bulfinch. Its chambers witnessed the political disagreements that tested the country's constitutional structure.

In December 1814, delegates from New England states gathered here for the Hartford Convention, to air grievances against the federal government during the War of 1812 — a meeting controversial in its day and argued about ever since.

In 1839 the building witnessed early legal proceedings involving the African captives of the schooner Amistad, whose struggle for freedom eventually reached the United States Supreme Court. The Supreme Court decision itself happened in Washington, D.C., not in this building; the Hartford proceedings were an early chapter of the case, heard where the captives were held.

## Then / now

- 1796: Old State House completed to Charles Bulfinch's design.
- 1814: Hartford Convention meets here during the War of 1812.
- 1839: early Amistad proceedings heard here.
- Today: a museum and landmark on Main Street amid modern Hartford.

## What's still there

The building itself: the columned entrance and cupola against the towers of the modern city — the contrast is the view. The chambers inside survive as interpreted historic space.

## The stop, step by step

Stand on Main Street facing the Old State House at number 800. Find the historic columned entrance, and take in the contrast between the eighteenth-century building and the surrounding modern city. Exterior interpretation is complete from the sidewalk; entry is optional under the museum's own hours.

## Honest gaps & disagreements

The Hartford Convention's intent and legacy remain genuinely disputed among historians; this entry states what met here and why, not a verdict. The Amistad proceedings in Hartford were preliminary to the federal case decided in Washington; accounts differ on the weight of individual hearings, and the National Archives case materials are the primary reference.
