## 2. The story

**Named primary archival record: Inez Rivera Romero**, cataloged as *Romero, Mrs. Inez*, recorded in Fort Collins **April 14, 1975**, interviewers **Charlene Tresner and Lloyd Levy**. The municipal/library collection describes a 43-page transcript and original cassette, with a digitized audio duration of 1:25:56. The item is publicly cataloged, but the full transcript and audio were **not successfully read/heard in this pass**. Therefore no supposedly firsthand words, wage, work schedule or personal anecdote are invented. [5]

Colorado State University's local-history teaching resource explicitly identifies this interview as a perspective from someone who worked in Fort Collins beet fields. That is a secondary description of the primary source, not a substitute for hearing her testimony. [6]

The city's community history describes the Romeros' bilingual assistance and advocacy within a community affected by employment and housing discrimination. Those are institutional historical claims, not quotations from the interview. [7]

**Original interpretive voice:** A city's growth can be counted in buildings and output, yet those totals cannot tell us what growth asked of a household. This entry turns toward the people whose work connected a crop to a neighborhood and a neighborhood to the city. The archive is where that inquiry becomes specific: a named person, a dated conversation and the responsibility to hear her before supplying a story on her behalf.

The original voice above is editorial interpretation. It must not be presented as Romero's opinion. This remains a research-ready dossier with a clear primary-content gate, not finished personal-story narration.

## 3. Then / now

Then: the city's historical account connects beet labor, family work and the growth of distinct neighborhoods, alongside discrimination and community organization. Now: a civic museum and local-history archive provide authorized places to examine the evidence. [7]

The museum is the visitor anchor, not an asserted location of Romero's work or interview. Neighborhood history does not establish public admission to houses, churches or workplaces. A former home mentioned in a historical source is not added as a second stop without its own operator verification.

A museum companion should distinguish the economic history from the testimony of individuals. It should also distinguish migrant workers, settled families and different community backgrounds rather than treating all agricultural laborers as one interchangeable population.

## 4. What's still there

The verified resource is the named oral-history catalog and the museum's public research room. Staff-assisted retrieval and permission to reproduce are separate from the existence of the record. [4–5]

An official municipal interpretive panel points readers to the museum's **Beet Shack** display. This is evidence of an institutional interpretation, but the panel was not a current floor inspection and does not guarantee that the display or any particular object is available today. [8] No Romero-specific exhibition or listening station has been confirmed.

The museum's collection policy warns that some material is stored off site and can require two to three business days for retrieval. A same-day walk-in should not depend on an original item appearing immediately. [9]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

One public museum/archive anchor. **45–60 minutes is an editorial estimate**, not a booked research session or tour.

1. **Choose the mode:** free archive research during its shorter window, or paid museum gallery visit. A gallery ticket does not extend archive hours. [2–3]
2. **Enter through public museum reception:** use the Welcome Desk to identify the public archive entrance. No collection storage access is implied. [4]
3. **Set the question:** how did the beet economy shape household life and belonging? Consult available public interpretation, without promising a particular exhibit. [7–8]
4. **Follow the named record:** use the catalog citation for a future properly arranged reading/listening session. Until the transcript is verified, treat this as a research question, not an already scripted first-person stop. [5]
5. **Strongest landing:** distinguish “we know this person left an account” from “we have heard what she said.” The visitor takes away a more honest understanding of how community history is made.

If the museum is closed, use online institutional context and the catalog from home. No alternative visit to a field, plant, family house or neighborhood worksite is offered. No industrial procedures or agricultural technique are included.
