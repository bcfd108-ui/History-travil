# Gowen Field / Boise Army Air Base and the people who trained and stood watch there

**Research draft · Labeled THEME SWEEP (Family 5 — Military installations, 20th-century & Cold War) · Boise, Ada County, Idaho · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate

The lawful public anchor for Gowen Field is the **Idaho Military History Museum**, 4692 W. Harvard Street, Boise, Idaho 83705, located outside the secured perimeter of the active Gowen Field Air National Guard / Idaho Army National Guard installation and commercial Boise Airport. The museum's posted hours are **Tuesday–Friday, 10:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m.** and **Saturday, 12:00 p.m.–4:00 p.m.** (Mountain Time); closed Sunday, Monday, and most federal holidays. Admission is free (donations accepted), with indoor galleries and an outdoor armored-vehicle and aircraft compound. [1]

The active Air National Guard Base, Army National Guard training complex, flight line, and surviving World War II cantonment structures inside the secured perimeter (including Recreation Building 303 on Fourth Avenue) are **excluded from general public walk-in visitation**. A historic airfield or district pin does not grant base entry. [1, 2]

## 2. The story

Gowen Field began as a Depression-era municipal gamble that turned into one of the Pacific Northwest's busiest heavy-bomber training schools and, later, a Cold War air-defense and Air National Guard interceptor post. During the late 1930s, Boise city leaders spent $64,000 alongside $316,000 in Works Progress Administration (WPA) funds to grade a 960-acre sagebrush tract near the Oregon Trail 3.5 miles south of downtown into Boise Field. After the Army Air Corps passed over Boise in 1939 in favor of Ogden and Denver, the field threatened to become a municipal financial burden until Idaho Adjutant General M. G. McConnel pressed the Ninth Corps Area headquarters in September 1940 to reconsider Boise for a military air base. [2]

Approval followed by January 1941. Under the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Portland District, Boise contractors J. O. Jordan and Son and the Morrison-Knudsen Company—using lumber supplied by Boise's Hill Lumber—mobilized roughly 900 to 1,000 local workers to raise a standardized Quartermaster General 700-Series wooden cantonment in eight months. Temporary operations buildings, including Recreation Building 303 (Quartermaster Plan 700-310 / Type TH-2, designed by Col. Charles D. Hartman's staff), went up by April 1941; the initial cantonment phase finished in October 1941 alongside a $30,909 WPA landscaping grant and a 2,560-acre bombing range secured on Federal Grazing Service land 20 miles south of the field. In the summer of 1941, the War Department named the post **Gowen Field** for First Lieutenant **Paul R. Gowen** of Caldwell, Idaho, who died in a bomber crash in Panama in 1938. [2]

Wartime mobilization rapidly remade the post's daily life. From an initial garrison of roughly 1,100 men, the base swelled after December 7, 1941, to a peak wartime population of **10,000** personnel as a Second Air Force combat-crew training school for Boeing B-17 Flying Fortress and Consolidated B-24 Liberator crews (including units such as the 411th Bombardment Squadron, photographed on the Gowen flight line in 1943). In 1942, **126 members of the Women's Auxiliary Army Corps (WAAC)** arrived at Gowen Field, and in March 1942 **African American soldiers** were stationed on the post. As the Historic American Engineering Record (HAER) documentation for Building 303 notes, military segregation was enforced across installations during the war, making it likely that central recreation facilities were segregated and leaving an open research question regarding separate service-club provisions for Black troops at Gowen. [2, 3]

By the end of World War II, Gowen Field shrank to roughly 190 soldiers in deactivation status. Offered to the State of Idaho through the War Assets Administration and declined on cost grounds, the airfield returned to City of Boise ownership while the Corps of Engineers negotiated federal retention of select operational structures. The Cold War immediately pulled Gowen back into active air defense. Organized as an **Air National Guard (ANG) Training Site** serving eight western states and home to Idaho's **190th Fighter / Fighter-Interceptor Squadron**, Gowen Field transitioned from WWII/Korean-era F-51 Mustangs to **F-86A/E Sabres** and **F-102 Delta Daggers** under **Air Defense Command (ADC)** alert tasking during the 1950s and early 1960s. Although a 1961 Air Force close-out review shifted training-site focus and the Idaho Army National Guard expanded on-post training in 1965, the Air Force maintained its active lease, and Gowen remains a joint Idaho Air and Army National Guard, Navy, and Marine Corps tenant installation beside Boise Airport. [2, 4, 5]

### Haunted history / lore

No documented, primary-sourced haunted tradition is established in the military, HAER, or museum records reviewed for Gowen Field. Any informal ghost stories circulating online regarding wartime hangars or barracks are unverified and omitted as unsubstantiated; documented wartime training crashes and service deaths are recorded strictly as historical casualties, not hauntings. [2]

## 4. What's still there

- **Publicly accessible — Idaho Military History Museum (`4692 W. Harvard St.`):** Indoor galleries covering Gowen Field's WWII bomber-crew era, WAAC and Idaho National Guard service, Mountain Home AFB, and USS *Boise* (CL-47), plus an outdoor compound displaying Cold War aircraft including an Idaho ANG **North American F-86A Sabre** (marked for 190th FIS service), a **McDonnell Douglas RF-4C Phantom II**, a **MiG-21**, a WWII **Fairchild PT-23** trainer, and armored vehicles. [1, 4]
- **Restricted / excluded — Secured Gowen Field ANGB/ARNG perimeter:** Recreation Building 303 (`303 Fourth Ave.`, `HAER ID-36-C`), Magazine Area Heating Plant (`HAER ID-36-A`), Ammunition Assembly Building (`HAER ID-36-B`), wartime barracks rows, and active military ramps lie inside controlled military/airport security gates and are **off-limits without military authorization**. [1, 2, 6, 7]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Arrive at the public Idaho Military History Museum gate (`4692 W. Harvard St.`, via S. Orchard St. off I-84/Gowen Rd., following brown museum signs).** Confirm museum hours before arrival (Tue–Fri 10–4, Sat 12–4 MT). [1, 5]
2. **Walk the outdoor static aircraft and armor compound.** Start at the F-86A Sabre (representing Idaho's 190th Fighter-Interceptor Squadron Cold War transition) and the WWII PT-23 trainer to connect the WWII training base to its 1950s Air Defense Command interceptor role. [4]
3. **Enter the indoor Gowen Field & Idaho Guard galleries.** Examine wartime photographs and unit artifacts documenting the 1941 rapid cantonment build, B-17/B-24 crew training, and Cold War Guard deployments; allow at least one hour. [1]
4. **End the visit at the museum grounds.** Do not attempt to drive onto active Gowen Field military gates, Fourth Avenue cantonment streets, or airport operations roads. [1, 2]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Peak construction workforce:** The March 8, 1941 *Idaho Statesman* (cited in `HAER ID-36-C` footnote 1) reports `1,000` local workers employed on Gowen Field construction, while the HAER narrative text (p. 9) gives a peak payroll employment rate of `900` men. Both figures are retained. [2]
- **Museum F-86 Sabre tail identity:** Museum newsletter coverage notes the restored F-86A carries pilot Bill Coburn's name, while aviation-spotter records (`49-1050` / originally F-86E `51-2826`) and Wikipedia gallery notes characterize it variously as a restored F-86A or replica/re-marked airframe; exact airframe serial/bureau verification rests with museum staff records. [4]
- **Segregated WWII recreation facilities:** `HAER ID-36-C` explicitly flags as an unresolved gap whether separate recreation facilities were built for Black soldiers stationed at Gowen Field beginning March 1942. [2]
- **Magazine Area demolition/status:** `HAER ID-36-A` (Heating Plant) and `HAER ID-36-B` (Ammunition Assembly Building) document 1941 structures east of Broadway south of County Road, but current physical survival of those specific magazine buildings after 2004 mitigation is unverified in this dossier. [6, 7]
