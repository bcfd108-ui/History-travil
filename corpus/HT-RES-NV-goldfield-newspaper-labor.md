## 2. The story
**Primary documentary evidence:** the 1907–1908 city directory, published by National Directory Co., names people associated with the Goldfield News. Its catalog OCR lists **Grey Honeywell, printer**; **E. G. Isard, pressman**; **Margaret Sipple, stenographer**; and **Charles S. Sprague, editor**. These are contemporary occupational entries, not interviews or independently corroborated employment files. Sipple’s entry was additionally visually verified on printed page 181 (PDF page 95); the other names remain OCR-level and require image confirmation before use as public answer keys. [1]

**Original interpretation:** a newspaper usually enters history under an editor's name. A directory makes a different story possible: printing and office work also needed people, and the surviving job labels are small openings into that larger workplace. Sipple's entry prevents the workforce from becoming an all-male assumption, but does not establish her duties beyond the listed occupation, her pay, politics or household experience.

UNLV's collection biography identifies Sprague as editor and manager of the Goldfield Publishing Company, then a mining promoter and businessman. This supplies an institutional account of overlapping commercial and editorial interests, not proof that every employee endorsed his opinions. [2]

**One-minute hook, original:** Who made the town's news besides the person whose name became famous? Read a printer, a pressman and a stenographer back into the story before letting the editor speak for all of them.

**Firsthand verbal voice: GAPPED.** No invented quotation by Honeywell, Isard or Sipple. The primary basis here is the contemporary directory, not a memoir. No technical printing instructions are part of this entry.

## 3. Then / now
Then, a directory compressed work into a name and occupation. Now, that compression is both useful and limiting: it establishes a documentary association while leaving much of daily working life outside the record.

The UNLV-provided title history hosted by the Library of Congress frames Goldfield's press within mining promotion and labor conflict. Its discussion includes the rival Tonopah Daily Bonanza's attack on Sprague during his 1910 congressional campaign. This is a **later library essay describing partisan primary reporting**, not a directly inspected 1910 newspaper issue. Its characterization is not assigned to the printer or stenographer. [4]

**Original distinction:** there are at least three kinds of labor here: making the paper, the miners whose disputes became news, and the commercial work of selling the district to outsiders. The point is to keep those interests visible, not to declare every newspaper either a neutral witness or mere propaganda.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Enter through the digital catalog:** establish the directory's date, publisher and identifier before using its occupational entries. [1]
2. **Start below the ownership level:** read the printer and pressman entries, then the stenographer's. Keep their limits clear: a job label is not a personal statement.

4. **Introduce conflict carefully:** use the library title essay as a lead to newspaper rivalry, not as an unexamined final verdict about the employees. Exact issue retrieval remains future work. [4]
5. **Strongest story landing:** return to Margaret Sipple's short entry. Ask, “If this were all that survived of your working life, what would a historian know, and what would they have to leave blank?”

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Directory OCR has obvious errors elsewhere. Named entries above were read in the institution's transcription; Sipple alone was visually confirmed on printed page 181 (PDF page 95). No payroll or union record corroboration obtained. [1]
- **Founding year conflict:** the LOC-hosted essay says an initial issue on April 29, **1905**; UNLV's holdings begin April 29, **1904**. Do not quietly repeat the later year as settled. [4–5]
- **Purchase versus lease:** the Historical Society says Sprague purchased the paper in January 1906; UNLV's collection biography describes a lease from J. P. Loftus. Preserve this ownership distinction pending transaction evidence. [2–3]
