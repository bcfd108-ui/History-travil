# Camden’s Ouachita harbor and McCollum–Chidester House Museum
HT-RES-AR-camden-ouachita-river-workers · Arkansas OP04 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

“The actual record of business transacted is not available, being on board the boats.” That was the Camden mayor’s explanation in a 1900 statement accompanying his July 27 letter. Its signed version in the federal printing identifies him as A. L. Morgan. He was replying to an Army engineer who wanted the case for work on the Ouachita harbor. Morgan had to turn movement, waiting and freight work into a table for people upstream in the federal decision. The numbers came from local agents and Morgan described them as conservative estimates. [2, printed pp. 4–5]

Read the table horizontally. The City of Camden shows eleven arrivals and eleven departures, with draft rising from two feet six inches light to five feet six loaded. The Stella shows three arrivals and the Ouachita one. The city reported an open interval from January 25 to May 9 with intermittent failures to get through even then. Shippers needed the river deep enough to move a loaded boat, the town needed a wharf that could take the traffic and agents needed figures that could be borne out. Outbound cotton dominated Morgan’s cotton column, at an estimated 8,300 bales shipped and 280 received. The report records the work on the page without identifying each worker who handled it. [2]

The engineers and local officials disagreed about what to fix. Local leaders suspected high water changed the force of the current against the bank. Assistant engineer H. M. Marshall emphasized drainage from city streets at the head of the incline and judged an existing low bulkhead ineffective where the caving occurred. Major Thomas L. Casey considered the improvement beneficial locally but opposed work at federal expense. Morgan himself said he did not want the harbor proposal to harm the separate lock-and-dam project. In this river town, having a problem and agreeing on the scale, budget and remedy for it were several different things. [2, printed pp. 2–5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The historic harbor stood on the Ouachita riverfront in Camden. The federal print describes the wharf, incline and bank in relation to the city. A public route to these specific features, or evidence that they survive in usable form, is still open. The historic house museum on Washington Street gives visitors a staffed place to read the civic controversy. The National Register identifies the house as an 1847 Greek Revival building. Its boundaries and architectural designation are distinct from the working waterfront. [1–4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** McCollum–Chidester House Museum, 926 Washington Street, Camden. Drive up and approach the museum by its signed public entrance during ordinary tours. When the house is closed, use only an available ordinary public sidewalk without entering the property, or read remotely. Keep the riverbank, industrial waterfront, former landings and battlefield ground outside the trip. [1, 2]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Casey’s July 19 request prints A. S. Morgan. The mayor’s July 27 signed letter and its table print A. L. Morgan, as does Marshall’s narrative. The dossier follows the signed reply for attribution and leaves the name authority unresolved. [2]
- Morgan reports four months of navigation that year versus six or more in a usual year. The dates and figures are a single reported period, with missed passages inside it, and cannot be taken as an annual average. Morgan’s letter identifies the actual boat records as unavailable. [2]
- Local officials and Marshall identify different primary causes of riverbank failure. The report gives a disagreement over remedy and federal responsibility. The claim does not establish an identifiable, surviving modern landing. Exact historic wharf location and worker or on-board records remain open. [2]
