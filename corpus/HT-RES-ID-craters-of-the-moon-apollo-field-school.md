# Craters of the Moon · The Apollo geology field trip

History Travel · Idaho · Draft for Dash QC · Desk review 18 November 2026

## 2. The story

On August 22, 1969, four pilots came to Idaho to learn to read rock. Alan Shepard and Edgar Mitchell would walk on the Moon as members of Apollo 14. Eugene Cernan and Joe Engle were training as backups. A photograph the park identifies as Broken Top shows two astronauts beside geologist Ted Foss. Nobody is wearing a spacesuit. The equipment that mattered here included a geologist who could ask what happened first and a landscape where a pilot could work out an answer. [4, 5, 6]

The science-training schedule is unusually specific about the assignment. On August 22 the party flew over the country, then examined exposed flows in the monument. They compared the ages of a cinder cone and the lava around it, looked at where flow fronts ended, and inspected collapse pits and fresh glassy surfaces. Butte Crater was seen **from the air**. On August 23 the party went on to the Wapi area, stopping at King’s Bowl and at places where silt had drained into fissures. A traveler standing at Broken Top sees one part of a trip that crossed several volcanic landscapes. [5, 6]

The point was to recognize a worthwhile sample when time, carrying capacity and the view from a helmet were limited. The lunar answer survives on the radio. In the Apollo 14 mission transcript, Mission Control asked about a pale boulder, Shepard distinguished it from rock immediately around them, then said, “But I definitely feel it’s worthwhile sampling.” The phrase is Shepard talking on the Moon in 1971. It is not dialogue from the Idaho photograph. What the two records give a visitor is the exercise and a later occasion when the judgment mattered. [4, 5, 7]

## 3. Then / now

Then, a field trip linked ground observation with aerial reconnaissance. Now the National Park Service provides a public visitor center and a 1.8-mile Broken Top Loop when accessible. The modern walk interprets the landforms. No evidence in the training schedule establishes that its present tread follows the pilots’ footsteps. The NPS entrance coordinate identifies a modern gateway. [2, 3, 5, 8]

## 4. What’s still there

- **Public interior** — Robert Limbert Visitor Center and its museum exhibits, during posted hours. [1, 2, 9]
- **View from public ground, seasonal** — volcanic terrain on the signed Broken Top Loop from the Tree Molds area. Use only if staff confirm the road and trail are open and conditions fit the party. [1, 8]
- **Not accessible as a verified point** — the precise 1969 field station where the group was photographed. The park identifies the formation, with no visitor waypoint for the frame. [4]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Start at the visitor center. Read the field schedule’s August 22 entry alongside the landscape map. Separate the ground stops from the flyover. [2, 5, 9]
2. When the Loop Road and trail are open, examine one boundary between different rock surfaces from the signed trail. Describe what overlies what without collecting a specimen. If access is held, use the park’s photograph of the field party and NASA’s photographs instead. [1, 4, 6, 8, 10]
3. **Strongest story moment** — read Shepard’s short exchange with Mission Control, then look again at the Idaho training frame. The later decision came down to a pilot explaining why one boulder, among the many he could see, deserved a place on the trip home. This ending works inside the visitor center. [4, 7]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

NPS dates arrival at Arco to August 22. NASA’s schedule gives August 22–23 for the Idaho exercise. One date is arrival and one a two-day field trip. The phrase “Apollo 14 astronauts” on the park page includes two backups. NASA captions identify who trained in which role. Cernan later landed with Apollo 17. Engle flew the Space Shuttle. [4–6]

The park’s hours page says the road typically operates into late November. Its basic information page says it usually closes by mid-November. The November 10 condition report confirms facilities and contains no day-specific vehicle clearance to Broken Top for November 18. Its notices permanently close Buffalo, Boy Scout and Beauty caves. There is no cave leg in this stop. [1, 2, 11]

## 7. Photo record

**NASA field photograph, Broken Top, 1969**, reproduced by NPS. NPS identifies Cernan, Engle and Foss. The frame shows three people on loose dark material, a near ridge and distant hills. It offers no identified pair of durable skyline points or bearing from which to locate the photographer. No Photo Point. NASA’s two Idaho field-trip images also identify prime and backup participants but do not identify both camera stations. [4, 6]

**NASA/JSC S71-29184**, a lunar breccia photographed after the mission. NPS reproduces it on the same page. It is an image of returned material, not a photograph of the Idaho outcrop or proof that the specimen is displayed at this visitor center. No Photo Point. [4]
