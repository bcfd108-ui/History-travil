# Fort Clatsop: whose winter, whose country?

Checked 19 November 2026. Public NPS visitor-center and replica visit only. Local dossier, not a route group, booking, release approval or invitation into a living community.

## 1. Trip gate

**Public entrance anchor:** Fort Clatsop Visitor Center, Lewis and Clark National Historical Park. NPS's directions explicitly give **92343 Fort Clatsop Rd., Astoria, OR 97103** as the physical navigation address and warn that searching only the park name may lead elsewhere. A commercial listing returned 92345; use the operator's address, not that discrepancy. [1]

**Winter and holiday gate:** The superintendent's compendium, approved **27 May 2026**, establishes **9 a.m.–5 p.m.** for the Fort Clatsop Unit from early September to late June, local Pacific. It closes the unit on **Thanksgiving, December 24 and December 25**. For this year, Thanksgiving is **26 November 2026**, also explicitly listed in the NPS basic-information search result. The dated 2025–26 range on the separate hours page is stale for this November; the 2026 compendium supplies the governing seasonal rule. [2][3][4]

**Temporary conditions gate:** NPS's conditions page warns that winter storms can close the park at sustained winds of 35 mph and trails at 25 mph. These are published closure triggers, not proof of current wind conditions. The page text was last updated January 2024; its extracted Alerts area showed no specific live notice. That absence does **not** establish an all-clear. Recheck current NPS alerts and posted restrictions before travel; do not proceed onto a closed path. [5]

The visitor center is the intended first check-in for fees, maps and current access. NPS lists a $10 standard per-person entrance fee and $35 annual park pass; eligibility and pass coverage should be checked on the fees page rather than assumed for every traveler. No demonstration or ranger program is promised in winter. [3]

**a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.** No shortcut, historic footprint search, village visit or off-path archaeological exploration is included.

## 2. The story

On **25 December 1805**, William Clark recorded moving into huts and drying wet belongings. His Christmas dinner account emphasized spoiled food. **John Ordway**, writing about the same day, described poor elk without salt but also the party's expectation that this would be its final winter away. **Joseph Whitehouse** called the huts **“comfortable, excepting smoaking a little.”** This five-word excerpt preserves his spelling. The accounts are not interchangeable: one event could be written as privation, endurance and improvement at once. [6]

**Patrick Gass's** entry continues into 26–28 December, describing work to remedy smoky huts and preparations for salt-making. The fort was therefore not simply a finished shelter into which the expedition stepped; everyday work continued after moving in. These are edited journal transcriptions read on the University of Nebraska's scholarly edition, not manuscript images inspected firsthand. No construction or weapon instructions are extracted for visitor use. [6]

The captains' discomfort is only one frame. In **15 July 2009 congressional testimony**, **Ray Gardner**, then chairman of the Chinook Nation Tribal Council, described the winter in Clatsop country within an existing trading world. He stated: **“Our people traded with them on fair terms”** and discussed both continued exchange and the expedition's theft of a canoe. He also described the transfer of the fort and furnishings to Clatsop leader Coboway on departure. This is a named Tribal leader's published account, not a voice reconstructed from the captains' descriptions. His title is attributed to the testimony date, not asserted as a current office. [7]

Gardner identified the Chinook Nation's five constituent tribes as Lower Chinook, Cathlamet, Clatsop, Wahkiakum and Willapa. That naming matters: “local Indians” is not an adequate replacement for the political and cultural identities in his account. The testimony supplies a Chinook Nation perspective; it is not presented as an exhaustive statement for every present-day community with Clatsop heritage. [7]

The central interpretive contrast is not hardy explorers versus hostile weather. It is temporary visitors struggling through a season in a place that was already home, trade network and country to others.

## 3. Then / now

NPS explains that the original fort no longer survives. A **1955 replica** burned in **2005**, and a replacement was rebuilt in **2006**. What visitors can enter today is interpretation in timber, not timber handled by the expedition. [8]

Nor should “same location” in a general visitor description be enlarged into a surveyed identification of every original wall. NPS's 2006 archaeological news release describes investigations undertaken to answer questions about the original location, configuration and appearance. That is evidence of inquiry, not a license to invent precise historical footprints. [9]

Then/now here is strongest as a comparison of **accounts and interpretation**. How does a maintained visitor exhibit help people imagine dampness, crowded work and dependence on neighbors, and where does its apparent completeness make uncertainty harder to see?

## 4. What's still there

The public visitor center provides exhibits and films; the fort replica provides a physical interpretive setting. Their existence does not guarantee that every room, path or service is available on a particular stormy day. NPS also distinguishes peak-season programs from ordinary site access. [3][10]

The 2019 NPS cultural-landscape inventory describes the present replica as differing from the earlier reconstruction in durability and in interpretation based on later scholarship. It records changed land uses and a historic landscape broader than the building. This dossier does not turn those landscape resources into a list of places to locate or enter. [11]

The journals and Gardner testimony remain the visitor's evidence even when weather prevents an outdoor visit. Do not treat a closed replica as a reason to seek another entrance or a remote original camp location.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Start indoors:** Check current access with the visitor center and read one expedition account beside the Tribal testimony. Keep the source dates visible.
2. **Walk only the authorized visitor route, if open:** Approach the replica as a reconstruction. Do not claim that a threshold precisely matches an 1805 doorway.
3. **At an accessible public viewing point:** Compare the journal descriptions rather than trying to stage an expedition scene. Shelter could be welcome and still smoky; moving in did not end the work.
4. **Finish with the strongest story moment:** Return mentally to Gardner's account of exchange and the handover to Coboway. The expedition's departure was not the end of the place's human history. Ask: **What changes when the winter is told by the people whose country the visitors were in?**

This is a proposed interpretive sequence, not a booked tour or guaranteed exhibit order. If outdoor access closes, use the indoor/remote reading version without substitute trails. No route to a living community is suggested.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The separate operating-hours page still contains a winter range ending June 2026. The approved 2026 compendium and general winter visitor guidance support the November rule; do not copy the stale range as current-season confirmation. [2][4]
- Permanent seasonal rules, holiday closures and temporary weather closure are distinct gates. None alone settles the other two.
- A reproduced journal page includes multiple Clark versions and accounts by other men. They should not be silently merged into a single narrator or treated as identical evidence. [6]
- The exact original footprint is not established here. No excavation report was used to assign an original wall or gate location.
- An OHS photo page prints a 2006 date while its narrative identifies the depicted structure as the 1955 replica. The image's chronology needs catalog clarification. It also gives a December 5 dedication date, while other NPS material gives differing reconstruction milestones. No exact dedication day is adopted. [12]
- No current forecast, live trail inspection, accessible-path survey or winter program calendar was verified. No reservation or outreach occurred.
