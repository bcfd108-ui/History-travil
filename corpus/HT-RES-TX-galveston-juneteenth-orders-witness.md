# Galveston Juneteenth — what the order said and what freedom meant
Research draft · Texas OP05 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

### The words on two pages

At Galveston on June 19, 1865, the U.S. Army under Maj. Gen. Gordon Granger issued General Order No. 3. The original manuscript image in the National Archives identifies the place and date and declares that “all slaves are free.” It follows that declaration with “an absolute equality of personal rights and rights of property.” Then it says that freed people should remain at their present homes and work for wages and that they would not be allowed to collect at military posts. The four phrases let a reader hear the promise and the restrictions written by the same authority. The manuscript scan and the Archives transcription were compared [3–4].

### How the news sounded inland

The Federal Writers’ Project typescript records Felix Haywood remembering, “We was free. Just like that, we was free.” He was recalling the experience of receiving news in Texas, with soldiers and singing, and describing people setting out to understand what freedom meant. His account is a later interview, mediated by its typescript. It gives a Texas voice beyond the issuing office, without placing Haywood at the Galveston corner on June 19. The transcription records him distinguishing the pride of freedom from the material means to live it [5].

### The city made its own public memory

The Galveston Historical Foundation reproduces a newspaper report of a sizable emancipation procession on January 1, 1866 despite a storm. It also describes generations of local commemoration and the eventual ceremony at Ashton Villa. Read these as later public acts. The modern mural takes its title from the written order and places those words on a present-day city wall [1–2, 6].

## 3 · Then / now

**Historic place.** Union headquarters in the former Osterman Building near Strand and 22nd. The building is demolished. The old headquarters and the later commemoration at Ashton Villa carry different histories [2, 6].

**Lawful physical anchor.** Ordinary public sidewalk at the Absolute Equality mural and marker at Strand and 22nd Street. Use legal parking and the marked sidewalks/crossings for a short walk. Stay out of traffic, occupied passages and private interiors [1–2].

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Orient at the mural from the public part of the corner and leave room for others [1–2].
2. Read the original order or its Archives transcript and note the change in wording between its first and final sentences [3–4].
3. **Strongest story moment — The order and the voice.** Put the federal text beside Haywood’s short recollection, then allow a quiet pause. The stop ends here [3–5].

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Visit Galveston uses 2217 Strand Street in its listing and 2211 Strand for the façade in its walking article. The street intersection is the stable public anchor [1–2]. The surviving order establishes issuance in Galveston. It gives no site-by-site log of readings. The Galveston Historical Foundation documents Ashton Villa as later commemoration, and its quoted 1866 press account was not checked against the original page [3, 6].
