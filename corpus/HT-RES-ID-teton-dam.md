## 2. The story

On **June 5, 1976**, Teton Dam failed and downstream communities faced devastating flooding. Reclamation's current history attributes **11 deaths** to the disaster. The immediate event and the recovery must both be remembered; rebuilding did not erase bereavement or disruption. [3]

**First-person published survivor account:** **Lester J. Petersen**, interviewed by **Alyn B. Andrus**, is identified on the transcript cover with **August 24, 1977**. His account begins with learning the dam had broken and says, **“We really didn't know what would happen.”** He describes responsibility for fifty people at the Golden Living Center, changing evacuation decisions, temporary shelter and the subsequent work of reconnecting residents with families. This is his account, not a modern emergency plan. [4]

An important provenance complication remains visible: the transcript includes passages framed as three weeks after the flood and a later section explicitly dated September 17, 1976. The file therefore contains internally earlier material despite its 1977 interview cover. Do not assign every statement to a single interview-day perspective. The transcript was read; audio was not played and the relationship between recording, earlier notes and edited transcript was not independently resolved. [4]

A second primary witness, **J. Kent Marlor**, interviewed by **David L. Crowder on July 7, 1977**, recalled learning of the collapse while at home and trying to verify the warning on a radio. His civil-defense role did not mean that he possessed complete information at the first moment. This account shows fragmented information, not proof that every household received the same warning. [5]

The strongest human story is uncertainty followed by responsibility: people making consequential decisions for others while the scale of the flood was still unclear.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** families and public institutions faced damage, displacement and extended recovery. Petersen's recollections include efforts to restore housing and businesses, with help from others. His optimism about rebuilding is one survivor's outlook, not an obligation imposed on every survivor. [4]

**Now:** the city's *Just Add Water* commemorative exhibit addresses water, flood effects and community response. The museum's current relocation is itself a reason to avoid stale visitor instructions. [1]

The disaster also changed institutional attention to dam safety. Reclamation describes Teton as a catalyst for its safety program. That historical policy consequence is appropriate context; this dossier does not reproduce failure mechanisms, prescribe repair methods, evaluate today's dams or turn photographs into an engineering diagnosis. [3]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Gentle, optional museum progression; not a hunt:**

1. Begin with the date and affected community, not a spectacle of destruction. Read the museum's framing before choosing whether to continue.
2. Encounter one named person's account. Petersen's uncertainty is enough; there is no need to reproduce intimate medical or family details.
3. Compare that account with Marlor's description of learning and verifying the news. Notice where recollections are specific and where they cannot represent everyone.
4. Make room for recovery work and continuing loss. Avoid turning rebuilding into a simplistic happy ending.
5. End with a quiet reflection: who depended on someone else's decision that day, and what forms of care continued long after the water receded?

Allow a flexible visit of perhaps 45–60 minutes as editorial guidance, with permission to stop early. No timed challenge, reward, badge or completion requirement. If the museum is closed, a brief reading of the public transcripts is a complete alternative; no dam-site detour is proposed.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**Death counts differ in authoritative and primary records.** Use Reclamation's current **11** as its explicitly attributed public figure. Reclamation's older *Dams and Public Safety* gives **11–14**; the December 1976 independent panel report states **14** attributed deaths. A 1976 congressional hearing record gives **12** with categories that include deaths during rehabilitation. These are different published accounts, not numbers to average or silently reconcile. [3,6–8]

Petersen's transcript itself mentions ten; that recollection is not adopted as the final count. No individual-by-individual reconciliation was completed, and no unsupported direct-versus-indirect explanation is asserted for every difference.
