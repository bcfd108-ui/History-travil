## 2. The story

**The ballot was not the last task.** A victory announcement can reveal what an organizer thought citizenship required after the votes were counted.

The University of Arizona reproduces **Frances Willard Munds's** words from **“Greetings to Arizona's New Citizens, The Women,” Tucson Citizen, November 16, 1912, page 4**. Her brief published voice urges women to **“make a study of the laws that govern women and children”**. The newspaper facsimile was not inspected; this is a primary statement quoted through a university exhibit. [2]

**Campaign work.** The same exhibit describes the move from legislative disappointment to a popular initiative, approved **November 5, 1912**. Phoenix appears specifically in its account of Mayor Lloyd Christy's October permission for a Votes for Women banner. This is a historical connection, not a surviving-banner claim or street destination. [2]

**Another organizer's voice.** **Pauline M. O'Neill**, in **“Equal Suffrage and What it Means to the Women of Arizona,” Tucson Citizen, January 28, 1912, page 4**, predicted a future **“until women vote in every state”**. The university quotes the article and describes her leadership alongside Munds. Again, the original newspaper page was not read. [3]

**Original interpretation.** Put these two statements together without making them identical: O'Neill anticipates expansion; Munds describes responsibility after victory. The story's strongest moment is not a triumphant finish but the work still ahead.

## 3. Then / now

**A 1912 change, not universal access.** Arizona's successful state initiative preceded the federal Nineteenth Amendment. It did not eliminate every citizenship, racial, language or practical barrier. The university exhibit documents exclusion of non-English-speaking Mexican communities in campaign strategy, followed by later outreach. This contradiction belongs beside the success, not in a footnote that disappears from the visitor version. [2]

**Continuing barriers.** The Arizona Historical Society describes the 1948 Arizona Supreme Court decision opening registration to Native Americans previously excluded under the state's interpretation, and literacy barriers continuing afterward. [4] This is historical context, not present-day election guidance and not a claim that all Native people had one uniform experience.

The Phoenix reading room preserves and makes evidence available. It is not the place where every statewide campaign event occurred. A public archive can teach how to test a historical claim without turning a political workplace into a tourist stop.

**No universal victory claim.** Broad institutional summaries sometimes compress citizenship and suffrage histories. This dossier confines itself to the documented Arizona campaign and continuing barriers; it does not derive legal eligibility for every population from a single date.
