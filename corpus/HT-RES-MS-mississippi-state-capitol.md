# Mississippi State Capitol and the people behind the stone

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March OP10 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

**Staffed weekday interior, exterior if the building is closed.** The legislature lists 8 a.m.–5 p.m. Monday–Friday at 400 High Street, Jackson, and free guided starts at 9:30 and 11 a.m. and 1 and 2:30 p.m. Ground-level public entry is on High Street or under the steps on Mississippi Street. The legislative schedule lists Thanksgiving Day and warns that related closures can span more than one day. Confirm the date during holiday week. Use an open sidewalk in daylight for an exterior stop and follow posted weather, event and access controls. [1–3]

## 2. Story

Choosing an architect was only the first decision. The legislature authorized a building on the former penitentiary site in 1900. Governor Andrew Houston Longino chaired the State House Commission. An architectural competition led to the selection of Theodore C. Link of St. Louis. The construction contract was made with the Wells firm of Chicago, which reorganized during the job. The Commission’s account books kept the older firm name, W.A. and A.E. Wells, even when the company advertised itself as Wells Brothers. Judd E. Wells represented the firm in dealings on site and Theodore H. Schlader supervised construction for it. [4–6]

The ornamental finish was divided among specialist firms. The recorded subcontract list assigns cut stone to George Dugan of Bedford, Indiana, marble and mosaic to N.O. Nelson Manufacturing, steel, iron and bronze to American Bridge, and plastering to August Zander. The stained glass is attributed to Louis J. Millet’s firm. Robert P. Bringhurst designed the sculpture for the pediment. A.R. Grieve made the copper eagle that sits above the dome. These names are roles in records; the individual cutters, setters, plasterers and glaziers are much less visible. An inspected construction photograph gives this gap a human scale: workers stand and bend around earthworks and temporary equipment, while a penitentiary building is still standing behind them. [4,6–7]

The State House Commission’s surviving invitation names a surprisingly late milestone. It invites its reader to the “Laying of the Corner Stone of the New State House” on June 3, 1903. The building was already largely complete. Treat the public ceremony as a dedication and keep it distinct from commencement, formal acceptance and occupation. The legislature says back taxes recovered through a settlement with Illinois Central funded a project whose cost exceeded $1 million. The archive’s commission history shows why the cost drew attention: the act authorizing it set a $1 million limit, while the reported cost reached $1,093,641. Different statements may include different costs. [4–6,8]

## 3. Then and now

The Beaux-Arts elevations and drum remain. The ceremonial front faces Mississippi Street, while the current visitor service entrance can be reached from High Street. Inside, the state’s visitor materials describe the rotunda and the two legislative chambers. Spaces assigned to the courts and library in the original building have changed uses. The present Capitol and the Old Capitol Museum are separate stops. The removed penitentiary is indexed as a separate historical subject and has no distinct visitor route here. [1–2,4–7,9]

## 5. Public progression

Start at the posted public entry and orient at the north-side visitor desk. Where staff permit, look at how hard stone, decorative finishes and colored light meet in the rotunda. End outside at the public Mississippi Street frontage if conditions allow. **Story landing — Work divided into trades.** The finished portico lets the reader connect Dugan’s stonework contract to an object that still stands, while the earlier construction photograph restores the mostly unnamed workers to the story. [1–2,4,7,9]

## 6. Uncertainty and disputes

The 1903 proceedings place the start of actual work on January 1, 1901. The Commission report describes contract operations starting then, while the later official register and archive say physical construction began in March. Retain both accounts. NPGallery metadata renders the consultant’s name “Barnard R. Green”; the nomination uses Bernard R. Green. The 2017 legislative commendation describes the November 2 designation announcement, while MDAH records October 31, 2016 as the designation date. Worker payroll, scope of the cost overrun and direct support for individual tradespeople’s identities remain open. [4–6,10–12]
