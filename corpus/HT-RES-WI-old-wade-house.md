# HT-RES-WI-old-wade-house — The halfway house the railroad passed by
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Halfway between Sheboygan and Fond du Lac, where the stage road crossed the Mullet River, Sylvanus and Betsey Wade built the largest stagecoach inn on the road — a Greek Revival house of some twenty rooms, with a ballroom on the third floor and closet-sized cells beside it where the stage drivers slept. It was filled nearly every night for a decade. Then the railroad chose a route two miles north, Greenbush stalled into a sleepy hamlet, and the inn outlived its road by three generations of Wades — until the Kohler Foundation bought it in 1950, restored it room by room with the family's own furniture, and gave it to the State Historical Society. It opened as a historic site on June 6, 1953, with Carl Sandburg at the dedication.

## 2. The story
In 1844, Sylvanus and Betsey Wade and their nine children settled in what would become Greenbush, the first permanent settlers of European descent there, in a northern Kettle Moraine forest the Society's site history describes as virtually uninterrupted. Betsey Wade would tell her children years later that the forest was so dense that, even on a clear night, she "could hold in her apron all the stars she could see." The Wades had not come to carve out a single homestead: they came to establish a town. They chose the site deliberately — halfway between Sheboygan and Fond du Lac along a well-used trail, where the Mullet River crossed the trail and offered waterpower, and they purchased several sections of land around a potential mill site.

Sylvanus Wade helped make the road his inn would serve. The nomination records that he was one of the commissioners who laid out the route for the territorial road between Sheboygan and Fond du Lac, completed in 1845, and that before the big house he had already established a blacksmith shop and a simple hostelry on the ground. By 1848, when Wisconsin became a state, Greenbush was, in the Society history's words, a booming little town with two stores, a school, a sawmill, a wagon shop, a blacksmith, and a doctor, and Wade's "Half Way House" was a regular stop for the stagecoach lines running between Sheboygan and Fond du Lac.

The inn itself was built between 1847 and 1851, per the nomination (the Society's history gives its opening as 1850), of locally harvested and sawn lumber, three stories in the Greek Revival style of the "civilized" East (Society's history). The nomination's description carries the fabric: a large, substantial wood structure with a story-and-a-half wing and a single-story wing in tandem at the rear, frieze windows under the cornices, the roof shingled with hand-cut cedar shakes, walls clapboarded, principal windows double-hung six-pane sash with green louvered shutters — and some twenty rooms, including a number of closet-size "cells" or tiny bedrooms off the third-floor ballroom where the stagecoach drivers bedded down. Native woods were used throughout — butternut, walnut, white pine, oak, beech, ash, and basswood — with most interior woodwork of butternut, often beautifully hand-tooled, and window sash so thin and hand-worked that parts were fastened with solid wooden pegs. The nomination judges it almost certain that Charles Robinson, Sylvanus Wade's son-in-law, who helped build the house, was responsible for much of its design and fine detail.

At its height the house was a town center as much as a hotel. The Society's history records cotillions, business meetings, political caucuses, and circuit court sessions in its rooms, and a taproom buzzing with debates "as mundane as last year's crops and as heady as secession and the abolition of slavery." The nomination, quoting an earlier account, calls it "unusually large and pretentious for its day," a famous landmark whose rooms "were filled to capacity practically every night," termed by drivers and passengers "the best on the road." It was at meetings in the Wade House, the nomination records, that plans were made for the Sheboygan and Fond du Lac Plank Road; construction of that road began in 1851 (Society's history).

The heyday lasted little more than a decade. In the 1860s the railroad became the main artery between Sheboygan and the interior and bypassed Greenbush entirely, establishing its terminal two miles north in Glenbeulah (Society's history). Greenbush stalled into a sleepy rural hamlet. The building continued to operate as a hotel for another decade, and three generations of the Wade family continued to live in it until 1941 — a span that preserved what a busier fate would have scattered: when the house was later sold, the nomination records, the great bulk of the Wade family's original furnishings had remained stored in the ballroom, and pieces that had been taken away were tracked down, purchased or donated, and returned.

In 1941 Sylvanus's grandson William Wade sold the hotel to family friend Mary Dorst for $5,200 (Society's history). Dorst planned to restore it to its nineteenth-century splendor using the Wade furnishings still inside, but by 1949 she no longer had the funds to continue. The rescue came from Kohler. It was the vision of Marie Christine Kohler, daughter of Kohler Company founder John Michael Kohler, to restore the hotel to its 1850s heyday; her sister-in-law Ruth DeYoung Kohler took up the cause, and the Kohler family and the Kohler Foundation purchased Wade House in 1950 (Society's history). The restoration ran 1950 to 1953 under architect Richard Philipp, F.A.I.A. (nomination), with Ruth DeYoung Kohler personally overseeing the furnishing of the interior with original Wade family items (nomination). The Foundation also acquired and restored the circa-1855 Butternut House — the Robinson House, home of Charles Robinson, the inn's own craftsman (Society's history) — and a blacksmith shop was reconstructed near the original location of the Henry Dockstader smithy. Ruth Kohler did not live to see the opening: she died three months before it. On June 6, 1953, amid great fanfare and an appearance by poet Carl Sandburg, Wade House opened to the public as the Society's second historic site (Society's history).

The site kept growing. In 1963 the Wisconsin Legislature voted a permanent home at Wade House for the carriage collection of Wesley W. Jung, grandson of a Sheboygan carriage maker; the Wesley W. Jung Carriage Museum opened in 1968. In 1999 the Kohler Trust for Preservation pledged $1.8 million to rebuild the Herrling Sawmill on its original site; the mill, which had stood in Greenbush through the second half of the nineteenth century, opened to the public on June 16, 2001. On June 8, 2013 — sixty years to the week from the site's opening — a new 38,000-square-foot Visitor Center and Wesley W. Jung Carriage Museum opened (Society's history).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the 1850s — a stagecoach inn at the halfway point of a day-long road, its rooms filled practically every night, its ballroom hosting cotillions and court sessions, its drivers asleep in cells off the ballroom floor. **Now:** the same house, restored to its Civil War-era appearance (nomination-era phrasing: its 1850s heyday, in the Society's), furnished largely with the Wade family's own pieces, and run as a Wisconsin Historical Society site.

**Then:** a booming plank-road town — stores, school, sawmill, wagon shop, blacksmith, doctor — laid out by a founder who had helped survey the road himself. **Now:** a sleepy rural hamlet, in the Society history's own words, and a historic site whose grounds hold the carriage museum, the rebuilt sawmill, and the Robinson House; the railroad that ended the boom still runs through Glenbeulah, two miles north.

**Then:** the HABS photograph in §7 — the inn in snow before restoration, its two-tier porch and painted WADE HOUSE sign intact, a bench on the lower porch and the nose of a period automobile at the frame's edge: a landmark still standing, waiting out its obsolescence. **Now:** the inn is the centerpiece of the site, its sign line and porches the first thing the visit reads.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The Wade House inn itself — wood Greek Revival, main block with story-and-a-half and single-story rear wings in tandem, frieze windows under returned cornices, hand-cut cedar shake roof, some twenty rooms including the drivers' cells off the third-floor ballroom — restored 1950–1953 under Richard Philipp with original Wade family furnishings, the bulk of which had never left the house (National Register nomination, 1971)
- The Robinson House (the circa-1855 Butternut House), home of Charles Robinson, the son-in-law credited with the inn's design and craftsmanship — acquired and restored by the Kohler Foundation alongside the inn (Society's site history)
- A reconstructed blacksmith shop, framed with early timbers, near the original location of the Henry Dockstader smithy that fronted on the Plank Road (Society's site history)
- The Herrling Sawmill, rebuilt on its original site with a $1.8 million Kohler Trust for Preservation gift pledged in 1999 and opened June 16, 2001 — a water-powered up-and-down sawmill on the Mullet River (Society's site history)
- The Wesley W. Jung Carriage Museum — created by the Legislature in 1963, opened 1968, and reinstalled in the 38,000-square-foot Visitor Center and carriage museum building opened June 8, 2013 (Society's site history)
- The Mullet River crossing ground itself — the waterpower site around which the Wades bought their sections in 1844 (Society's site history)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the conditions are the operator's: the inn and core buildings are seasonal museum space, and the carriage museum keeps its own year-round season:

1. **At the junction of Highway 23 and Kettle Moraine Drive:** Read the siting before the building — halfway between Sheboygan and Fond du Lac, on a road Sylvanus Wade himself helped lay out, at a river crossing with mill power. The inn is where it is because the road and the river meet here.
2. **At the inn's front:** The two-tier porch and the WADE HOUSE sign line, the frieze windows under the cornice. Compare the front against the pre-restoration HABS photograph in §7 — same elevation, in snow, decades before the restoration.
3. **Inside, on the operator's terms:** The ballroom and the drivers' cells beside it — the inn's social order in one floor plan: dances and court sessions in the big room, the men who drove the stages in closet-sized rooms off it. Note the butternut woodwork and the pegged, hand-thin window sash the nomination singles out.
4. **At the Robinson House:** The builder's own house — Charles Robinson, who likely designed the inn's fine detail, lived in the Greek Revival house the Foundation restored beside it.
5. **At the Herrling Sawmill on the Mullet River:** The waterpower the Wades bought land around in 1844, working again in a mill rebuilt on its original site — the town's first industry, outlasting the road that made the inn.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The build dates print in four forms across the primary sources and are not reconciled here. The nomination gives the house as "built in 1847–1851" and its period form carries 1847–1851 as the specific dates. The Society's site history says the Wades "opened the new Wade House hotel to the public" in 1850. The Society's Register record gives "Date of construction: 1850." The HABS record for the §7 photograph carries "1849 Initial Construction." The NPS Register asset record lists significant years 1847 and 1851. Section 2 therefore prints the nomination's range with the Society's opening year attributed, and no single year is asserted.

The story count splits the same way: the nomination describes "a 2½ story wood, Greek Revival structure," while the Society's site history calls it "three-story." Both print. The room count also varies: the nomination counts "some twenty rooms"; a 2013 published question-and-answer with the site's director calls it a 27-room hotel. The nomination's figure is used in this entry's text; the director's figure prints here as the later, larger count, unreconciled. The nomination's most quoted line — that "as many as 200 people sought shelter there at a time" — is carried in the nomination itself as hearsay ("It is said"), inside a quoted earlier account, and this entry does not print it as a count. The end of the inn's hotel operation is likewise unfixed in the sources checked: the Society's history says the building "would continue to operate as a hotel for another decade" after the mid-1860s stall; longer operating spans circulate in secondary accounts and are not printed. The Kohler Foundation's 1950 purchase price circulates in secondary accounts as $17,000; no primary source checked in this pass states it, so no purchase price prints (the 1941 Dorst price of $5,200 prints because the Society's own history states it). Site acreage figures in circulation — 240 acres in some accounts, 48 acres in others — appear in no primary source checked here, and no acreage prints. Coordinates: the nomination carries boundary-rectangle corner coordinates only, and in the file copy consulted the northwest corner's longitude seconds are illegible (N 43° 46′ 36″, W 88° 05′ —); no single verified point for the house is carried in the primary sources checked, and none prints.
