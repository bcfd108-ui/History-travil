# HT-RES-NJ-twin-lights — Two stone towers over the Navesink, and the hill where wireless began
Division NJ seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a hill above Highlands, New Jersey, the federal government built the same light station twice: first in 1828, then again in 1862 as a brownstone, castle-like building whose two unlike towers — one square, one octagonal — still stand 223 feet apart over the approach to New York Harbor. The station was the Lighthouse Service's test bench: the first Fresnel lenses tried in the United States were tried here in 1841, mineral oil was first tested in a first-order lamp here in 1883, and in 1898 the south tower carried the first electric-arc lighthouse lamp in the country. In 1899 Guglielmo Marconi reported the America's Cup races from this hill, and the National Historic Landmark nomination records that he established the nation's first wireless station capable of regular commercial sending and receiving here. The lights are out; the towers, the powerhouse, and the Marconi ground are now Twin Lights State Historic Site.

## 1. Trip gate
The operator is the New Jersey State Park Service, which runs the site as Twin Lights State Historic Site, 2 Light House Rd, Highlands, NJ 07732; phone 732-872-1814. The facts in this gate are the operator's, from the State Park Service page checked November 26, 2026.

Grounds are open 9 a.m.–4:30 p.m. The lighthouse is open Memorial Day–Labor Day daily 9 a.m.–4 p.m. (last climb 3:50 p.m.), and October 1–late May, Wednesday–Sunday, 10 a.m.–4 p.m. Tours are by appointment, 9 a.m.–3 p.m. Entrance fee: none. The tower climb (Memorial Day–October 31) is $5 for visitors over 12 and $2 for visitors under 12; tours are $12 for visitors over 12 and $2 for ages 5–12. The visitor center and grounds are ADA accessible; the towers are not. The site has no trash receptacles — what a visitor carries in, the visitor carries out. Interior photography is not permitted except by prior written permission, for educational purposes only. Those are the operator's terms, and this entry prints no hours, fees, or permissions beyond them.

## 2. The story
The hill was a signal place before it was a light station. The National Historic Landmark nomination for the Navesink Light Station records a colonial signal outpost on this ground in 1746 and a Washington militia post, and a Merchant's Exchange semaphore tower in 1829, the year after the light station itself was established. The station dates to 1828: the first twin light houses were built of blue stone (New Jersey Historic Trust). The present building replaced them. Congress appropriated $72,941 on June 20, 1860; the new station, designed by Joseph Lederle, was first lit in 1862 — a Gothic Revival building of reddish-brown sandstone quarried at Belleville, New Jersey, with brick and a granite foundation, its two towers set 223 feet apart center-to-center and joined by the keepers' quarters and workrooms, completed in 1863 with 18 rooms housing four keepers' families. The south tower is square, 53 feet tall with a focal plane 246 feet above the sea and 66 steps; the north tower is octagonal, with 65 steps (NHL nomination).

The station's working distinction was that new light technology was proved here first. In 1841, two Fresnel lenses were installed at Navesink — the first test of the Fresnel lens in the United States; the State Park Service page carries the pair as a first-order fixed lens in the north tower and a second-order revolving lens in the south (the Coast Guard fact sheet's differing version is printed in §6). The 1841 lenses came out when the new lights were lit in 1862. In 1883, kerosene — mineral oil — was first tested in a first-order lamp at Navesink (NHL nomination). The communications layer kept pace: a magnetic telegraph in 1854, Western Union in 1872, Postal Telegraph in 1898 (NHL nomination).

The electric chapter opened in 1898. A second-order electric-arc bivalve lens was installed in the south tower — the first electric arc lamp in United States lighthouse service, per the nomination, which notes that an earlier installation planned at Fire Island four years before was never carried out; the State Park Service page carries the beacon as visible 22 miles, and the Lighthouse Service Bulletin's claim, quoted in the nomination, that for years Navesink was "the only primary lighthouse lighted by electricity in this country and the only important shore station having a plant for the generation of electricity." The powerhouse that fed it was built April 22–June 30, 1898, of wood, and enclosed in brick in 1909; it measures 42 by 26 feet. The north light went out of service the same year, 1898. The keepers who ran all this were numerous and modestly paid: more than 12 principal keepers and 70 assistants served at the station, at salaries of $400–$600 in 1861, topping out at $1,000 in 1921 (State Park Service).

The wireless chapter is carried here as the record states it. In 1899 the New York Herald hired Marconi to report the America's Cup races off Sandy Hook; the demonstration worked, and per the NHL nomination he established at Navesink the nation's first wireless station capable of regular commercial sending and receiving. The Navy built its own wireless house sometime after 1903 — using a competitor's set, the nomination records, because it could not come to terms with Marconi — and abandoned it by 1909 for Fire Island. Postal Telegraph bought and moved the Marconi structure. The Army tested thermopile equipment from Fort Monmouth at the station July 30–August 9, 1935, and the State Park Service page records Army radar tests from Fort Monmouth at the station in the 1920s–30s; the Department of Environmental Protection's 2006 landmark release adds that radar and electronics from Fort Monmouth were tested at the station in the Second World War.

The end of the working station came in stages the sources do not date identically (see §6). The State Park Service page carries the station as decommissioned after 121 years — 1828 to 1949 — and the Coast Guard fact sheet records automation in 1949. The New Jersey Historic Trust carries the sequence: decommissioned by the Coast Guard in 1949, passed to the Borough of Highlands in 1954, and to the State in 1962, the year the site opened as a historic site (NJDEP, 2006). The north tower received a fifth-order lens dating to 1881 in 1962 and serves today as a private aid to navigation (NHL nomination). The original listing on the National Register came December 2, 1970; the station was designated a National Historic Landmark on February 17, 2006. The State acquired the Marconi-era complex in 1985; its Navy telegraph building and Postal Telegraph tower base stand outside the landmark boundary and are administered as part of Twin Lights Historic Site (NHL nomination).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** two working lights 223 feet apart, the south tower from 1898 carrying an electric-arc bivalve beacon visible, per the operator, 22 miles. **Now:** both towers dark as aids — the north tower's fifth-order lens, installed 1962, is a private aid to navigation — and the bivalve lens is displayed in the 1898 powerhouse, where visitors read it as an exhibit instead of a beam.

**Then:** 18 rooms of keepers' quarters between the towers, four keepers' families in residence, more than 12 principal keepers and 70 assistants across the station's life, at $400–$600 a year in 1861. **Now:** the same rooms are the museum and visitor center of a state historic site, and the keepers' work is the interpretation.

**Then:** a semaphore tower (1829), telegraph wires (1854, 1872, 1898), and in 1899 Marconi's antennas reporting a yacht race — the first regular commercial wireless sending and receiving in the nation, as the landmark nomination records it. **Now:** the Navy telegraph building and the Postal Telegraph tower base survive outside the landmark boundary, administered as part of the site; the hill's communications century is read on the ground, not heard on the air.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The 1862 light station building — both towers (square south, octagonal north), the connecting keepers' quarters and workrooms — standing and operated as Twin Lights State Historic Site (State Park Service; NHL nomination)
- The 1898 powerhouse (wood, 1898; brick enclosure, 1909), displaying the second-order electric bivalve lens (NHL nomination)
- The fifth-order lens of 1881 in the north tower, installed 1962, in service as a private aid to navigation (NHL nomination)
- The Navy telegraph building and the Postal Telegraph tower base, outside the National Historic Landmark boundary, administered as part of the historic site (NHL nomination)
- The grounds, open 9 a.m.–4:30 p.m. on the operator's current schedule, with the view over Sandy Hook Bay and the Navesink that made the hill a signal post in 1746 (State Park Service; NHL nomination)

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The Coast Guard Historian's fact sheet conflicts with the landmark nomination and with itself. Its header swaps the towers' shapes ("OCTAGONAL; North Tower: SQUARE") against its own general information and the nomination, which are consistent: north octagonal, south square. Its tower heights — 46 feet and 73 feet — do not match the nomination's 53 feet for the south tower. On the 1841 Fresnel installation, the fact sheet lists "Original Lens: Second Order 1841; North: First Order 1841" and its chronology places the Fresnel installation in the south tower; the State Park Service page carries a first-order fixed lens in the north and a second-order revolving lens in the south. The lens orders per tower print in §2 in the operator's form, with this conflict standing; the fact that the 1841 Navesink installation was the first Fresnel test in the United States is common to all three sources.

The end of service prints three ways. The State Park Service page: decommissioned after 121 years (1949). The Historic Trust: decommissioned by the Coast Guard 1949, to the Borough of Highlands 1954, to the State 1962. The Coast Guard fact sheet: the light discontinued in 1952, a daybeacon until 1963, and the station decommissioned and turned over to the state in 1953. Section 2 uses the 1949/1954/1962 sequence with its sources named; the fact sheet's 1952/1953/1963 dates stand here unreconciled. Visibility also prints two ways: 22 miles (State Park Service, for the 1898 beacon) against the fact sheet's 19 miles at the same 246-foot focal plane.

The ground itself prints two sizes: the landmark nomination gives the designated boundary as the 1828 federal plot, approximately 2.66 acres; the operator manages approximately 5 acres today. The address prints two ways: the operator's 2 Light House Rd against the nomination's address as filed, 100 Government/Lighthouse Road, Highlands. Section 1 and §10 use the operator's address for the visit and carry the filed address here. The Department of Environmental Protection's 2006 landmark release prints hours from that year; they are superseded by the operator's current page and are not used in §1.
