## 2 · Story — When work and a place to live arrived on different schedules

In October 1937, federal photographer Russell Lee made a picture of two people near Crookston. The two sit on the edge of a bed. A small bird rests in the man’s hand. A window and a shelf fit into the room behind them. The Library of Congress identifies them as newly married beet workers and preserves a note allowing that they may be Mexican or Mexican American. Their names and the location of that room are unverified. Five years before the United States and Mexico formalized a wartime labor agreement, the subjects already had a domestic life here, within a regional industry a photographer had come to record. [4–6]

In 1992, Minnesota Public Radio’s Mark Zdechlik reported from Crookston as migrant families arrived to look for jobs and housing. An independent read of the publisher’s machine transcript identifies Armando Cuellar as a former migrant worker helping match workers and growers. Cuellar’s words in that transcript are “It depends on how the chemical applications work.” He was explaining why the amount of available work was uncertain. The quotation is from the publisher’s transcript, which warns of automatic-transcription errors; the audio has not been independently heard in this pass. [7]

The report documents different accounts of who should provide housing. American Crystal’s office disclaimed a role with migrant workers, according to Zdechlik. The growers’ association director described steps some growers were taking, acknowledged shared responsibility and said some people arrived for whom growers had no work. Sister Justina Violet, who ran a shelter, argued that fields and factory depended on these workers and that workers should be housed. Proposed seasonal housing in the report was a plan contingent on funding. Its completion is unverified here. [7]

Ken Mendez, in a published 2024 interview, remembers his parents’ different routes into the region. His father came from Mexico to East Grand Forks in 1927. His mother arrived from Texas in the 1940s and helped welcome returning families. “When the families came up, they generally always seemed to stop at her house.” Mendez uses a family house to explain the social network behind the seasonal work. The identities of Lee’s subjects are a separate unresolved question. [4,8]

## 3 · Then / now

Then, crews worked beet fields, families had to find places to stay and local processors handled the crop. American Crystal dates dedication of its Crookston factory to 1954, later than Lee’s picture. Central Park is the public green-space anchor and is also named in the 1992 report as a place where families camped. The reported campsites and the 1937 photographed room have no verified visitor locations. The city now operates a separately scheduled campground there. [1–4,7,9]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- MPR’s 1992 story gives a broad regional arrival estimate and explicitly notes uncertainty. It cannot give a Crookston family count. The named parties disagree about responsibility and the availability of work, and the article preserves their distinct views. [7]
- The city prints dates for camping, while its park directory lists day-use amenities without a verified November access statement. Winter approach, signs and footing need a field check. No camp or museum appointment is offered as a seasonal fallback. [1–3]
- The MPR web-reader attempt returned a DisabledError; the original publisher HTML and machine transcript were subsequently obtained directly. Audio and housing-project completion were not verified. MSUM's S4868 locates growers' papers and oral-history descriptions; original interviews and payrolls were not obtained. [7,10–11]
- The mirror displays January 1, 1937 as a date. LOC identifies October 1937. Use the repository’s month, no day, and preserve the discrepancy. No identities, parcel or probable campsite are inferred from the picture. [4,12]
