# HT-RES-NH-mount-washington-cog-railway — The Railway to the Moon

Division NH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On the western slope of Mount Washington, in Coös County, New Hampshire, a toothed wheel climbs a ladder of steel laid up a mountainside: the Mount Washington Cog Railway, the world's first mountain-climbing cog railway, opened to the 6,288-foot summit on July 3, 1869. Sylvester Marsh's rack-and-pinion line rises from Marshfield Base Station at 2,700 feet on an average grade of twenty-five percent, over trestle-built track that tops out at 37.41 percent on Jacob's Ladder. It has run, with interruptions only in the two World Wars, ever since: a steam railway for its first 139 years, now worked mainly by biodiesel locomotives built in its own shops at the base, with two coal-fired steam engines, built in 1875 and 1908, still climbing.

## 1. Trip gate

The Mount Washington Cog Railway is a privately owned, working railroad, operated by the Mount Washington Railway Company from Marshfield Base Station, 3168 Base Station Road, six miles east of Route 302 near Bretton Woods; the state tourism listing gives the phone as 603-278-5404. The base station is open seven days a week all year, closed Thanksgiving Day and Christmas; trains depart frequently, daily, all year.

The summit run is seasonal because the summit is. Trains climb to the summit from late April through late October — the posted 2027 season opens summit service April 30 — and the round trip takes about three hours, including a one-hour layover at the top. At the summit, which is Mount Washington State Park, the visitor plant is the Sherman Adams Visitor Center, the Tip Top House Museum (built in 1853), a weather exhibit, a cafeteria, gift shops, and a summit post office. From late October through early May, when the state park's summit facilities are closed for the winter, trains run instead to the railway's Waumbek Station at 4,000 feet; the posted winter season runs November 2, 2026 to April 29, 2027.

At the base, Marshfield — at 2,700 feet, the railway notes, the highest year-round full-service facility on the mountain — holds the Cog Railway Museum (admission free), a gift shop, and a convenience store and food court. Peppersass, the original 1866 locomotive, stands on display outside. The visitor boundary is a railroad's: you board at Marshfield, ride the one line up and the same line down, and spend the summit hour inside the state park's facilities and weather, which the railway itself warns can be the worst on earth.

## 2. The story

Sylvester Marsh was a Campton, New Hampshire, native who made his money in Chicago meatpacking and grain. The ASME's landmark account gives the origin plainly: on a trip east in 1852, Marsh and a friend climbed Mount Washington and almost failed to reach shelter ahead of one of the mountain's rapidly developing storms, and the experience started him thinking of a railway up the mountain.

Marsh retired in 1855 and in 1858 applied to the New Hampshire Legislature for a charter, exhibiting a working model of the cog mechanism. Legend holds that an amendment was offered to extend his railway to the moon; the nickname stuck, and the line is still proud of "The Railway to the Moon." In 1861 he patented the cog mechanism, designed to make the locomotive safe either ascending or descending a grade. The Civil War then held the work for five years.

The Mount Washington Railway Company was formed in the spring of 1866, and construction began that May. Everything had to be hauled in from Littleton, twenty-five miles away, to the base camp at Marshfield. A water-powered sawmill on the Ammonoosuc River cut the trestle timbers on site: due to the rugged terrain, most of the track is built on trestles, along Crawford's trail route of fifty years before.

The first locomotive, built by Campbell, Whittier and Company of Roxbury, Massachusetts, was hauled in pieces from Littleton and reassembled at the base. No. 1, named Hero, carried a vertical boiler hung on trunnions so gravity would keep it upright whatever the grade — and the boiler's shape earned the engine its permanent name, Peppersass, for its likeness to a pepper sauce bottle. A demonstration run on August 29, 1866, convinced the investors.

By the summer of 1868 the crews were building the line's hardest structure: at 4,725 feet, a high trestle across a boulder-strewn gap, in the stretch Crawford had called Jacob's Ladder. On August 14, 1868, Marsh held a formal opening there, and regular passenger service to the trestle commenced thereafter. The last stretch was finished the next summer: on July 3, 1869, the first train reached the summit, and the Cog became the first mountain-climbing cog railway in the world. The construction cost, per the ASCE and the state marker, was $139,500.

The machine Marsh built worked like this, and still does. Between the running rails — four feet eight inches apart, a half-inch under standard gauge — runs the cog rail: two angle irons with rungs bolted between them four inches on center. A cog wheel under the locomotive — nineteen cogs, the ASCE counts — meshes with the rungs to draw the train up the grade or brake its descent. The locomotive runs below the coach and pushes it up; the cars carried their own braking systems, and Marsh's engines carried friction brakes and a holding ratchet. The grades are the railway's whole argument: twenty-five percent on average, 37.41 percent on Jacob's Ladder.

The early fleet burned wood, until 1910, when the railway converted to coal. The first engines came from Walter Aiken's shop in Franklin, New Hampshire, then from the Manchester Locomotive Works. Marsh died in 1884; control passed to the Concord & Montreal Railroad, then in 1889 to the Boston & Maine, and in the early 1930s to Henry N. Teague. Colonel Arthur S. Teague, a former employee, bought the railway in 1962; his wife Ellen ran it after his death in 1967. In the 1980s a partnership including Wayne Presby and Joel Bedor of Littleton bought it, and Presby remains the owner the modern railway is identified with.

Twice, the machine killed. On July 20, 1929, Peppersass — retired in 1878 and restored by the Boston & Maine, after decades on exhibition, for the sixtieth anniversary — made a commemorative climb. The Interstate Commerce Commission's report records the rest: the engine climbed to Gulf Tank, about three thousand feet from the summit, took water, and started back down light, running backwards; about 2,100 feet below the tank it derailed — a gear tooth had broken — and ran away, leaving the trestle some 2,050 feet farther on at about thirty-five miles per hour, destroyed except for its boiler. The engineman, fireman, and a photographer were injured; Daniel P. Rossiter, a photographer-writer riding for the Boston & Maine, was killed. On September 17, 1967, locomotive No. 3 derailed at the Skyline switch, about a mile below the summit; eight passengers were killed and seventy-two injured, the worst day in the railway's history.

Except for one year during the First World War and three years during the Second, the railway has operated continuously since 1869. On June 26, 1976, the ASCE and ASME together designated it a National Historic Mechanical and Civil Engineering Landmark.

The steam era ended by decision, not by failure. In the summer of 2008 the railway introduced its first biodiesel locomotive, M-1 Wajo Nanatasis, designed and built in its own shops at Marshfield — a diesel-hydraulic machine that could make three round trips for the cost of one steam trip. The shop kept building: seven biodiesel locomotives by 2019, M-1 through M-7, all built and maintained on site, as are the coaches. Steam did not leave: the railway continues to operate a pair of coal-fired locomotives — No. 2 Ammonoosuc (Manchester Locomotive Works, 1875) and No. 9 Waumbek (1908) — on scheduled steam trips in summer and fall, both, as the railway says, well over a hundred years old.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the railway was a trestle of native timber climbing out of a construction camp, worked by wood-fired and then coal-fired steam, its passengers carried up in high season only — in 1929 the ICC described a railway whose trains ran only in July, August, and September. Its safety case rested on the cog, the ratchet, and the brakeman's arm.

Now, the same alignment carries a year-round railroad. The trestle roadbed still lifts the track above the slope on bents, the ladder rack still runs up the center of the gauge, and a locomotive still pushes one coach up a twenty-five-percent average grade — but the locomotive is most likely a biodiesel machine built in the railway's own shop, and the winter train turns back at a purpose-built station at 4,000 feet with warming huts and a firepit. What has not changed is the arithmetic of the climb: 2,700 feet at the base, 6,288 at the top, Jacob's Ladder at 37.41 percent, and the summit cone's weather in any month of the year. The railway's survival is not a preservation act. It is an operating decision renewed every season since 1869, interrupted only by two wars.

## 4. What's still there

The line itself is the artifact: roughly three miles of single track on trestlework, bents spaced about twelve feet apart and varying from two to twenty feet high, in the ICC's 1929 measurement, with the contour of the land. The track is laid to a gauge of four feet, eight inches. Down its center runs Marsh's ladder rack — two angle irons, three-eighths of an inch thick in the original construction, joined by rungs of one-and-a-half-inch diameter bolts placed four inches on center. Passing sidings — at Waumbek, and at Skyline below the summit, on the side-switch pattern installed in the 1940s — let up and down trains cross on the single line.

Jacob's Ladder stands about two-thirds of the way up, at 4,725 feet: nearly three hundred feet long, curving to the left, twenty-five feet above the surface of the mountain at its highest, climbing at 37.41 percent. The railway calls it the steepest railroad trestle anywhere in the world — and, in its history page's pairing, the second highest.

At Marshfield, the base complex holds the station, the free Cog Railway Museum, the railway's shops, and the display ground: vintage steam equipment, the jet-engine snow-clearing machine, and Peppersass herself, No. 1 of 1866, reassembled after the 1929 wreck. The working fleet stands there too: seven biodiesel locomotives, M-1 Wajo Nanatasis (2008) through M-7 Kenison (2019) — the M-7 is the locomotive in this entry's first photograph — and the two working steam engines, No. 2 Ammonoosuc (1875) and No. 9 Waumbek (1908), their boilers slanted at the grade's angle. Partway up stands Waumbek Station at 4,000 feet, the winter terminus; above tree line the line crosses the summit cone's hiking trails, the Appalachian Trail among them, before the summit station just short of the peak.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Begin at Marshfield Base Station, and begin in the museum: it is free, and it gives you Marsh's machine before the mountain gives you the grades. Walk the display ground next: Peppersass, the 1866 engine that built the line and wrecked on it in 1929, and the jet-engine snow-clearing machine.

Board your coach at the platform. The locomotive takes its place below the coach and pushes; the brakeman rides the coach, works its brakes, and tells the railway's story as the climb begins. The first stretch climbs through northern hardwood forest on the trestle roadbed to Waumbek Station at 4,000 feet — the winter train's terminus, and in summer a passing point.

Above Waumbek the forest thins and the grade stiffens. At 4,725 feet the train leans onto Jacob's Ladder, the trestle curving left high above the slope at 37.41 percent — the front of your coach riding some fourteen feet higher than the back of it. Past the Ladder the trees give out entirely around five thousand feet, and the last stretch crosses the open summit cone — Skyline, the Gulfside and Appalachian Trail crossings — to the summit station just below the peak.

You have about an hour at the top, inside the railway's three-hour round trip: the visitor center, the Tip Top House of 1853, the weather exhibit. Then the descent, which is the machine's other half: the coach leads, the brakeman's brakes holding it against the grade, the locomotive following above, its cog wheel turning in the rack to brake the train's fall down the same ladder it climbed.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The charter year is stated two ways: the ASME's brochure has Marsh applying in 1858 and receiving his charter that year (secondary accounts fix the grant at June 25, 1858), while the ASCE's landmark page states he applied in 1858 and "was granted permission in 1859." Section 2 prints 1858; the 1859 stands unresolved.

Credit for the invention is likewise divided. The ASME brochure attributes the conception and execution of the idea directly to Marsh. The ASCE's page states that inventor Walter Aiken built a model of the roadbed and track with a cog rail system, and that Marsh "is credited for launching the Cog Railway and bringing Aiken's ideas to fruition." Marker No. 45 names "Herrick and Walter Aiken of Franklin and Sylvester Marsh of Campton" together as the builders. No source fetched resolves the division, and none is asserted here.

The line's length is given four ways: approximately three miles (the railway), 3.15 miles (the ICC's 1929 report), three and a quarter miles (the Congressional Record's 2019 salute), and three and a half, of which three on trestles (the ASME brochure). The maximum grade is stated two ways: 37.41 percent on the railway's own Jacob's Ladder pages (37.4 in the ASME brochure), against the ICC's stated maximum of 36.6 percent; the railway's figure is carried in §2 and §4, the ICC's unresolved against it. The superlatives are printed as sourced, not reconciled: Jacob's Ladder the steepest railroad trestle in the world and the Cog the second steepest rack railway after Switzerland's Pilatus line (the railway); the Cog's average grade "the steepest rail line in the world" (the ASCE, unqualified).

The summit's elevation is 6,288 feet in the railway's and the ASCE's print, but Marker No. 45's inscription reads "the 6,293-foot summit" — the older survey figure, still standing at the roadside. The vertical rise conflicts too: the ASCE states the railway "climbs 3,719 feet from the base station to the summit," while the railway's published elevations — 2,700 at Marshfield, 6,288 at the summit — differ by 3,588 feet, and the ASME brochure says only "almost 3,600 feet below the summit." The construction cost is $139,500 in the ASCE's facts and on the marker, $150,000 in secondary accounts; §2 follows the ASCE and the marker.

The 1929 wreck is placed two ways: the ICC's report, written within two weeks, puts Peppersass's runaway on the upper mountain, from Gulf Tank down — §2 follows it — while popular accounts run the engine down over Jacob's Ladder, recorded here rather than adopted. The Presby–Bedor purchase is dated 1983 in the railway press and 1986 in the standard reference summary; §2 says only "the 1980s." The 1967 derailment is dated September 17, 1967 in the standard accounts; one local account gives September 6 and a different cause, and the ICC's report on it was not read in this pass, so no cause is asserted.

Two locator facts are printed with their seams showing. The railway's letterhead and the Mount Washington Commission's minutes give the address as 3168 Base Station Road, Mount Washington, NH 03589, while the state tourism listing prints the same address under Bretton Woods — and the ground is in neither incorporated town: the base station and most of the line stand in Thompson and Meserve's Purchase, an unincorporated township whose 2020 census population was one, and the summit end in Sargent's Purchase. Finally, the ASCE record prints a point — 44°16′26.0″N, 71°19′55.0″W — for the landmark, without stating which point on a three-mile line it marks; no primary record fetched gives a point scoped to the railway or its base station, so §10 carries no coordinates.
