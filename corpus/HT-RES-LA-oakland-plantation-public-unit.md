## 2. Story

Elvin Shields remembered a six-foot sack for children picking cotton. Adults carried larger ones. The NPS transcript of his June 25, 2019 interview records, “The entire family of a share cropper would be on the field from sunrise to sunset.” Shields remembered that some children missed school until the end of the picking season. His family moved to Oakland in 1954. They worked cotton, hay and pecans. He describes how charges for supplies and services reduced the share that a family kept. A vegetable patch, chickens or fish could help a household limit what it bought. [5]

The plantation was called Bermuda before the Prud’homme family divided the property in 1873. The federal building survey describes the tract with the main house and outbuildings passing to Jacques Alphonse Prud’homme, who renamed it Oakland. Earlier, while the place was still Bermuda, people enslaved there supplied the labor that built and sustained the operation. In its reconstruction of the last years of enslavement, the NPS brings together the owner's journal, overseer records and other surviving sources. These are records of people as their owners or clerks documented them. Shields’s interview gives a later resident direct space to explain what a household had to do to get by. The change from slavery to sharecropping changed people’s legal status and choices, while access to land and credit could still leave a family with little at harvest. [5–8]

Start with the cabin and store in mind. Shields remembered a front porch as a place where people could sit in better light and moving air, and he describes the later kitchen at the rear using water kept in a cistern. Read those details alongside the park’s account of a one-room cabin built around 1860 and modified during later occupancy. The arrangement of the rooms bears witness to people adapting a house handed down from an earlier system. [5–7]

## 3. Then and now

Then — Houses built during slavery remained in use after emancipation. In Shields’s lifetime a household could work the crop, keep a garden and manage its account with the plantation store. The building survey records how transport and farm technology changed the business over time. [5–8]

Now — The federal unit preserves the compact plantation center, including the store, house, agricultural buildings and cabins. The park uses the store as a visitor contact and retail point during posted hours. The HABS cabin view below records both buildings at the time of the survey. A same-view present-day frame is absent. ## 5. Public progression

Use the NPS’s published directions to the Oakland pavilion and designated parking. At the pavilion pick up the current path map. Follow the signed inland path to the store and then to an open tenant-cabin exterior. Pause at the cabin without moving into a fenced area or off the public path. [1–4, 6]

**Strongest story moment — The cabin porch and the six-foot sack.** Read Shields’s description of children joining the harvest at the cabin exterior. The porch makes it possible to hold work and a family's life together in the same account. Quiet reflection. [5–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The compendium’s general statement says grounds and historic structures open daily except federal holidays. The NPS’s 2026 visitor schedule and guided-tours page give Wednesday–Sunday and put the main-house interior on weekends only. Use the narrower visitor schedule and same-day instructions. [1–2, 4]
- Shields’s interview mentions a French-government specification and general occupancy estimates. The exact regulation and applicability to this building have not been independently established. The physical cabin and mid-century family memories can be described without claiming a documented colonial mandate. [5–6, 9]
- The NPS’s Oakland historical pages draw on period records. This account has not inspected the original 1860s journals or listened to the interview recording. The quote is from the printed NPS interview transcript. [5, 7]
