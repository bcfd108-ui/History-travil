# Washita: letting Cheyenne witnesses set the terms of remembrance
OK OP01 · Draft for Dash · November 20, 2026 · Quiet witness only

## 1. Trip gate
**Washita Battlefield National Historic Site visitor center only, 18555 Hwy 47A, Ste. Use signed visitor parking and paved approach; center is listed as accessible, with an elevator. No reservation for ordinary museum access. Recheck operating notices before travel. **Visitable anchor:** Washita Battlefield NHS visitor center, signed public parking and short paved approach; indoor public galleries only, no historic village route.

## 2. The story
**The Tribes describe what remembrance asks of a visitor.** At the 2018 commemoration, Max Bear, Historic Preservation Officer for the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes, distinguished hallowed ground from a place of prayer: “It is not sacred ground, it is hallowed ground.” The Tribes' newspaper reports him describing Washita as a reminder of pain and distrust for descendants, some of whom could not bring themselves to visit. His words frame why reading at home is a complete option, not a lesser version. [4]

**A village, not only an Army report.** The Tribal Tribune centers Cheyenne families, including their loss of relatives, homes and provisions in the November 27, 1868 attack on Black Kettle's village. The historic site's federal name includes “Battlefield”; the Tribal Tribune records “Washita Massacre” as another name and reports Donald Fixico's account that many Cheyennes reject the word “battle.” A federal document transmitting official correspondence identifies the attack as November 1868, which it distinguishes from an erroneous year in the congressional request. Its Army terminology is evidence of the government's record, not the last word on what happened to families. [4,5]

## 3. Then / now
The 1868 village and today's NPS exhibit building are distinct. The visitor center interprets the attack and its context with displays, art and other materials. Do not infer that the 2018 commemorative exhibits are still installed or that a past commemorative walk grants access today. [2,4]

## 4. What's still there
- **Public interpretation:** the signed NPS visitor center, designated public parking and paved paths to the entrance; use public galleries only. [1–3]
- **Historic ground:** the Cheyenne village and its associated landscape are not the center's footprint and have no visitor approach in this entry. No community, ceremonial, riverbank, burial or private routes, even where older public sources describe special events. A historic road or district pin is not a public entrance. [1,4]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
One short indoor witness stop, or read remotely. Start with Bear's distinction, then read the Tribal Tribune account beside the federal document's differing terms. End with the reported difficulty of returning to the place, not a reconstruction of the attack. [4,5]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
No total is adopted here: accounts count different groups and the Tribal Tribune coverage includes varied estimates. Federal request year and reply year differ; 1868 is corroborated by the Tribe's publication and NPS. The Tribal Tribune is a Tribe-published account of the 2018 commemoration, not itself an 1868 eyewitness transcript. No first-person village testimony, precise event footprint or date-specific exhibit inventory established. [2,4,5]
