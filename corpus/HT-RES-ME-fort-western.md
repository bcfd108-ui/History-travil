# HT-RES-ME-fort-western — Fort Western, 1754: The Store at the Head of the Tide

Division ME seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On the east bank of the Kennebec River in Augusta, Maine, at the point where tidal water meets the river current and sailing ships could go no farther, stands the main building of Fort Western: a 100-foot wooden garrison raised in 1754 as a fortified trading post for the Proprietors of the Kennebec Purchase. The building is original — restored in 1920 and, in the National Register nomination's words, largely so — and it is the staging ground from which Benedict Arnold's expedition set out for Quebec in September 1775. The blockhouses and stockade around it are reconstructions.

## 1. Trip gate

Old Fort Western is operated by the City of Augusta, on the east bank of the Kennebec River across from downtown. The visitor address carried by the Maine Office of Tourism's listing is 16 Cony Street, Augusta, ME 04330; the National Register nomination locates the property on Bowman Street, and its boundary description sets the parcel between Cony Street and the river bank.

The operator's homepage publishes a 2026 season, all days 10:00 am to 4:00 pm: opening day is Memorial Day weekend, Saturday through Monday; June runs Friday through Monday; July and August are daily; September to October 13th returns to Friday through Monday. The operator states plainly: "PLEASE NOTE: THE LAST TOUR OF THE DAY STARTS AT 3:30 PM." The visit runs on tours, and the interiors are seen on the tour, not by wandering.

Admission as the operator's page states it: Adults $15.00; Seniors (60+) $13.00; Veterans $13.00; Students $10.00; Children 5–14 $8.00; Children under 5 free; Family Household up to 5, $35.00; Active Military free. Group discounts are available. The operator publishes no regular hours outside the season. ## 2. The story

The ground was a trading place long before it was a fort. Cushnoc, on the operator's account, was a strategic point in the Abenaki trade route network because it is where tidal water meets the river current. In 1628 the Plymouth Colony built a trading post on the bank here; it became the center of the colony's Kennebec fur trade and helped the Pilgrims pay their London backers. The trade peaked in 1634, declined, and by 1649 the Clark and Lake Company held the post on lease. In 1676, during King Philip's War, what remained of it burned, and English settlement on the river was swept away with it.

In 1660 the Plymouth patent passed to the Proprietors of the Kennebec Purchase. Wars kept them from settling the valley for two generations; by 1719 resettlement had crept only as far up the river as Richmond. Massachusetts feared a French descent down the Chaudière corridor, and any provincial fort had to stand at Teconnet Falls, seventeen miles upriver — but ships could sail only as far as Cushnoc. The bargain the Proprietors struck with Royal Governor William Shirley solved both problems: Massachusetts would build and man a provincial fort at the falls, Fort Halifax, and the Proprietors would build a fortified storehouse at Cushnoc, Fort Western, which Massachusetts would also man. Construction of both forts began in July 1754.

The National Register nomination (Charles Snell, 1972) records the build: Fort Western was erected in 1754 under the direction of Gershom Flagg, a master carpenter from Boston, for the Proprietors of the Kennebec Purchase, as a fortified fur trading post. A log stockade about 160 by 62 feet enclosed a large two-and-a-half-story main building and two two-story log blockhouses at opposite corners. The post was garrisoned under James Howard. Its work was logistics: provincial stores came up the Kennebec by ship, were unloaded and stored at Fort Western, and when river conditions were right were loaded into bateaux and carried seventeen miles upriver to Fort Halifax, whose truckhouse also drew its Indian trade goods from these stores. No settlement was attempted around the fort until after the fall of Quebec in 1759; within a year the province cut the garrison to ten men, and the garrison years ended in the 1760s — the record splits on the exact year, and the split is printed in §6.

Howard stayed. He received grants of land in 1763 and 1767, and in the late 1760s he bought Fort Western itself from the Proprietors — the price and year are also split in the record, and printed in §6. His sons Samuel and William ran the S & W Howard Store from the premises for forty years: William lived in the north end of the garrison and ran a store and blacksmith shop out of the south end, while Samuel, a sea captain based in Boston, brokered for him. In 1784 the store moved to a new building just north of the garrison and traded until 1807, when the Embargo Act put it out of business. Five of the store's account books survive, covering 1767 to 1807.

The fort's last military chapter is its most famous. In September 1775 Colonel Benedict Arnold made Fort Western the starting point and main supply base of his expedition against Quebec. The nomination gives the itinerary: on September 19 Arnold's force — 1,050 men on the nomination's count, with Daniel Morgan, Christopher Greene, Henry Dearborn, and Aaron Burr among the officers — sailed from Newburyport, Massachusetts, to the Kennebec and upriver to Gardinerstown, now Pittston, transferred to bateaux, and rowed six miles upriver to Fort Western, arriving September 24. Over the next several days the troops moved north in divisions. The operator adds where the command slept: Arnold stayed at Captain James Howard's Great House upriver, while Morgan and Burr stayed with William Howard in the old garrison. In 1779 the fort helped save the survivors of the failed Penobscot expedition, struggling south overland toward Boston.

After the Howards, the building's life turned ordinary. Descendants lived in the garrison until the mid-1850s; then it became a tenement, divided at one point into as many as eight apartments housing mill workers — the Kennebec Journal, on the operator's account, called the fort the "mean" part of town. In 1919–1920 Howard descendants William H. Gannett and Guy P. Gannett rescued it, working with the City of Augusta: they restored the main building, built two new blockhouses patterned after Fort Halifax's surviving original, and the fort passed to the city and opened as a museum in the early 1920s; the stockade was rebuilt again in 1960. In 1983, ahead of the new Augusta City Hall, archaeologists investigated the ground and found proof of the 1628 Cushnoc trading post; the fort was then reconstructed and furnished from the probate inventory, the account books, and muster rolls. Fort Western is a National Historic Landmark — though, as §6 records, the landmark's own paperwork cannot agree on the year it became one.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the fort stood at the working edge of the English Kennebec: the last point a sailing ship could reach, where cargo broke bulk into bateaux for the seventeen-mile carry to Fort Halifax. Inside its stockade, the main building did every job at once — officers' quarters at each end, and in the center a kitchen, mess, barracks, and storage rooms that later became the fur trading room and store.

Now, the same ground is a parcel of less than one acre — the nominated property — in the middle of Maine's state capital. City Hall stands beside it; Cony Street runs in front; the Kennebec still forms the parcel's western line, as the nomination's boundary description has it. What a visitor sees is one original building inside a rebuilt skin: the 1754 main building, restored and furnished, and two blockhouses and a stockade that are twentieth-century reconstructions. The fort no longer guards anything. It is read now, and the reading is unusually well documented, because the paper record of the place survived along with the building.

## 4. What's still there

The Main Building is the survival. The nomination's description: 100 feet long, 32 feet wide, two and a half stories, with a gabled, dormered roof and four large brick chimneys; walls of square hand-hewn timber 13 inches thick, covered on the exterior by wood shingles; 20 rooms and seven staircases. Restored in 1920, the nomination states, "the Main Building is largely original" — the strongest fabric claim in the record, printed here as the nomination's judgment. When first built, officers' quarters stood at each end and the center held the kitchen, mess, barracks, and storage; the center later became the fur trading room and store, and the ends were refinished as the Howard family's rooms. The nomination records the building furnished as an eighteenth-century trading post, with exhibits on the naval, military, and Indian history of the region; the operator records that after the 1983 archaeology the fort was furnished from the probate inventory, account books, and muster rolls.

The two blockhouses, each about 24 feet square on the nomination's measure, are complete reconstructions — built by the Gannetts, patterned, on the operator's account, after the original blockhouse still standing at Fort Halifax. The stockade is likewise a reconstruction, rebuilt in 1960 on the nomination's record and renewed after the 1983 investigations. Neither should be read as 1754 fabric.

The ground itself is part of the record. The nominated parcel — bounded west by the Kennebec River, its lines set 90 feet from the building's ends and 80 feet from its eastern side — holds less than one acre, and the 1983 excavations found evidence of the outbuildings and palisades and proof of the 1628 Cushnoc post in it. The site has been documented three times over: by the Historic American Buildings Survey (1933, 1965), the 1972 nomination, and the 1983 archaeology.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

The visit is a tour, and the operator's clock governs it: the last tour of the day starts at 3:30 pm, so the progression below assumes a visitor arrives with a tour in hand.

Begin at the Cony Street side, with the whole composition in view — the view this entry's first photograph takes from the lawn: one long shingled building, four chimneys, a pointed-log stockade, a blockhouse at the corner.

Inside the main building, read the plan the nomination records, because the plan is the story. The center was the working space — kitchen, mess, barracks, and stores first, then the trading room and store. The ends were officers' quarters, then the Howard family's rooms: William lived in the north end and ran the store and blacksmith shop from the south, and the building was a home and a business at once for forty years, then a tenement for sixty more — that domestic century is why it survived to be restored at all. Hold the sources in mind as you move: the operator states the furnishings were rebuilt from Samuel Howard's probate inventory, the account books, and the muster rolls.

Step out to the blockhouses and stockade and read them as what they are: 1920s reconstructions, patterned on Fort Halifax's surviving original. The honest comparison — one original building here, one original blockhouse there — is the point of the pair.

End at the river bank, the parcel's western line. This was the working edge: ships unloaded here because they could go no farther, bateaux left here for Fort Halifax, and in late September 1775 Arnold's divisions embarked here for Quebec. The head of tide is the reason everything else on the parcel exists.


## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The landmark year is not settled anywhere in the record. The Maine Historic Preservation Commission states "Year Listed: 1969" and "National Historic Landmark Designated: 1969," and the National Park Service's gallery record dates its published record December 2, 1969. The National Archives' copy of the landmark file, however, carries the date 11/07/1973 in its title. And the operator's history page states: "In 1976, Fort Western became a National Historic Landmark." Three years — 1969, 1973, 1976 — stand in the state, federal, and operator records. This dossier calls the fort a National Historic Landmark, which all the sources support, and prints no designation year.

James Howard's purchase of the fort is dated two ways. The operator's history states that in 1767 Captain Howard purchased the fort for £500. The nomination states that in 1769 he purchased Fort Western itself and about 900 acres of surrounding land from the Proprietors. The two accounts are not reconciled here.

The end of the garrison is likewise split. The nomination states that a small garrison held the post until 1763. The operator states that Fort Western was decommissioned in 1767 (and Fort Halifax in 1766), and the Maine Historic Preservation Commission states the fort "remained an active military post until 1767." The operator adds the step the nomination omits: within a year of Quebec's fall in 1759, the province reduced the garrison to ten men. This dossier prints the garrison years as ending in the 1760s and records both dates here, unresolved.

Howard himself is ranked and numbered differently. The nomination garrisons the post under "Lieutenant James Howard and 20 men." The operator names "Captain James Howard, who commanded 23 men, including his four sons: John, James Jr., Samuel & William." Both stand. Arnold's force is counted two ways as well: 1,050 men in the nomination, whose figure §2 prints, and 1,100 soldiers on the operator's history page.

The rescue of 1919–1922 is told two ways. The nomination states that in 1919 Guy P. and William Howard Gannett, descendants of the original Howard family, purchased the neglected main building, restored it in 1920, and donated it to the City of Augusta in 1921, reconstructing the blockhouses and stockade at the same time. The operator states that the city took the fort through eminent domain in 1919 and turned it over to the Gannetts in 1920, that they renovated the garrison and built the blockhouses, gifted the building back, and that it opened as a museum on July 4th, 1922. Who acquired the building in 1919, and whether the city's title dates to 1921 or the opening to 1922, stand as the two records give them.

The register's own dates contain an unexplained oddity. The nomination form states the property's specific dates as 1754–1773, and the register's database lists the significant years as 1754 and 1773 — yet the Arnold expedition, the association the same form leads with, took place in September 1775, outside that span. No source consulted explains the year 1773.

The operator's season page carries a date that does not fit its own heading. The schedule is headed "2026 season," but it dates opening day "Saturday, May 24th"; May 24 falls on a Sunday in 2026, and fell on a Saturday in 2025. The discrepancy stands here, unresolved.

Two lesser items are printed rather than smoothed. The nomination gives the Main Building's height as 16 feet — for a two-and-a-half-story building, that figure stands as the form states it, unexplained. And the "oldest" claim is worded three ways: the operator's homepage calls the fort "America's oldest wooden garrison"; its history page claims the distinction jointly — this garrison and the Fort Halifax blockhouse as "the two oldest standing French & Indian War-era wooden buildings in the country"; the nomination makes no superlative claim, calling the building "an original, superb, and little-altered example of an 18th century log fur trading post."

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** Billy Hathorn's photograph of the Main Building of Fort Western, taken from the lawn south of the stockade. The long shingled garrison fills the frame: two stories of small-paned windows trimmed in dark red under a steep shingled gable roof, four brick chimneys, two small gabled dormers, red doors. In front of the building runs the reconstructed stockade, a fence of pointed logs; at the left edge stands one of the reconstructed blockhouses with its pyramidal cap, and behind the roofline a church spire and city trees rise under a bright, lightly clouded summer sky. The building in the frame is the original 1754 main building, restored in 1920; the stockade and blockhouse are reconstructions. The Commons title calls the subject "Restoration of Fort Western," but the frame shows no work in progress — it shows the restored fort as it stands.
