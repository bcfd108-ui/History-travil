# HT-RES-NJ-edison-nhp — The invention factory: Edison's West Orange laboratory as a working plant
Division NJ seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

In November 1887 Thomas Edison moved his enterprises into a six-building brick laboratory complex on Main Street in West Orange, New Jersey — designed by Henry Hudson Holly as the largest laboratory ever built — and ran it as a working industrial plant for 44 years, until his death in 1931. Half of his 1,093 patents came out of West Orange: the motion picture camera and commercial films, the improved phonographs, the long-life alkaline storage battery, Portland cement processes, ore-milling machinery. The laboratory was the research core of a factory complex that at its wartime peak employed, on the Park Service's figures, as many as 11,000 people. The laboratory buildings the National Park Service received in 1955 still stand in their fenced quadrangle, and the park preserves them — with some 400,000 artifacts and more than five million pages of documents — as Thomas Edison National Historical Park. Glenmont, the Edison family estate in Llewellyn Park about a mile west, is the park's second unit, carried in this entry only as the park carries it: as the home Edison bought in 1886, visited by a separate ticket, on a grounds pass issued at the laboratory.

## 1. Trip gate
The operator is the National Park Service. Every visit begins at the Laboratory Complex: the park's fees and operating-hours pages both state that all visitors must begin at the Laboratory Complex Visitor Center (Building 1), and that includes visitors holding Glenmont house-tour tickets, who must stop there first for a Glenmont Grounds pass before proceeding into Llewellyn Park.

The fee is the park's Standard Pass, $15 for visitors 16 and older; children 15 and under are free. The park Annual Pass is $45, and America the Beautiful passes are accepted. The park does not accept cash — fees are paid by card at the Visitor Center or through Recreation.gov. The same entry fee is required for Glenmont. A Glenmont house guided tour is a separate ticket, and an annual pass does not guarantee Glenmont house entry. At the Visitor Center, staff collect entry fees and issue interagency passes, audio tour devices, and Glenmont Grounds passes; the building also holds the information desk, the Eastern National shop, the orientation film room, and restrooms (NPS operating-hours page, last updated January 2, 2026; NPS fees page, checked November 26, 2026).

What is open when the park is open, per the operating-hours page: the Main Laboratory Building (Building 5) — Edison's library, machine shops, music room, and other exhibits, all three floors, by stairs and elevator, at the visitor's own pace; the courtyard between the buildings, with the wood pattern shop, blacksmith shop, vaults, water tower, and the replica of the Black Maria film studio left in place by the Edison company; and the Chemistry Laboratory (Building 2), open periodically as staffing permits. Glenmont's grounds are usually open whenever the Laboratory Complex is open; the Glenmont Garage, holding Edison's historic vehicles, opens as staffing allows on Friday, Saturday, and Sunday. The park's clock schedule is treated as a gap, not borrowed from any third-party mirror — see §6.

## 2. The story
Edison bought Glenmont just before his February 1886 wedding to Mina Miller — a three-story mansion on an estate in Llewellyn Park that the park's history page gives as 13½ acres (the nomination's differing acreage is printed in §6). The laboratory followed. Henry Hudson Holly designed the West Orange complex in 1887, and the Park Service's Historic Structure Report for the Main Laboratory Building dates the construction precisely: Buildings 1 through 6 were built between July 1 and November 24, 1887, and first occupied by Edison and his research staff on November 24, 1887 — the date the National Register nomination also carries for Edison's move of his industrial enterprises to West Orange. The complex was six brick buildings forming a fenced quadrangle with a gate guard (nomination): the Main Laboratory (HS 5), about 200 feet long and three stories high, holding Edison's office and library, the machine shops, stockrooms, a music room, a photography shop, and a drafting room; the Power House–Boiler Room (HS 6) on Lakeside Avenue, built concurrently with the main building as one unit with it; and four one-story specialized buildings — HS 1, the Physics and Electrical Laboratory, called the "Galvonometer Room" in the early days; HS 2, the Chemical Laboratory; HS 3, Chemical Storage and Pattern Shop; and HS 4, the Metallurgical Laboratory. Later buildings were added after 1887, and the archives building and vault and the full-size Black Maria replica were erected after Edison's death (nomination).

The point of the place was production. Historians, the park's history page records, call the research-and-development laboratory itself Edison's greatest invention — and around it stood factories much larger than the laboratory, where what the laboratory perfected went into manufacture. As many as 200 men worked in the laboratory complex; Thomas A. Edison, Inc. employed as many as 11,000 people during the First World War, and fewer than 2,000 four years later. Factory work ran six days a week — ten hours Monday through Friday and five on Saturday (NPS). The work that came out of the quadrangle, per the nomination, includes the first successful motion picture camera and commercial films, improved phonographs in both cylinder and disc form, ore-milling machinery, Portland cement processes, and the long-life alkaline storage battery; the nomination also records the pure-science work — the "etheric force" and the "Edison Effect" — and the Physics Laboratory's later turns to educational motion pictures (1912–15) and alkaline-battery automobile tests. The Historic American Engineering Record's note for the site adds that in 1914 fire gutted the surrounding factories but left the laboratory complex untouched.

The decline was as steep as the rise. By 1930 only seven men were working in the laboratories (NPS). Edison died at West Orange on October 18, 1931, aged 84, having worked at the laboratory for 44 years. In 1955 Thomas A. Edison, Inc. gave the laboratory to the National Park Service; by 1962 the laboratory and Glenmont were combined into a single national park unit. Charles Edison sold the company to McGraw Electric in 1959; McGraw-Edison had left West Orange by 1971, and most of the factory buildings were torn down (NPS). The laboratory quadrangle the factories once surrounded is what survives.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the winter of 1888, in the §7 photograph — the new quadrangle behind its picket fence on Main Street: one-story laboratory buildings in front, the three-story Main Laboratory behind them, the water tower over the roof, snow on everything, the plant weeks into its first year. **Now:** the same quadrangle, preserved as a museum; the Park Service maintains the 1931 appearance of the main building's first floor and library, and displays the Chemical Laboratory as it appeared in 1927–31 (nomination).

**Then:** as many as 200 men in the laboratory and, at the wartime peak, 11,000 across Thomas A. Edison, Inc., working six-day weeks. **Now:** the buildings hold the park's collection — some 400,000 artifacts and more than five million pages of documents (NPS) — and the workforce is the park staff and the visitor.

**Then:** the Black Maria, the first purpose-built motion picture studio, part of the laboratory complex from 1893 until 1903 (NPS). **Now:** a full-size replica of it stands in the courtyard, erected after Edison's death (nomination), on the ground the original turned on.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The 1887 laboratory quadrangle on Main Street at Lakeside Avenue — six brick buildings in a fenced compound, the Laboratory unit of the park, 5.69 acres (NRHP nomination)
- Building 5, the Main Laboratory: Edison's library, machine shops, music room and exhibits, all three floors open when the park is open; first floor and library maintained in their 1931 appearance (NPS operating-hours page; NRHP nomination)
- Building 2, the Chemistry Laboratory, displayed as it appeared in 1927–31 and open periodically as staffing permits (NRHP nomination; NPS operating-hours page)
- The courtyard structures left in place by the Edison company: wood pattern shop, blacksmith shop, vaults, water tower, and the Black Maria replica (NPS operating-hours page)
- The park's holdings: some 400,000 artifacts and more than five million pages of documents (NPS)
- Glenmont, about a mile west in Llewellyn Park: the Edison family estate and mansion, its grounds usually open when the Laboratory Complex is open, its house by separate guided-tour ticket only, its garage open as staffing allows Friday–Sunday (NPS operating-hours and fees pages; NRHP nomination)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Within the park's terms as §1 prints them — begin at the Laboratory Complex, fee paid, Glenmont Grounds pass in hand before any Glenmont stop:

1. **Building 1, the Visitor Center:** Orientation film, entry pass, audio tour device. Every visit, including a Glenmont house tour, starts here.
2. **Building 5, the Main Laboratory, all three floors:** Library, machine shops, music room — the 200-foot building that held the office, the shops, the stockrooms, and the drafting room of the working plant.
3. **The courtyard:** Pattern shop, blacksmith shop, vaults, water tower, and the Black Maria replica — the plant's yard, read as a yard.
4. **Building 2, the Chemistry Laboratory, if staffing has it open:** The one-story laboratory displayed as of 1927–31, the laboratory's last working years.
5. **Glenmont, on the Grounds pass:** Grounds and greenhouse; the garage if it is a Friday–Sunday staffing day; the house only with its separate tour ticket.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Glenmont's acreage prints two ways in the Park Service's own record: the park's history page gives the estate Edison bought in 1886 as 13½ acres; the National Register nomination gives Glenmont 15.67 acres and the Laboratory unit 5.69 acres. Both forms print here; §2 and §4 use each figure only with its source attached. Peak employment also prints two ways in Park Service texts: the park's history page gives Thomas A. Edison, Inc. as employing as many as 11,000 during the First World War; the Park Service's Google Arts & Culture partner text gives the factory complex reaching a peak of about 10,000 in 1919–1920. Section 2 uses the 11,000 figure with its source named. Laboratory staffing prints two ways as well: the park's history page gives as many as 200 men in the six-building complex, while the Historic American Engineering Record's site note describes Edison working "along with a staff of about sixty assistant researchers" — different scopes, research staff against all hands, printed unreconciled.

The park's clock schedule could not be read from the operator's own pages in this pass. The operating-hours page (last updated January 2, 2026) describes in full what is open when the park is open, and refers visitors to the park's Basic Information page for the latest hours; in the copies of those pages read for this entry, the hours table itself is rendered from a data feed and was not legible. Third-party directories publish a schedule they attribute to the Park Service — Thursday through Monday, 9:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m. — but under this entry's rule a schedule prints only from the operator's own readable text, so §1 prints no clock hours and this gap stands in their place.

The nomination's geographical data could not be used for a point: in the copy of the nomination checked, that section is not legibly reproduced (its combined acreage reads 21.36, which the two unit figures — 5.69 and 15.67 — sum to, but its UTM reference does not read cleanly), so no UTM from the nomination prints in §10. The point that does print in §10 comes from the Park Service's own NPGallery asset records for the park, scoped exactly as those records scope it. The nomination's specific-dates line gives Edison's move to West Orange with the day partly obscured in the copy checked ("November 2k, 1887"); the day, the 24th, is carried by the Historic Structure Report's statement that the buildings were first occupied November 24, 1887, and §2 rests on the two together.
