## 2 · Story — The trade in waiting

The schoolhouse is the place to begin in season. The loading ground is a different place. Read’s Landing sat across the Mississippi from the Chippewa, where captains, lumbermen and merchants had reasons to stop that had little to do with the size of the settlement. The river carried logs south. Lumbermen needed supplies for work upriver. Steamers coming north had to contend with ice on Lake Pepin, the wide reach immediately upstream. [3–5,7]

The *Winona Daily News* bicentennial feature, reissued online in 2016, puts Fordyce S. Richards’s 1855 store and warehouse at Water and Richards streets and Knapp, Stout & Co. next to it the following season. It calls the interval between the opening of the river below and Lake Pepin above “about two weeks in the early spring.” This is the newspaper’s retrospective characterization, rather than an annual ice record. Read that sentence in the schoolhouse and imagine what the interval meant to three people: a passenger awaiting passage, a clerk responsible for stock, and a rafter looking for supplies. [4]

The newspaper assigns different dates to the end of those trades. The 1871 railroad damaged the steamboat business. It dates the displacement of the remaining Chippewa Valley supply trade to the construction of the Chippewa Division in 1882. The surviving street photograph, dated about 1882, shows residents gathering along a business street. It gives the supposed decline a human scale. A changing trade still had a town full of people to face it. [4,7]

## 3 · Then / now

Then, the river-edge businesses could exchange goods between boat, bank and lumber supply network. The late nineteenth-century photo records the settlement’s street fabric, not the warehouse interiors. Today the public visit is to an 1870 Italianate brick schoolhouse, operated seasonally by the county historical society. The operator describes a museum main level and separate Agricultural Heritage Building. The schoolhouse is evidence of a community built beside the trades. The roadside overlook, built in 1939–40, is a much later project on different ground. [1,3–5,7]

## 5 · Trip progression

In season, follow the museum operator’s approach, start in the former schoolhouse and read the warehouse names there. Pair the town-street image with the account of Lake Pepin ice. **Story landing — Two weeks at the foot of the lake.** Read the newspaper’s interval from museum space, where a visitor can grasp what waiting meant for a river town. Outside the season, use the same story at Lake City’s public Concourse in safe conditions. That is an alternate view of Lake Pepin and a different historic location. [1,4,7–8]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- The operator’s homepage shows Friday–Sunday hours without the season. Its visit page limits those hours to mid-May–mid-October. The detailed visit page governs. [1–2]
- A cached MnDOT project result explicitly says the overlook is barricaded pending repair. Direct page requests returned HTTP 502; no reopening date was established. The static historic-property description does not establish open access. [5–6]
- The circa 1882 image is a view of an inhabited street, not proof of ongoing warehouse use at a named lot. The Richards store lot, pre-rail loading line and any safe public vantage need independent map and land-manager confirmation. [4,7]
- MNHS Federal Writers’ Wabasha place-name and county manuscript scans were retrieved. No verified passage identifying the warehouse parcel or a firsthand rafters’ quotation was extracted for this draft. The newspaper’s waterfront account is retrospective. [4,9–10]
