# Duncan — the labor of getting a herd north
Research draft · OK OP03 · Dash review pending

## 2 · The story

### A river can take the whole day

George C. Duffield was driving cattle from Texas toward Iowa in 1866. Near the Red River, his diary describes a cold morning with “all hands shivering.” On a subsequent day the work continued, and fifty cattle were still on the Texas side at the end of his tally. He records moving the last animal across near dusk on the final day. The same entry records Mr. Carr’s drowning. The pauses on the page show people and cattle having to endure the river a crossing at a time. They are not a generic illustration of a northbound arrow on a map. [4]

That account survives here in the published diary transcription in the *Annals of Iowa*. This party’s destination was Iowa, and the ride preceded Joseph G. McCoy’s 1867 Abilene operation. Read Duffield in Duncan as an eyewitness to the work of a documented earlier drive, with no claim his party passed the Duncan site. [4–6]

### How a town grew next to the work

McCoy established a market at the railhead in Abilene in 1867. The Oklahoma Historical Society’s route account places the commercial cattle corridor near Monument Hill and the Old Duncan Store after it crossed into Indian Territory. The OHS historical-markers register connects William Duncan’s store to the drovers who stopped along the way. The store is a clue to the kind of traffic a cattle trail produced. A town could grow where traveling crews needed to replace and buy supplies. The original store parcel and its remains still require verification. [5–7]

The National Park Service study examines the Chisholm and Great Western trails as distinct subjects. The story here follows the corridor serving the Abilene market and the work of keeping animals and people moving, not a reconstructed single wagon track through modern Duncan. [5,8]

## 3 · Then / now

- **Historic location.** The cattle corridor near Old Duncan Store, along the broader route the OHS describes through southwest Oklahoma. The OHS places the historic path east of its marker in town. Treat private land and roadside traces as archival context. [5,7]
- **Lawful physical anchor.** Chisholm Trail Heritage Center, 1000 Chisholm Trail Parkway. Drive-up with a short walk to the visitor entrance. Ordinary galleries and the site's visitor approach comprise the physical stop. [1,9]
- **Optional public exterior.** City Memorial Park on North US-81 between Pine and West Beech. Use signed park access and paths. The stone marker is a public interpretation lead listed by OHS. Park section, marker approach and continuous footing need local confirmation. Use the municipal park only. [2,7,15]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. If the park is usable, start from its signed entrance and stay within its paths. The OHS record is available to read even if the marker is unreachable from a path. [2,7]
2. Continue to the Heritage Center. Read the commercial railhead context alongside its descriptions of cattle, riders and travel. The narrated exhibit is interpretation. [1,5,9–10]
3. **Strongest story moment — Fifty still on the Texas side.** Finish inside the Heritage Center with Duffield’s preceding days of work and the end-of-day tally. Read the subsequent line about Carr quietly. The full trip works at the museum when the park is unsafe or closed. [1,4]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The Duncan tourism summary describes the trail as running from the Fort Worth stockyards to Abilene and through modern Duncan. OHS describes several Texas approaches converging at the Red River and locates the route east of its modern town marker. Plan around the museum and park anchors and leave the exact route overnight stops and traces unresolved. [3,5,7]
