## 1. Trip gate
**Three different places must remain separate:** the original Hudson's Bay Company trading-post landscape near Parma; the later replica/museum in Parma; and the **1863 military Fort Boise in Boise**, now associated with the VA medical campus. They are not interchangeable stops. [1,2,3]

Permanent rule: **“a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.”** For the original-post landscape, NPS identifies interpretation at the **boat-launch parking lot within Fort Boise Wildlife Management Area**, managed by Idaho Fish and Game. Use only the operator's signed public entrance, parking and open interpretation. No river crossing, island landing, presumed fort footprint or private route is proposed. [1]

IDFG's current rules specify **5 a.m.–10 p.m. day use**, with internal roads closed to motor vehicles and some areas closed **February 1–July 31** for nesting birds. Use Mountain Time. Its headquarters address, **30845 Old Fort Boise Road**, is not a guarantee of visitor-center service. Current map and signs govern the allowed route. [4]

A **October 19, 2026** notice announced a Gold Island closure for herbicide application planned October 20, with weather-dependent scheduling. No reopening confirmation was located. Do not turn the passed date into clearance; the island is excluded regardless. No closure of the entire mainland is inferred from that notice. [5]

Parma's city page publishes replica tours on **Friday–Sunday afternoons, June–August**. No November interior visit or exact clock hours are confirmed. Exterior park use and museum entry are different permissions; municipal office hours are not museum hours. [2]

## 2. The story
The original post was a commercial establishment, not the later army fort. NPS describes Thomas McKay's 1834 Snake Fort, HBC's takeover in 1836 and relocation to the Boise–Snake confluence in 1837. The institution served fur trading and later emigrant resupply. Its history includes Hawaiian employees, not just the managers whose names usually headline the account. These are NPS's historical conclusions; individual employment records were not inspected. [1]

A named traveler supplies a narrow primary-document view. The NPS comprehensive management plan quotes **Joel Palmer, September 2, 1845**, on flour at the fort: **“for which they demand twenty dollars per cwt. in cash.”** This is a quotation mediated by an agency document, not a direct inspection of Palmer's original journal. It demonstrates one reported transaction context, not a universal price schedule or today's equivalent value. [6]

The crossing and the trading post were connected but not identical. The same NPS management document describes low-water fording versus ferrying at other conditions. This is historical transport evidence only, never advice to test the river now. [6]

A supply-post story is incomplete without the people whose homeland was being transformed. In **Sho-Ban News, June 15, 2017**, **Lori Edmo-Suppah** reports on the *Original Boise Valley People* exhibit and its intergenerational oral histories. Her article describes forced removal, family separation and imprisonment at forts. The contemporary Tribal publication supplies a direct published voice about how descendants preserve that history. Its indexed text was retrieved; the full PDF returned a fetch error. Do not relocate every experience described in that regional article to the older Parma trading post. [7]

The VA confirms that the military Fort Boise dates to **1863** and that its present medical center uses some old fort buildings. The military institution belongs to a distinct history and location; no hospital or campus tour is authorized by this dossier. [3]

## 3. Then / now
The original building complex is not standing at the river. NPS describes flood destruction and subsequent abandonment, with the exact fort location now uncertain. A modern commemorative marker is evidence of remembrance, not a surviving wall or surveyed footprint. [1]

The Parma replica is deliberately a reconstruction. Its walls help interpret a type of place but do not prove that the original stood there. [2] The modern wildlife landscape has its own management purpose; archaeological interest does not override habitat closures or private ownership.

## 4. What's still there
- **WMA public interpretation:** NPS identifies waysides and a commemorative statue at the river boat-launch parking lot. Current condition and readability were not field-checked; use only signed open surfaces. [1]
- **Parma replica/museum:** city-documented public interpretation with a summer tour season, not authentic original-post fabric. No winter interior promise. [2]
- **Wildlife-management facilities:** IDFG documents a viewing platform and designated public access. These are not permission to search islands, wetlands or riverbanks for artifacts. [4]
- **Military Fort Boise:** separate Boise location and working medical institution, not an alternative entrance to the Parma story. [3]

No original ferry hardware, intact trading-post structure, burial location or recoverable object is identified for visitors.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Proposed interpretation sequence, not a released route:
1. Before departure, choose the subject explicitly: original trading-post landscape or Parma replica. Check the corresponding operator's access information; do not navigate from a generic Fort Boise search pin.
2. If conditions and signs permit, read the public WMA interpretation from the designated parking/wayside area. Look at the river as a changing landscape, not an invitation to find an exact lost building.
3. Read Palmer's short price observation. Ask what cash, food and an uncertain crossing meant to travelers, without assuming all people experienced the post as a refuge.
4. Pair that with Edmo-Suppah's account of descendants preserving removal and family-separation histories. This is reflection, not an excursion to loss ground.
5. When seasonally available, the Parma replica can be a separate interpretive visit. In November it is not a required interior stop.

**Strongest story moment:** one familiar name can conceal different institutions, places and consequences. A resupply post, a later army fort and a modern replica cannot responsibly be collapsed into one reassuring frontier story.

Reflection question: **Whose movement did this place support, whose freedom was constrained in the wider history, and what does the evidence actually place here?** No reenactment, casualty hunt, ceremonial attendance or photograph of sensitive ground is requested.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Current NPS history gives 1837 for relocation and 1855 for abandonment. State tourism text gives 1854 abandonment. These are unresolved interpretive discrepancies; this draft does not silently select a universal final date. [1,8]
- The NPS page, updated November 11, 2026, contains a contradictory lower address in **Granger, Wyoming**. That is not used. Its Parma identification is cross-checked against IDFG; the printed GPS remains only a site locator. [1,4]
- A specific Gold Island reopening notice was not found. No inference that all WMA land is closed or that the island has reopened. [5]
- The Tribal article's reference to imprisonment at Fort Boise must not be automatically attributed to the pre-1855 trading post. Detailed family/source histories would be required. No ceremonial or burial coordinates are supplied. [7]
- No original Palmer manuscript, HBC staff register, precise fort footprint, current water-edge access inspection or nineteenth-century site photo was verified.
