## 2 · Story — The people who met the train

A railroad station could be someone’s first place to ask where to spend the night. Melvin Whitfield Carter Sr. remembered that Black travelers who were unwelcome at most hotels asked redcaps “where they could find a room.” He also remembered luggage, parking cars, cleaning and polishing brass. These are the worker’s recollections as excerpted by Lisa Heinrich, rather than sentences verified against the unabridged oral history. Their range shows why the people in red caps did more than carry a bag from a train. [4–5]

Gloria Ellen Gilbreath Wilson remembered becoming a depot matron. The published excerpt describes the job as cleaning restrooms and helping travelers. The archive’s independently cataloged interview summary says she started at 31, and that her father worked as a redcap here for 41 years. It also connects the family to a home taken for a freeway bridge. The separate Rondo dossier follows that displacement. Here the traveler can attend to the public work her family did inside the station. [4–5]

The Main Hall was where passengers bought tickets, looked for a telephone or telegraph and used businesses and services around the perimeter. The register expansion’s research describes three mail elevators in the former parcel-check room east of the Concourse Passage. In the 1920 interior photograph, benches channel people toward service windows and doorways. It makes clear how much waiting stood between reaching the depot and catching a train. Photograph and architectural plan document the room. The worker accounts tell us what happened when a traveler needed help. [6–7]

## 3 · Then / now

Work on the present complex began in 1917, but the railroad continued using parts of the site while construction proceeded. The 2014 boundary-increase documentation places the headhouse in use by 1920 and completion of the entire complex in 1926. The former public interior was restored in 2012 and the depot again serves surface transportation. The public hall is a lawful interpretive anchor; rail operations and booked spaces remain separate. [1,6,8–9]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- The original 1974 nomination describes construction from 1917 to 1923. The expanded designation uses 1917–1926 for the full complex and describes phased openings. Keep the 1920 headhouse and 1926 full-complex milestones distinct. [6,8]
- MNopedia dates the sale of the concourse and waiting room to the Postal Service to 1978; Heinrich’s account says 1977 for purchase of train deck, concourse and waiting room. A deed or closing record was not consulted. [4,9]
- The operator Contact page prints 10 p.m.–6 a.m. for ticket-restricted access to the Waiting Room and seating. The Policies page literally displays 9 p.m.–6 p.m. under this rule, while both print 6 a.m.–10 p.m. as exterior-door building hours. This draft relies on ordinary daytime public Main Hall circulation and posted controls. [1–2]
- Wilson’s legacy item link returned HTTP 404. The Wilson and Carter records are supported by the archive finding aid and published excerpts, respectively. Full interviews and contemporary personnel and wage records were not read. [4–5,11]
