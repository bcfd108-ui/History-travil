## 2. The story
Rich lead-silver-zinc deposits were found in Burke Canyon on **May 2, 1884**, and the town of Burke was established in **1887** at about 3,700 feet in the Coeur d'Alene Mountains. The canyon set the terms for everything after: at its narrowest it is about **300 feet wide**, so buildings were constructed over Canyon Creek and over the railroad itself, businesses on the west side reportedly had to **retract their awnings when trains passed**, and the town's main street *was* the railroad. The Canyon Creek Railroad — incorporated by S.S. Glidden on July 6, 1887, a three-foot narrow-gauge line from Wallace to the Tiger Mine — reached Burke in 1888.

The **Tiger Hotel** took its name from the Tiger Mine. The Tiger Hotel Company Records at Archives West describe it plainly: *a three-story frame hotel with 150 rooms, built over the South Fork of the Coeur d'Alene River, with the Northern Pacific Railroad tracks running through the lobby*, extending virtually from one canyon wall to the other. It was built in the **fall of 1896** as boarding rooms for miners. When the railroad was widened in **1906**, there was nowhere for the hotel to move, so the hotel was modified to let the railroad run through it, with an enclosed walkway above the tracks so guests could cross between the two halves. In its 1890s heyday, by *Wild West* magazine's account, **five trains a day ran through the lobby** and the attached boardinghouse served **1,200 meals a day**; the *Spokane Daily Chronicle* noted that *“only heavy sleepers were put in the rooms above the railroad tracks.”* Burke's population peaked around **1,400 in 1910**.

The canyon also collected disaster the way it collected buildings: avalanches, floods, and fire. A **1923 fire** destroyed the Tiger Hotel and much of Burke; the hotel was rebuilt and enlarged — the 1927 Sanborn map shows it as two distinct buildings. Mining faded across the middle of the century, the last mine closed in **1991**, and the population fell to about **15**. The hotel did not survive to see it: see the gaps section for its tangled end.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a working town folded into a slot canyon — trains through a hotel lobby, awnings cranked in for the noon run, 1,200 boardinghouse meals a day. **Now:** a ghost town in the same slot: skeletal mill and mine buildings against the canyon walls, empty brick storefronts along the single street, the creek and the grade still sharing the floor exactly as they did in 1887. The canyon is the exhibit; it explains the whole town in one look up.

## 4. What's still there
- The canyon itself — stand on Burke's street and measure the 300 feet with your eyes; every strange fact about this town follows from that width
- The mill and mine ruins along the canyon — the Hecla, Hercules, Star, and Tiger-Poorman ground that fed the town
- The Tiger Hotel ground on the main street: the hotel is gone (demolished 1954), but the street, the creek, and the rail grade still run through where its lobby stood
- Surviving brick commercial buildings and the holes and foundations cut into the canyon walls where homes were built into the slope

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The hotel's dates disagree between honest sources, and this entry prints both: Archives West and the standard town history date the original hotel to fall 1896 (some popular retellings say 1887 or 1888, apparently confusing it with the railroad's arrival). Its end is tangled too: Archives West records the hotel destroyed by fire in April 1944 and later sold to Ed Woods of Osburn, who had it torn down as a vandalized fire hazard; the commonly repeated demolition year is 1954. The 'five trains a day' and '1,200 meals' figures are the Wild West / hotel-era accounts, carried as period claims.
