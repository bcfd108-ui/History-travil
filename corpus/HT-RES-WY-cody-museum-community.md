## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** museums are not filing cabinets that somehow explain themselves. People select, describe, research, record, scan and revise what visitors encounter. The work is often least visible when the result feels effortless.

**Named first-person worker voice:** in her July 29, 2016 post **“Farewell For Now,” Elenita Nicholas** describes an eight-week curatorial internship at the Whitney Western Art Museum. The museum publishes her account under her own name. She reports creating digital interpretation and audio-guide material, while explicitly crediting colleagues' contributions. [2]

Her direct statement is **“I couldn’t have imagined succeeding in any of these projects without the help of others.”** Nicholas names Seth Johnson for digital collaboration and Mack Frost for scanning historical photographs. This is a contemporary self-authored reflection on her internship, not an independent audit of project completion. [2]

**Original visitor narration:** An audio stop has a speaker, but it also has people behind it: somebody found the source, somebody checked the words, somebody prepared the image, somebody made the device work. When a visitor hears one clear voice, that does not mean one person did the work. A museum can be read as a community of labor, not only a collection of famous names.

Nicholas's account makes this a specific Cody workplace story rather than a generic explanation of museum careers. It does not establish her current employment or imply visitors can meet her today. Her account's named supervisors and colleagues refer to 2016, not a verified present staff directory.

**Historical institutional context:** Michaela Jones's 2017 museum account describes **Mary Jester Allen** beginning as curator in 1927 and undertaking duties ranging from guiding and registration to accounting and custodial work. This is later institutional history, not Allen's firsthand voice. [3] It provides a useful comparison: interpreting a collection has long depended on labor beyond the title on an office door.

The comparison is an interpretive proposal, not proof that the two women's working conditions were equivalent. Pay, employment status, hours, authority and resources were not reconstructed. The aim is to notice work without replacing missing evidence with a sentimental story of universal dedication.

**Public-gallery version, editorial allowance 45–60 minutes, not an operator program:**
1. Confirm current Center admission and Whitney access. Enter at the designated public admission door, not a staff or collection entrance.
2. In an admitted gallery, choose an available ordinary interpretive label or public audio-guide description. No specific object is promised in advance.
3. Read Nicholas's short collaboration passage, with author and 2016 date visible. Ask which kinds of work a finished interpretation can conceal.

5. End with the strongest story moment: a visitor-facing explanation is the result of people helping one another make meaning, not merely an object placed in a case.

**Documentary fallback, editorial allowance 20–30 minutes:** compare the two published accounts and the current digital-guide description. A closed museum or unavailable gallery need not end the history; it changes the visit to reading rather than an improvised exterior walk.
