## 2. The story

**Named firsthand source: Allan Grant**, interviewed by Dale E. Treleven on April 29–30 and May 1, 1991 for California State Archives, through UCLA’s Oral History Program. Selected portions of the transcript were directly downloaded and read. In session 1, printed pages 19–20, Grant recalls taking oil-field work in the Signal Hill/Long Beach area after his father’s death because he needed higher earnings. He corrects the interviewer’s proposed mid-1920s date to **1928**. He then explains that supporting siblings required more than food, and that the need to cover clothing helped move him toward sharecropping. [3]

This is a brief, retrospective worker-and-household passage within a much longer interview centered on Grant’s later public life. It does not establish a typical oil worker’s income or experience, exact employer, tenure, job classification or daily work routine. No direct quotation is reproduced: the manuscript’s literary-rights statement requires written permission for quotation for publication. [3]

**Original interpretive voice:** An oil boom can look enormous in a skyline photograph and very small in a household budget. This story begins with that difference. A slightly better wage mattered, yet the test of its value came at home. The useful question is not what one worker produced, but what his earnings could actually sustain. Leaving a job can be as revealing as taking it.

Grant is one voice, not a stand-in for every worker, woman, immigrant household or community affected by oil. The next historical depth would come from other publicly released worker and household records, not from extrapolating his later prominence backward into the experience of an entire workforce.
