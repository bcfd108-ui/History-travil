# Fayetteville Lincoln School and Walker Park public reading stop
HT-RES-AR-fayetteville-black-education-workers · Arkansas OP05 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Preston Lackey Jr. remembered that “we went to school over here til the ninth grade at Lincoln School.” In the oral history published by Julianne Lewis Adams and Thomas A. DeBlack, he explains that he and his sisters had to continue their high school studies outside Fayetteville under the segregated system. Their earlier schooling in town and the arrangement for continued study were parts of one education, which he recalled in the language of a student and family who had to make it work. When they joined the first group of Black students at Fayetteville High in 1954, it changed where they could finish school. The source is an Arkansas education agency’s excerpt of a published interview. [3]

A previous generation had worked under different constraints. The city’s context statement identifies teachers Herman and Hazel Davis at the older Henderson School, with separate age groups sharing the same building. It follows the land exchange that made a new school possible at South Willow and East Center. The document reproduces a 1934 newspaper reference to donated labor and federal work on the building. The city’s account interprets that exchange within a shifting segregated housing pattern. Its maps and the property references take the reader from a broad story of segregation to the decisions about rooms, labor and land that affected where children learned. [4, 5]

Lackey remembered apprehension, confusion about timing, and the simple act of going to homeroom. His own experience and that of his sisters had differences. His story belongs with records of those students individually, including the accounts still sought. The old Lincoln building was later demolished for Willow Heights housing. The park reading stop asks visitors to consider the testimony at a public place without going to occupied homes or active schools. [1, 3–5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Lincoln School stood at South Willow Avenue and East Center Street. The city identifies it with the later of two schools that shared the Henderson name. Willow Heights housing replaced the demolished Lincoln School. The older Henderson building on Olive and Lafayette survives as a private residence. Walker Park is a separate public anchor and outlook for reading the community history. [1, 4, 5]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Walker Park, 10 W. 15th Street, Fayetteville. Drive to designated public parking and take a short walk on an open municipal path near the entrance. The city lists a pavilion and an historical mural, but no Lincoln exhibit is claimed here. Keep residential grounds, former school footprint, private buildings and active campuses outside the visit. [1, 2, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Enter the public park and take a short walk to an available shared path or seating area. Read the city’s maps and Lackey’s testimony with the difference between the two Henderson buildings in mind. **Strongest story moment · Taking a seat in homeroom.** In the student’s account, entering Fayetteville High made an ordinary school routine possible in his own city. Give the account quiet attention. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The city distinguishes the older Henderson building and the new school that also opened under the Henderson name. Its overview mentions Jefferson School’s integration, while Lackey’s 1954 testimony concerns Fayetteville High School. They are separate schools and the overview does not establish a common end date for segregation across grades. [3–5]
- Lackey speaks of Lincoln’s grade limit from personal experience. Keep his recollection distinct from any district-wide grade limits in earlier or later years. Original district minutes, rolls, press pages and the complete oral history audio were not retrieved. The state-hosted excerpt was read in the indexed PDF. A direct PDF request returned HTTP 503. [3, 5]
- The 1948 Sanborn excerpt is a map. Treat it as site evidence and withhold any location inside the housing grounds. [4]
