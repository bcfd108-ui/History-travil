## 2. Story
**Buffalo Trace Distillery / George T. Stagg Distillery · Research draft · Fred**

Before the familiar distillery name, there was a building used for moving and processing goods. The federal nomination traces the Swigert family's warehouse from a mid-century pork business to Daniel Swigert's distillery. An October 1858 advertisement quoted in that nomination calls the steam engine “splendid” and reports that it had cost one thousand dollars. The original newspaper scan was not retrieved. The excerpt shows what a seller wanted a buyer to see: machinery and a purpose-built production operation. [4]

Edmund Haynes Taylor Jr. took over in 1870 and invested in the operation. George T. Stagg later became a principal figure, and Albert Bacon Blanton is a named figure of the twentieth-century business in the National Register record. The everyday work was distributed through mashing, fermenting, cooperage-related storage, shop work, fire protection and warehousing. A painted “Employees Only” sign recorded in the 1940s carpentry shop indicates where one kind of work took place. The small infrastructure of fire protection, documented by the nomination's hydrant and hose-house survey, also mattered at a complex holding large amounts of combustible stock. [4–5]

During Prohibition, the trade in authorized medicinal spirits gave part of the business a regulated market. The nomination research records a million pints of medicinal whiskey bottled in 1925, and describes disagreements among physicians about its legitimacy. The present operator reports renewed work following the 2025 flood. Its evidence describes continued production and later recovery; access still belongs to the specified visitor boundary. [1, 4, 6]

## 3. Then and now
Warehouse A, described by the operator as an 1881 aging warehouse, is its current gallery and visitor shop. The preserved district also includes buildings of different dates and functions. Public admission to one former work space does not establish access to adjacent operations. [1, 3–4]

## 4. Surviving features and access
- **Public interior** — Warehouse A visitor center and gallery within staffed hours.
- **Public exterior** — Signed visitor entrance and ordinary operator-approved paths.
- **Controlled tour** — Only a confirmed operator-led itinerary. Tasting is optional and governed separately.
- **Outside the visit** — Production, storage and other work areas outside the guide boundary, plus riverbanks and flood works. [1–4]

## 5. Public trip progression
Begin at the signed Warehouse A entrance. Use the visitor gallery to distinguish the making of spirits from the separate work of storing them. Finish at **The warehouse was part of the machinery**, looking from an open visitor area at how an aging building became a record of a larger industrial system. A guided tour adds access only where the operator authorizes it. [1–4]
