## 2. The story

**Named campaign voice: Aurora Lucero.** Historian Cathleen D. Cahill's NPS-hosted account reproduces Lucero's words: **“I speak for the Spanish American women”**. The surrounding passage describes her concern for household welfare and bilingual suffrage advocacy. This is a historical participant's statement reproduced in later scholarship, **not an original speech manuscript inspected here**. Its exact date and item-level archival provenance remain unresolved. [4]

Cahill identifies Lucero and Arabella Romero among speakers seeking Senator Thomas Catron's support in 1915. Nina Otero-Warren's later organizing and lobbying are separately documented; she is not silently inserted into that parade. Economically secure, politically connected women were prominent in this coalition. [4]

**Original interpretive voice:** A campaign's language tells us whom it expects to hear. Translating a demand is not merely changing words on a page. It recognizes people as political participants. Yet a coalition can widen its circle without including everyone. The strongest account holds both facts together: women changed the political argument, and their victory did not finish the work of equal participation.

This is historical interpretation, not a current voting guide or advice about eligibility.

## 3. Then / now

The University of New Mexico's historical timeline records New Mexico's ratification on February 21, 1920 and later struggles over Native voting rights. It prevents the false ending that every woman gained practical ballot access in 1920. [5] Detailed Tribal perspectives are a further research requirement; this draft does not impersonate them.

A modern museum/library is the public learning anchor, not evidence that an original campaign meeting occurred in its current rooms. No live suffrage display or Lucero document availability was verified.
