# Joliet Iron Works Historic Site — the ruins of Joliet's first great steel works
HT-RES-IL-joliet-iron-works-historic-site · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Four years after the Civil War, the Union Coal, Iron and Transportation Company began building an iron and steel factory at Joliet, where the Illinois & Michigan Canal, the Des Plaines River and several railroads met, and where dolomite limestone was quarried along the river valley. The operator's history dates the works' operation from the 1870s into the 1930s and names the company as the works' creator; the rolling mill's first blow is dated March 1873 in the works' standard summary history. What was rolled here mattered far beyond Will County: railroad rails from Joliet helped lay the track of a continent still binding itself together after the war, and the plant was among the early American adopters of the Bessemer process, which made high-volume steel production possible. At its fullest the works carried four blast furnaces, a stock house and casting house, hot-blast stoves, a gas washing plant, blowing and gas engine houses, and a dam on the Des Plaines for power. Together with the quarries, it gave Joliet its standing name — the City of Steel and Stone. [1, 2, 5, 6]

Ownership followed the consolidation of American steel. The Joliet Iron and Steel Company was acquired by the newly formed Illinois Steel Company in 1889; Illinois Steel passed to Federal Steel, and Federal Steel became a central piece in the formation of United States Steel in 1901. The works thus moved, in one working lifetime, from a local canal-side enterprise to a plant inside the largest business combination of its age, while the work on the ground stayed the same work: ore, limestone and coke charged into furnaces, iron cast, steel rolled, rails shipped. [5, 6]

The workforce is the second half of the story, and the preserve's own interpretation keeps it in front. The operator records that thousands worked at the Iron Works, and that many of the jobs were filled by immigrants — the first arriving from northern and western Europe with industrial skills already in hand. Later summaries of the District's own brochure, carried in the works' reference history, put about 2,000 workers on the payroll around the turn of the century, describe a later workforce drawn from southern and eastern Europe, and name the hazards plainly: toxic fumes, falls from height, and extreme heat. Joliet workers joined the national Steel Strike of 1919. A site account drawn from the preserve's signage adds the harder edge of that succession — that the later arrivals, with less English, held lower-paying, less-skilled jobs and were slower to join unions — and records early furnaces turning out about 50 tons of iron a day. Employment figures rise across the sources to roughly 4,000 around 1926 (see Gaps for the variance in every one of these numbers). No individual furnace crew, striker or casualty is named in the sources checked this run, and none is invented here. [1, 5, 6, 7]

The end came with the Depression. The operator's history is specific: the plant peaked during World War I, the stock market crash of 1929 ushered in the Great Depression and the works' ultimate end, U.S. Steel closed the facility in 1933, and the site was dismantled by the end of the decade. That closure date applies to this Collins Street / Columbia Street works. Broader summaries that carry Joliet steelmaking forward to a final cessation in the early 1980s are describing the successor U.S. Steel operation that had moved east from this location (see Gaps) — not furnaces still standing here. For fifty years the foundations lay under what the District's chief operating officer called a thicket of trees and shrubs, mostly invasive — a jungle of vegetation over stone and concrete. [1, 2, 5]

Recovery was a land transaction before it was a history project. In 1989 the Forest Preserve District entered an agreement with CorLands, the land arm of the regional conservation agency Openlands, to accept the parcel; CorLands had received the 45-acre site as a donation from USX Corporation. The District took ownership in 1991 — the same year the Joliet Steel Works district was entered in the National Register of Historic Places, on February 28, 1991, for its significance in industry — hired an industrial archaeologist to sift the rubble and map what survived, including the gas washing plant and furnace locations, and added about 7 acres on Columbia Street for parking in 1996–97. A federal transportation-enhancement grant in 1993 helped build the trail that also anchored a new I&M Canal Trail section north toward Lockport and Romeoville. On August 1, 1998, the site opened with architectural tours and a family bike ride, its interpretive signs telling the history of the people who worked there and what they made. It has stayed what it was made to be: an industrial archaeological site, read from a path. [1, 2, 3, 4]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The works stood at the confluence the operator names — I&M Canal, Des Plaines River and railroads — on the east side of downtown Joliet, in the corridor between Collins Street and Columbia Street, with the Old Joliet Prison ground in the same industrial landscape to the north (the prison is visible in period views across the works and is a separate dossier). The Register record gives the district address as 927 Collins St.; present-day visitor listings give Columbia Street addresses (see Gaps). The canal that fed the works' early transport is the same canal interpreted at Lockport, the previous dossier in this march. [1, 3, 4, 5]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Joliet Iron Works Historic Site, Forest Preserve District of Will County, Joliet — the 52-acre preserve itself: Columbia Street parking and shelter, the paved interpretive trail among the foundations, signage and the smartphone audio tour, and the connecting I&M Canal Trail. Open-air only; stay on the trail, climbing on the ruins is prohibited by the operator. There is no building to enter and no staffed interior gate; in bad weather or after sunset there is no version of this visit, and the audio tour can be taken remotely through the app the District names. [1, 2]

## 5. Public progression

Take the preserve as a slow out-and-back on the interpretive trail, in daylight, and read it in the order the plant worked. Start at the Columbia Street end: parking, shelter, and the first signage, with the canal corridor behind you — the transport that made this ground valuable before the railroads took over. Walk the foundations as a process, not a ruin field: where ore, limestone and coke arrived and were stocked, where the furnaces stood and were charged, where gas was washed and engines blew the blast, where iron was cast and steel rolled for rails. The audio tour, started from the QR signage at either end of the trail, carries dozens of historic photographs, so the visitor can hold the standing plant — furnaces towering over the yard — against the foundations at their feet. **Strongest story moment · Fifty tons to a steel city.** The signage figure for the early furnaces is about 50 tons of iron a day; within one generation this was a works of four furnaces and thousands of men, and within two it was a thicket no one entered. The foundations are the honest middle term: neither the towering plant of the photographs nor empty ground, but the footprint at full scale, walkable end to end. Finish on the I&M Canal Trail connection and look back: canal, river, rail and furnace foundations in one frame is the whole argument for why Joliet exists where it does. Reflection posture at the labor ground: the hazards named in the interpretation — fumes, heat, falls — were borne by named communities of immigrant workers even where this record keeps no individual names. [1, 2, 5, 7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Street-address conflict, printed in full: the National Register record for the Joliet Steel Works district gives 927 Collins St. Present-day visitor listings give 31 Columbia St. and 75 Columbia St., Joliet, IL 60432, east of Route 53 / Scott Street. The operator's preserve page checked this run prints no street address for the preserve at all — only the District headquarters address (17540 W. Laraway Road) in its footer. The trip gate therefore names the Columbia Street parking/trailhead descriptively and does not settle a house number. [1, 3, 4, 5]
- Trail-length conflict, three ways: the operator's current preserve page gives a 0.59-mile paved interpretive trail; the District's audio-tour announcement gives the interpretive trail as 1.25 miles round trip; older preserve summaries carried in review listings give a 1.5-mile paved trail. These may measure different routings (interpretive loop vs. round trip vs. an earlier layout), but no source checked this run reconciles them. The gate uses the operator's current 0.59-mile figure and promises nothing tighter. [1, 2, 5]
- Operating-span and closure conflict, resolved by scope and printed: the works' summary history gives initial operation 1869–1936 and states the steel works eventually became unprofitable with all operations ceased by the early 1980s. The operator's own history gives operation from the 1870s until the 1930s, U.S. Steel closure in 1933, and dismantling by the end of the decade, and separately records that USX had moved its main steel factory east from the Iron Works location to Collins Street. This dossier treats 1933/dismantled-by-1939 as the end of THIS works, and the 1980s date as the end of the successor operation elsewhere in Joliet; a reader who merges them will misdate the ruins by forty years. [1, 2, 5]
- Production-figure variance: an early-furnace figure of about 50 tons of iron per day (site signage, via the site account); a summary figure of four blast furnaces capable of up to 2,000 tons of pig iron daily; a site-signage report of 400 tons a day by 1910. The figures describe different dates and possibly different measures (one furnace vs. the works; iron vs. capacity), and no source checked this run builds the series. All are printed; the Story uses only the 50-ton early figure, attributed to signage. [5, 7]
- Workforce-figure variance: about 2,000 around the turn of the century; about 3,000 a day at peak in the site account; approximately 4,000 around 1926. No payroll series was retrieved this run; the Story keeps the operator's unnumbered "thousands" as its load-bearing claim and prints the numbered claims here. [1, 5, 6, 7]
- Preservation-system acreage variance: the operator's current preserve page puts the Des Plaines River preservation system at 2,600 acres; older preserve summaries put it at more than 2,400 acres. The preserve's own 52 acres is consistent across sources (45-acre donation plus the later Columbia Street acquisition). [1, 5]
- Second-largest claim held as attributed claim, not as a finding: the works is widely described as once the second largest steel mill in the United States. The description traces to secondary summaries; no production ranking table was retrieved this run, so the dossier does not rest any claim on the ranking. [5]
- Bessemer detail: the claim that Joliet was the ninth American plant to adopt the Bessemer converter comes from a University of Alabama at Birmingham report to the District (Dr. Jack Bergstresser's blast-furnace study), as relayed in a District-linked site history. The report itself was not retrieved this run; the Story claims early adoption only. [6]
- Individual-worker record gap: no crew list, strike roster, injury log or named worker testimony for this works was retrieved this run. The labor story is therefore told at the level the sources support — communities, numbers with their conflicts printed, and hazards — and no worker is invented to personify it. [1, 5, 6]
- Haunting claims: a paranormal-investigation page reports isolated claims of activity at the Iron Works. No documented event underlies the claims in any source checked this run; they are filed here as unsubstantiated lore, kept separate from the documented record, and they are not a reason to enter the preserve after its sunset closing — the gate is 8 a.m. to sunset and the ruins are off-limits to climbing at all hours. [1, 8]
