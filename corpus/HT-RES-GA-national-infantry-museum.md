## 2. Story
**National Infantry Museum and Soldier Center, Columbus · Research draft · Fred**

When the Army had to train thousands of people during World War II, it needed places for them to sleep, eat, collect supplies and see an orderly. The Army's historic properties plan calls the buildings “temporary wooden buildings,” grouped with tents and hutments erected for mobilization. The buildings stayed in use after that emergency. Decades later the Army, Georgia's preservation officer and the museum foundation had to decide which pieces of that physical record to save. Their agreement identifies seven eligible temporary structures. Six had already been moved from original locations in 1993. The seventh followed a separate relocation and documentation decision. In the agreement, only the administration and sleeping buildings have a specific association with George S. Patton's 1940–41 presence. The other buildings tell the lives of people unlikely to be named on a plaque. [2–4]

The museum's exhibit record describes rescuing representative structures from demolition and putting them together at the museum. The reopened installation involved foundation president Pete Jones, museum director Scott Daubert and Fort Moore public works director Kirk Ticknor, named in the museum's reopening account. The purpose of placing the buildings together was to make everyday relationships visible. A mess hall says how a group ate. A supply room shows how it acquired the material of everyday soldiering. Neither role depended on having a famous occupant. [2–5]

## 3. Then and now
The wood buildings were constructed for wartime use. Company Street is a later museum grouping of relocated and restored fabric. Its arrangements and interpretive fittings refer to wartime life without making every object original to one address. The Army's museum record describes a location outside the controlled installation. [2–4]

## 5. Public trip progression
Start with the Fort Benning and Columbus gallery when open, then use the information desk to check Company Street. If the tour operates, finish at **The room that fed a company**, reading the mess hall together with the barracks and supply building. On weekdays or in poor weather the public museum galleries carry the same daily-life interpretation. [1–4, 6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
- The museum's general description describes the 1990s rescue as a single story. The preservation agreement records separate phases and a different history for the seventh building. Original locations and the reconstructed ensemble remain distinct. [3–4]
- Army public account retrieval returned HTTP 429. The dated museum announcement, agreement and gallery records carry the preservation account. No name-level wartime bed assignment or mess roster was found. Current exterior opening depends on the signed program. [2–5, 7]
