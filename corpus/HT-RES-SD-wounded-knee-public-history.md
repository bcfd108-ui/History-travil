# Wounded Knee public history: remembrance belongs to the descendants

Division SD • OP01 • Local research draft • Checked November 18, 2026 Pacific

**Read from home; reflection only.** This unit offers no physical stop, grave location, approach, ceremony invitation or hunt. Publicly accessible testimony is not public access to sacred ground. Draft for Dash.


**Digital-only decision.** No unequivocal independent community-authorized visitor venue was established for this assignment. The usable entry is the published testimony and named Lakota voices below. No museum business listing or historic designation is adopted as permission to visit.

**Winter boundary:** no on-site winter opening, holiday access, accessible entrance, parking, public approach or visitor permission has been verified. None is implied. The online Smithsonian exhibition used here dates to **2011–2013** and must not be mistaken for a current physical exhibit or contemporary event schedule. [1]

**Authority before itinerary.** In his June 7, 2023 written testimony, **Frank Star Comes Out, then testifying as President of the Oglala Sioux Tribe**, explains that the Oglala Sioux and Cheyenne River Sioux Tribes reclaimed land for sacred remembrance and pledged against commercial development. He places descendants within decisions about its care and use. This is a Nation's named public statement, not a tourism operator's invitation. [2]

## 2. The story

**Start with mourning, not a spectacle.** In the National Museum of the American Indian's published quotation, **Birgil Kills Straight (Lakota)** explains how unresolved mourning affected individuals and the nation: **“they are in a period of mourning.”** The museum attributes the statement directly to him. The quotation was read; its original interview date and recording were not established, and no audio was heard. [1]

**Family history is present history.** At the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs hearing on **July 19, 2023**, **Wizipan Garriott** identifies himself both as an Interior official and a member of the Rosebud Sioux Tribe. He names his great-grandmother **Topaki Najinwin** as a survivor, and says she alone survived from her family. That passage was read in the published hearing, printed page 3. His family account should remain attributed to him, rather than being presented as an independently reconstructed family census. [3]

**A sovereign voice about what follows.** Star Comes Out's testimony frames reclamation as an act of responsibility to victims, survivors and future generations. Its governing purpose is healing and protection, not development of an attraction. [2]

**Original interpretive thread.** These accounts move the reader away from an ending imposed on Native history. They connect loss with surviving families and continuing authority. The strongest moment is hearing a descendant name a relative in a present-day public hearing: the past has a person speaking for it, not merely a marker standing over it.

### Read the two Nations separately

Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe Chairman Ryman LeBeau’s prepared testimony on July 19, 2023 quotes survivor Dewey Beard: “They murdered us.” LeBeau identifies Beard by his kinship and describes his losses. The quotation comes through the Chairman’s written testimony, not from a recording of Beard. LeBeau also describes the work to secure permanent protection for a portion of the massacre site. [6]

Frank Star Comes Out’s separately filed testimony explains the 2022 purchase as a joint act of the Oglala Sioux Tribe and Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe. The two officials spoke as representatives of distinct sovereign governments. Read each on its own terms, then return to the survivor’s words. The land measure records how the Nations sought to protect a place where their relatives were killed. [6,7]

## 3. Then / now

**1890:** the massacre occurred **December 29, 1890**, when the U.S. Seventh Cavalry killed Lakota people, including women and children. Star Comes Out's testimony describes the killing of hundreds and explains its continuing significance for his people. This dossier does not reproduce violence, deployment descriptions or tactical details. [2]

**1973 is a different event.** ICT's account of the occupation, published under **Kalle Benallie's** byline, includes **Madonna Thunder Hawk**, of the Oohenumpa band of the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe, discussing her experience and the closeness of ancestral memory. The occupation ran **February 27–May 8, 1973**. Its participants' experiences must not be merged with those of 1890 survivors. This source also recognizes internal community tensions rather than implying universal agreement. [4]

**2025 legislation, not a frozen 2023 proposal.** The **Wounded Knee Massacre Memorial and Sacred Site Act**, **Public Law 119-61**, was approved **December 19, 2025**. Its text directs Interior to complete specified actions within 365 days so the defined land is held by the two Tribes in restricted fee status. The enacted text was read; this draft has not verified an administrative completion receipt. The law does not establish a general visitor entitlement. [5]

## 4. What's still there

**Public documentary record:** a named Lakota elder's published statement, a tribal president's written testimony, descendant testimony recorded in a congressional hearing, Indigenous news reporting and enacted legislation. Each has a different author and purpose. [1–5]

**Continuing authority:** the enacted law identifies the Oglala Sioux Tribe and Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe and expressly addresses Oglala jurisdiction. Federal publication of a document is not a transfer of cultural authority to a museum, publisher or travel application. [5]

**No physical inventory offered.** This draft does not catalogue graves, memorial objects, ceremonial spaces, surviving structures or private land for visitor discovery. No archaeological feature is a candidate find.

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**Casualty figures differ in the sources.** The older Smithsonian page says Big Foot and 150 people; the 2023 Senate testimony refers to more than 300 Lakota people lost. These are source formulations, not reconciled counts. This unit uses “hundreds,” does not add figures together, and does not present an exact verified total. [1][3]

**Different kinds of primary evidence.** Star Comes Out's authored submission is a primary record of the Tribe's stated position in 2023, not an eyewitness document from 1890. Garriott's words are recorded descendant testimony, not his great-grandmother's own recorded interview. The Smithsonian excerpt is attributed speech within institutional interpretation.

**Federal and community sources remain distinct.** The 2025 statute proves enactment and specifies legal actions; it does not prove every implementation step was completed, resolve every historical dispute or authorize ceremonies and photography. [5]

**No live visitor status.** A future physical version requires explicit, current community authorization and an independently verified public venue. No application entry should silently promote this digital reflection to a visit. No contact was made to obtain such authorization.

LeBeau reports Beard’s estimate of 350 Lakota killed. Star Comes Out describes hundreds. The first is a reported survivor estimate and the second is broader language in separate testimony. Keep both beside the dossier’s existing figures and source attributions. The 2025 law addresses about 40 acres as a portion of the massacre site and gives the Secretary 365 days to take the listed actions. No final administrative transfer record or current visitor gate was inspected. Direct access to the PBS republication of the 1891 council report returned HTTP 502. It was excluded from the account.
