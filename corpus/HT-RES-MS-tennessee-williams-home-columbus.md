# The rectory that moved and Tennessee Williams’s first home

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP16*

## 2. Story

The gables at Main and Third once belonged to a church household several blocks away. The Reverend Walter E. Dakin served St. Paul’s Episcopal Church. His grandson Thomas Lanier Williams was born in Columbus March 26, 1911, and the Old Rectory was his first home. The nomination places him there for his first three years. It identifies the address where the rectory stood by the church and describes its rooms and timberwork. It supplies neither a birth-room record nor documentation of Williams writing here. [2][3]

Williams later made a different kind of home onstage. “The play is memory,” says Tom Wingfield in the opening of *The Glass Menagerie*. The words are Williams’s scripted dialogue, read in a printed classroom copy of the play. They belong to a character and establish a way of looking at the stage. Family experience can inform a play while the scenes, objects and lighting remain works of composition. Visitors can hold that distinction when they see a furnished room presented as part of the museum. [4]

A second story begins with the church’s need for the lot. The congregation sold the house to the Mississippi Department of Transportation on condition the building be moved. The nomination records the move on February 3, 1995, followed by rehabilitation to serve travelers. Its authors argued for listing on architectural merit, citing the surviving design and workmanship. The nomination thus preserves the case for moving the building and the cost of doing so in the same file. [2]

## 3. Then and now

The nomination places the rectory at 318 Second Street South at the time of its 1982 district listing. The state property record uses the Main Street address for the same moved structure. The original church relationship survives in the record. At the current corner, the surviving structure functions as the public welcome center. Staff interpret the household in a different setting. [1–3]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Public exterior.** The Main Street sidewalk affords a view of the front gables and ornament. Observe from the ordinary public walkway. [1][2]
- **Staffed interior.** Enter through the welcome center during staffed visitor access. Take in the rooms only on the route staff make public. The 1996 nomination documents the rehabilitated floor plan and ornament. Daily room availability can vary. [1][2]
- **Historical location.** The former church lot appears in the nomination. It has no stop or invitation in this dossier. [2]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

Begin on the downtown sidewalk with the twin cross-gables and the layered wood siding. Then take the public house tour if available. Read a room as an arrangement of household space and a later exhibit as the interpreter’s work. **Strongest story moment — The moved front door.** Look back at the façade from Main Street and picture the same frame on the church lot. The building that tells the story stayed together while the relationship between house and street changed. [1][2]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The operator dates the building to 1875. The nomination estimates 1876–1880 and the state property file gives circa 1875–1877. Use the late 1870s as the construction window. [1–3]
- The operator starts its rescue account in 1993 when demolition threatened. The nomination records a February 3, 1995 move. These dates refer to different stages. [1][2]
- The precise room of birth, names of the original builders, original ownership of individual display objects, and a family document fixing the precise departure date remain unverified. The classroom scan has an inconsistent year in its editorial heading. Use the original nomination for the birth year and the scan only for the playwright’s text. [2][4]
