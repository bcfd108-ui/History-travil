# Sioux Falls quarry and stonecutters: the labor inside a stone city

Division SD • OP01 • Local research draft • Checked November 18, 2026 Pacific

Document-led interpretation, with contemporary local-history evidence distinguished from worker testimony. Public museum candidate only; no quarry, river-edge, industrial or private-property route. Draft for Dash.

## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** A city's durable buildings can outlast the public memory of the people who made their stone usable. Start with the work, not a list of handsome facades.

**Contemporary evidence actually read:** **Dana Reed Bailey's 1899 History of Minnehaha County, South Dakota**, “Stone Industry,” printed pages **395–396**, describes quarrying, building stone and paving-block commerce. Bailey calls it a **“dinner-pail-brigade industry”** while urging renewed commercial effort after a period of reduced demand. The passage was read in a hosted text transcription, not from an original volume or page scan. [4]

**Perspective matters:** Bailey is a named contemporary local historian writing about the industry, not a stonecutter speaking about his own working day. His optimism about renewed prosperity and abundance belongs to his account. It does not establish wages, safety, worker satisfaction or present resources.

The county's later East Sioux Falls history connects the quartzite industry to a company town and identifies the Old Courthouse among local buildings made with this stone. This is institutional historical synthesis, not a named worker's diary. [5]

**Original interpretation:** The stone's permanence and the industry's unstable demand belong together. A surviving civic building is an entry into the labor story, not proof that the people producing its material shared equally in its prestige. Bailey's compact phrase makes workers briefly visible, while leaving their individual voices largely outside the frame.

No imagined stonecutter monologue has been used to close that gap. A first-person educational character is not automatically an authenticated interview, even when its name resembles a documented person.

### A stonecutter’s name

Bailey’s same county history names Alexander Reid as an ornamental stonecutter trained in Scotland. It places Reid in Rhode Island and Wisconsin before the Sioux Falls quarry work and records his later municipal service. The life sketch is a contemporary record of one skilled immigrant’s occupation. Its compressed chronology has a problem: the stated periods in Rhode Island and Wisconsin do not fit comfortably between the stated 1880 arrival in the United States and 1884 arrival in Sioux Falls. Keep the destinations and the reported dates as printed. No inspected payroll names Reid on the courthouse contract. [4,7]

The 1973 nomination describes the shell, tower, arch and quartzite of the former courthouse. These are ways of noticing where someone’s skill is still apparent. The masonry enclosing the doorway looks permanent. Every fitted stone represents work that had to take place before there could be a room behind it. Bailey’s enthusiasm for the industry and his account of Reid illuminate different scales of the same trade. Neither recovers a named cutter’s own words. [4,7–8]

## 3. Then / now

Bailey's text belongs to **1899**. Its present-tense statements are contemporary observations and advocacy from that year, not a description of today's quarry activity. He describes past shipments and a slowdown rather than providing an audited industrial census. [4]

Now, the public museum offers an independent cultural destination in a building associated with the stone industry. That does not mean any individual cutter's work on it has been identified. No quarry tour, stone-working demonstration, labor exhibit or accessible exterior photo station was verified.

The historical evidence can be encountered without returning to the extraction site. That is important both for safety and for avoiding the idea that industrial ruins are automatically public attractions.

## 4. What's still there

- **Published contemporary text:** Bailey's “Stone Industry” section is available in transcription. Exact visual layout and OCR accuracy require comparison against the original scan before extended quotation or facsimile use. [4]
- **Independent public institution:** the county lists the Old Courthouse Museum at its printed visitor address. Current operator entry exceptions remain a gate, not an assumed pass. [1]
- **Material connection:** county history names the museum building among quartzite structures. This supports a material-history connection, not a worker attribution or proof that every block came from one quarry. [5]
- **Unverified:** surviving tools linked to a named cutter, payrolls, worker correspondence, current displays and an authorized ground-level photographic target.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Start digitally:** read Bailey's labor phrase in its commercial context. Notice how a writer can acknowledge workers while making the industry's revival the center of the account.

**Conditional public museum stop:** once its exact-day gate is confirmed, use only the museum's open visitor areas. Do not follow a historical quarry address, enter a stone yard or walk to the river as a substitute for a missing exhibit.

**Deepen:** distinguish the visible material from the invisible supply chain. The museum building can ground the topic without a claim that the visitor has found a particular cutter's handiwork. Any current exhibit interpretation must be checked on its own terms.

**Strongest closing moment:** return to the gap between a durable stone building and the workers reduced to a collective phrase. Let that gap motivate careful archival research rather than invented biography. No hunt or test is needed to make the story land.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

1. **Primary-source branch met narrowly:** a contemporary named author's industry account was read. No stonecutter's firsthand diary, interview, wage book or original employment record was inspected.
2. **Transcription limit:** the online text has obvious broken-word artifacts. Only a short, clear phrase is quoted; no claim of visual page verification is made.
3. **Promotional tone:** Bailey's claims about prosperity, commercial value and abundance are not adopted as neutral technical or economic findings.
4. **Industry chronology:** later descriptions simplify decline in different ways. This draft does not treat reduced demand, a particular quarry closure and the end of all stone work as one date.
5. **Named-person leads:** John Elm and Loren H. Amundson's family-history paper were found as leads, but the full document could not be retrieved reliably. They are not used as verified firsthand testimony, and no family tragedy or medical diagnosis is reconstructed.
6. **Museum gate:** directory hours are corroboration, not a current operator guarantee. Winter holidays, weather and accessibility still need confirmation for a real visit.
7. **No permission by announcement:** proposed quarry improvements do not establish lawful visitor entry. They do not change this dossier's exclusions.

Bailey’s profile prints 1880 arrival in the United States, five years in Rhode Island, two more in Wisconsin, and 1884 arrival in Sioux Falls. The statements do not yield a consistent work timeline. The federal nomination gives 1889–1890 or 1890 for the courthouse, while the Society of Architectural Historians gives 1889–1893 and places completion in 1893. Separate the earlier documented building-date claim from the later reported completion date. Original payroll, contemporary reader access to the 1899 scan and a current confirmed public museum door remain gaps. [1–4,7–10]
