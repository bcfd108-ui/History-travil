# HT-RES-OH-serpent-mound — A quarter-mile snake in the grass no one can date
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A low earthen serpent, longer than four football fields end to end, uncoils along a bluff above Ohio Brush Creek in Adams County — an effigy with no burials and no artifacts inside it, saved from the plow in the 1880s by a Harvard archaeologist and Boston women, and still argued over today: built around 300 B.C. by the Adena, or around A.D. 1070 by Fort Ancient people? The operator's own page ends that argument the only honest way available — more work is needed. This ground is held sacred by many people today: a place to walk the path beside the serpent, not on it, and to leave the unanswered questions standing.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a walk around an effigy: Serpent Mound, a state memorial near Peebles, Ohio, preserving the Great Serpent Mound and three nearby burial mounds. The operator is the Ohio History Connection, whose own site page states the terms printed here. Museum and grounds may close in inclement weather. The site is closed on Christmas Eve and Day, New Year's Eve and Day, Easter Sunday, Independence Day, Thanksgiving Day, Black Friday, Memorial Day, and Labor Day, as the page lists them. Access is limited to the posted hours — Monday, holiday, and after-hours access is not permitted, and there is no trespassing when the gates are closed. ## 2. The story
The serpent lies on a high plateau above Ohio Brush Creek. The Historic American Landscapes Survey (HALS OH-9, Library of Congress) gives it as 1,348 feet long, five feet high, and twenty feet wide, built of earth along the ridge-top above a fork of the creek. At one end the body finishes in a triple-coiled tail; at the other, the head sits at the bluff's edge, jaws open around a hollow oval about 120 feet long. The ground itself is part of the story: the creek valley below is a circular, heavily faulted structure nearly five miles across — the Serpent Mound Disturbance, or cryptoexplosion structure — unlike the flat-layered bedrock around it. No source consulted states that the builders knew anything about that structure.

The effigy holds nothing that names its makers. The Ohio History Connection states that Putnam excavated Serpent Mound in the late nineteenth century and found no artifacts in the serpent that would let archaeologists assign it to a particular culture; the HALS record agrees there were no burials within the effigy, which is believed to have been created for ceremonial purposes. What stands near it does carry cultural names: three burial mounds are preserved beside the serpent — two Adena (800 B.C.–A.D. 100, in the Connection's dating) and one Fort Ancient (A.D. 1000–1650). Because two of the neighbors were Adena, later archaeologists attributed the serpent itself to the Adena, and that attribution held for most of a century. It is the first of two positions printed in §6; neither is adopted here.

The serpent entered the published record on **1848**, when Ephraim G. Squier and Edwin H. Davis reported it in *Ancient Monuments of the Mississippi Valley*, the first scholarly work published by the Smithsonian Institution. For decades afterward it sat on a working farm — the Lovett farm — exposed to plowing and washouts. Putnam visited in the mid-1880s, saw the damage worsening, and organized its rescue. A contemporary account in the *Ohio Archaeological and Historical Quarterly*, quoting Putnam's letter of **June 4, 1887**, records how the appeal moved through Alice C. Fletcher to a circle of Boston women, who raised the purchase money; the Peabody Museum's trustees agreed to hold the property, and about sixty acres were bought. The HALS record calls the result the first privately funded archaeological preserve in the United States. Putnam then worked three seasons excavating the effigy and the nearby burial mounds and restored them to original form.

In **1900** Harvard transferred the property to the Ohio State Archaeological and Historical Society — predecessor of today's Ohio History Connection — as a state memorial park. The deed, acknowledged on **October 8, 1900** and recorded at West Union on **November 22, 1900**, required the Society to provide for the mound's perpetual care, keep the park a free public park forever, and maintain a tablet recording the preservation and transfer. The tablet was erected in **January 1902** on the burial mound south of the tail. In **1908** the Society had the observation tower built. During the Great Depression the Works Progress Administration built seven structures at the park — barn, museum and concessions building, overlook, two comfort stations, caretaker house, and garage — praised in the HALS record as excellent examples of New Deal-era rustic architecture. The survey dates the museum's opening to **1967**. In **2009** the Arc of Appalachia partnered with the Connection as on-site manager; the Connection states it manages the site today.

The modern argument began with a trench. In **1991** — the year the Connection's page gives for the excavation; the study followed in 1996 — a team under Bradley T. Lepper reopened a trench first cut by Putnam and recovered charcoal from what they believed was an undisturbed part of the mound. Radiocarbon results put it at about A.D. 1070, which would place construction in the Fort Ancient period. In **2014** Edward Herrmann and colleagues published dates from sediment cores taken in 2011 and concluded the serpent was first built some 2,300 years ago, in the Early Woodland (Adena) period, around 320–300 B.C. Lepper and colleagues answered in print in **2018** and after. Section 6 prints both cases and resolves neither.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a serpent of piled earth on a farm, crossed by plowing and washing away a little more each year, known to scholarship from an 1848 survey but owned as farmland. **Now:** a state memorial park of about 54 acres (the HALS record's figure for the park created in 1900), held under a 1900 deed requiring its perpetual care as a public park, with a museum, a tower first built in 1908, and a paved path beside the serpent.

**Then:** a burial-ground landscape in which the serpent itself was the anonymous feature — no burials, no artifacts — dated by the company it kept: two Adena burial mounds and one Fort Ancient mound nearby. **Now:** the same three mounds preserved beside it, and the serpent's date carried by laboratories instead of neighbors — about A.D. 1070 on charcoal, about 300 B.C. on sediment — with the dispute in print and unresolved.

**Then:** Putnam's restored earthwork of the late 1880s, drawn for *The Century Magazine* in 1889–90 and tended as Harvard's preserve. **Now:** the same outline in grass above Brush Creek, photographed for the Historic American Landscapes Survey in 2016 (§7), and managed under a site plan the HALS record dates to 2015.

## 4. What's still there
- The serpent effigy itself: 1,348 feet long as the HALS survey measures it, seven undulations, triple-coiled tail, and open jaws around a hollow oval about 120 feet long, on the bluff above Brush Creek (HALS OH-9)
- The three nearby burial mounds — two Adena, one Fort Ancient — preserved with the effigy in the park (Ohio History Connection; HALS OH-9)
- The 1900 preservation tablet, erected in January 1902 on the burial mound south of the tail, as the deed required (Ohio Archaeological and Historical Quarterly, 1902)
- The observation tower overlooking the effigy, first built in 1908 (HALS OH-9)
- The New Deal-era buildings built by the Works Progress Administration in the 1930s — barn, museum and concessions building, overlook, comfort stations, caretaker house, garage (HALS OH-9)
- The Serpent Mound museum, opened in 1967 per the HALS record, with exhibits on the effigy's interpretations, construction, Adena culture, and local geology (HALS OH-9)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the museum:** Start with the operator's own presentation — the exhibits the HALS record describes, on what the effigy has been taken to mean, how it was built, and the geology under it
2. **Along the paved path, the tail first:** Walk beside the triple-coiled tail and follow the undulations along the ridge, staying off the earthwork. From ground level the serpent reads as low grassy swells; no single viewpoint gives you the whole animal.
3. **At the head and the oval:** Stand at the bluff edge where the jaws open around the hollow oval, above Ohio Brush Creek. What the oval is — egg, eye, sun, or something else — is an open question in the record (§6).
4. **At the three burial mounds:** The two Adena mounds and the one Fort Ancient mound are burial ground — the reason the serpent was dated to the Adena for a century. They are stops for standing quietly at a respectful distance, not for climbing.
5. **At the 1902 tablet and the tower:** Close with the preservation story — the tablet the 1900 deed required, and the 1908 tower, the one vantage from which the serpent's full course begins to resolve.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The builder attribution is a live scholarly dispute, printed here as one, with neither position adopted.

**Position one — Adena construction, about 300 B.C.** Herrmann and colleagues (2014), working from eighteen continuous cores taken in 2011, dated organic sediment from a buried soil horizon and from mound fill to roughly 2,300 years ago and concluded the serpent was initially constructed in the Early Woodland (Adena) period. Romain and colleagues (2017) restated that case and suggested the later, Fort Ancient-era dates reflect a refurbishment of an Adena original around A.D. 1100. The older Adena attribution rested on different evidence: the two Adena burial mounds near the effigy.

**Position two — Fort Ancient construction, about A.D. 1070.** Fletcher, Cameron, and Lepper's work, published in 1996 from the 1991 re-excavation of Putnam's trench, produced radiocarbon dates on charcoal from the mound fill of about A.D. 1070; the HALS record likewise carries "ca. 1070 — Initial Construction." Lepper and colleagues (2018, and a 2020 review) dispute the Adena case directly: the 2014 team dated bulk sediment from cores, not charcoal from an exposed profile, and comparative work by Henriksen and colleagues on Danish mounds showed core-derived dates running as much as 3,000 years older than a mound's true age. They add a cultural argument — effigy-mound building and serpent imagery are not otherwise known for the Adena, while the Fort Ancient culture built the Alligator Effigy Mound at Granville and used serpent imagery in its art. The Connection's page, reporting both results, concludes only that more work is needed. That is where this draft leaves it.

The serpent's length is also carried two ways. The HALS survey and modern Ohio History Connection materials give 1,348 feet. The National Register nomination (1974) and Mills's 1914 *Archaeological Atlas of Ohio* give 1,254 feet for the serpent proper, measured from the northern jaw to the tail tip following the undulations. The figures appear to measure different extents, but no source consulted says so in so many words, so both print unresolved. Heights vary likewise: five feet in the HALS description, four to five feet in the nomination.

The oval at the serpent's mouth has been read as an egg, the sun, the serpent's eye, a frog's body, and an altar or observation point; the HALS text frames its astronomical readings — including a head alignment toward a solstice sunset — as questions, not findings, and no source consulted presents the alignment claims as established. Whether the builders knew the valley was an impact structure is likewise unverified, and the crater's age is given across sources in a broad range (roughly 252 to 330 million years) not narrowed here.

Two smaller conflicts stand in the preservation record. The HALS history says the Works Progress Administration built the museum building in the 1930s, and also dates the museum's opening to 1967; whether the opening reused the New Deal building is not stated and is flagged as a gap. The year of National Historic Landmark designation is given as 1966 in federal records tied to the October 15, 1966 Register listing, while some secondary accounts give 1964; §10 rests on the Library of Congress record, and the discrepancy is left unresolved. The Department of the Interior placed the site on the U.S. Tentative List for World Heritage inscription on **January 30, 2008** (UNESCO ref. 5248); it remains a tentative-list property, not an inscribed site.

## 8. Candidate reflection
**Reflection stop only. No hunts, scoring, answer keys, hidden targets or reenactments.**

Candidate reflection: The serpent can be walked beside for its whole length without ever being seen whole — the animal exists complete only in survey plans and from the air, while the person on the ground meets one grassy ridge at a time. What does it mean that the builders made something no one standing on it could take in at once, and left nothing inside it to say why?

This ground is a ceremonial and burial landscape held sacred by many people today. Nothing in this reflection asks a visitor to walk on the effigy or the burial mounds, or to treat any part of the site as a puzzle to be solved.
