## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** Three names are not decorative initials. A shared Nation can preserve distinct histories, and a language booklet can show exactly whose memory carries one of them forward.

**Named MHA-published primary source:** MHA Nation's history-page digital library links *Hidatsa Ida nogaree*, a booklet credited to **Aaduu Marutash**, with a **2009** copyright date. The opening attribution page identifies the translator as **Aaduu Marutash (Lyle Gwin Sr.)** and describes the dialect as Hidatsa Proper, drawing on speech of **Pearl Burr Young Bear** and **Ailsa Anna Gwin**, and his own recollection. [4,5]

That is a concrete statement of transmission, not an anonymous “Native language” example. The attribution also acknowledges a model from the Manitoba First Nations Education Resource Center and **Helen Armstrong's** earlier Dakota compilation. These credits distinguish model, translator and remembered speakers rather than presenting the booklet as an unmediated ancient text. [5]

**Evidence boundary:** This is a named author's attribution and educational publication distributed by the Nation, not a personal-life interview or a recording listened to here. The original publisher/imprint beyond the cover's copyright line was not established. The Nation's present hosting should not silently be substituted for original authorship. No language lesson, pronunciation imitation or culturally sensitive passage is reproduced.

**Separate institutional history:** MHA Nation's history account emphasizes that Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara have their own histories and social traditions. It also uses **Sahnish** in discussing Arikara identity. Its relocation account describes damage to communities and agricultural livelihoods when families were displaced from bottomlands. [4] Those statements provide context without making harmed or submerged ground a visitor destination.

**Interpretive synthesis:** A family speaker's name is a form of historical precision. It locates knowledge in relationships without turning those relationships into places a visitor may enter. The museum can be a place to learn from that distinction, not to flatten three peoples into one generalized culture.
