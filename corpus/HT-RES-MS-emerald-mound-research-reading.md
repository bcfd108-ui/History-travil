# Emerald Mound, a Natchez center and living sacred ground

## 2. Story

Beginning around 1200 CE, ancestors of the Natchez reshaped a natural hill by carrying and placing earth until it supported a broad elevated platform. The finished earthwork covered about eight acres. Smaller earthen platforms above it helped organize civic and ceremonial life. The builders’ individual names are absent from the records consulted. The scale of the work testifies to generations of collective labor and political organization. [1,3]

## 3. Then and now

Mississippi’s Department of Archives and History places the move of the principal Natchez town to the Grand Village at about 1680. The Park Service gives a broader span of use extending to about 1730. Moving a political center and ending use of a place are different events. Neither date represents an end to the Natchez people or their relationship to this ground. [1–4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Two upper mounds remain visible on the main platform. Erosion changed their profiles. The Park Service stabilized and grassed the earthworks in the twentieth century. John L. Cotter published direct field observations in 1951. His evidence documents episodes of construction. A visitor looking at a modern photograph sees the additional effects of erosion and conservation. [1,3,5]

## 5. Public progression

Read the Park Service’s ‘Still Sacred’ panel alongside the Natchez Nation’s own description of its government today. The panel links the place to continuing gatherings. The Nation identifies its present seat in Oklahoma and describes leadership by two chiefs and four clan mothers. The Nation is the source here for its contemporary government. [2,4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

Archaeology can distinguish layers of construction. It cannot identify each builder or supply a complete account of community life. The site-specific interpretive panel is a Park Service publication. Direct, site-specific language from the Nation has not been established for this draft. The absence of an off-site anchor in this record is an evidence gap. [1–5]
