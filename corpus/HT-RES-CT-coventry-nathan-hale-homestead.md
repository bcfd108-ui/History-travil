# HT-RES-CT-coventry-nathan-hale-homestead — Coventry: the farm Nathan Hale grew up on
Division CT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

Nathan Hale grew up on this Coventry farm before he was a schoolteacher, a Continental Army officer, and the Revolution's most famous spy. He was captured and executed in September 1776, at twenty-one. The Georgian house that stands here dates to 1776 — built the year he died, on the property where he was born, but not the house he was born in.

Nathan Hale Homestead, 2299 South Street, Coventry, Tolland County, Connecticut

**Public access:** The grounds are open year-round, dawn to dusk, through the authorized museum entrance and designated visitor grounds; the operator is Connecticut Landmarks. Interior tours are seasonal and paid, and are separate from this entry. Preservation work during 2026 may restrict access to particular structures — confirm current conditions with Connecticut Landmarks before visiting.

## The story

The Hale family farm gives the civilian world behind the American Revolution a standing address. Nathan's father, Richard Hale, acquired the property in 1740, and the family worked it as a Connecticut farm. Six of Richard's eight sons served the Patriot cause.

Nathan was born on this property in 1755. He left for Yale, taught school, and joined the Continental Army as an officer. In September 1776 he volunteered to cross British lines to gather intelligence, was captured, and was executed as a spy. His death made him a lasting symbol of revolutionary sacrifice. The famous declaration attributed to him — that he regretted having only one life to give for his country — is presented here as an attributed statement, not a verified verbatim transcript; no stenographer stood at the gallows.

The surviving house dates to 1776. It is the family's house on the family's land, raised the year Nathan died — a memorial in timber whether or not anyone intended it as one. The surrounding agricultural landscape preserves the connection: this is the ground that produced him.

## Then / now

- 1740: Richard Hale acquires the property.
- 1755: Nathan Hale born on this farm.
- 1776: Nathan Hale executed, September 22; the surviving house dates to this year.
- Today: a Connecticut Landmarks museum; grounds open year-round, house tours seasonal.

## What's still there

The 1776 Georgian house with its symmetrical façade, and the farm landscape around it — fields, stone walls, and outbuildings carrying the agricultural setting the Hales worked.

## The stop, step by step

Enter through the authorized museum entrance at 2299 South Street. From an authorized visitor pathway, locate the symmetrical Georgian façade of the red house. The grounds are the visit — dawn to dusk, year-round. Interior tours run seasonally for a separate admission.

## Honest gaps & disagreements

The house is not Nathan Hale's birthplace building — he was born in an earlier house on this property, and this entry says so plainly. The "one life" line is attributed tradition, widely repeated and impossible to verify word-for-word. Six-of-eight-sons service is stated at the level the operator's record supports.
