## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** War did not suspend the ordinary need for a doctor, food or rest. The people who stayed had to keep a town going.

**Directly read firsthand testimony:** physician **Joe Perry Williams**, interviewed by **Jake Spidle in Roswell on August 17, 1983**, describes civilian practice during World War II. On printed transcript page **14**, he recalls roughly five doctors remaining to serve the community. His memorable line is **“we didn't get much sleep during all the war years.”** The published transcript, not audio, was read. This is retrospective personal evidence, not a verified staffing census. [4]

The source heading alternates **Joe Perry** and **Joseph P.**; retain the repository's Joe Perry naming without silently treating this as a different narrator. His civilian-care account provides the entry's anchor, not his unrelated recollections about military institutions. [4]

**A second civilian record, explicitly mediated:** the Farm & Ranch Heritage Museum's published abstract of **Emmett Knight Patterson's September 27, 2000 interview** describes a Roswell farmer/rancher who also worked at a bank. Its wartime account discusses rationing and scarce agricultural labor. This is an archivist's abstract, not a directly read verbatim transcript or a recording heard here. It widens the context but cannot supply Patterson quotations. [5]

**Original interpretation:** Put the exhausted provider beside the household trying to obtain ordinary goods. The story is not that everyone had the same wartime experience. It is that shortages were lived as time, waiting and work, not only as national statistics.

## 3. Then / now

The contrast is between a remembered civilian workload and the public record through which that memory can be examined now. It is not a then-and-now claim about an extant clinic or family residence.

An attractive museum building cannot stand in for the scene of testimony. The society identifies its house as completed in **1912**, now used for local historical interpretation; its archive contains photographs, manuscripts and other research material. That establishes an independent cultural anchor, not a connection between the house's occupants and the selected wartime accounts. [3]

Keep community members' unrepresented experiences visible as a gap. A physician's perspective and a farmer-employer's abstract do not speak for all patients, women doing unpaid care, agricultural laborers or incarcerated workers. No private patient records are needed to acknowledge that limitation.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- Williams is a later recollection; staffing, schedules and household impact were not checked against wartime rosters or diaries. The interviewer's rhetorical exaggeration is not a literal work-hour measure. [4]
- Patterson's public text is labeled an abstract. Its rationing examples are recollections summarized by the repository, not independently checked national policy. No operational camp details or farm locator are carried forward. [5]
- The society's archive-hours wording and its all-week appointment table are different access descriptions; do not merge them into guaranteed walk-in hours. [1,3]
- October 3 event access is not present winter access. [2]
- Search results for **Roswell, Georgia** and for people named Roswell were excluded. No Amy Crawford Georgia wartime account is mislocated in New Mexico.
- Patient/family firsthand testimony remains a desirable evidence gap; this draft meets the named civilian source requirement through a publicly interviewed provider, not through invented household dialogue.
