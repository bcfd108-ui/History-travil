## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** A neighborhood is more than its buildings. It is who fed you, who looked after the younger children, and whether you could move elsewhere without hostility.

**Directly read primary testimony:** **Leonard Waites**, interviewed by **Ellery Washington on June 20, 2023**, appears in Columbia's *I See My Light Shining: Oral Histories of Our Elders*. Printed pages **4–7** describe his Albuquerque childhood, siblings caring for one another, shared food and a later move into a predominantly white neighborhood. His concise description is **“We all took care of each other”**. This is published transcript evidence; audio was not heard. [4]

Waites recalls the family moving at night and learning from his father of a neighborhood meeting. That latter detail is remembered family reporting, not something established here from meeting minutes. His account supports the contrast between everyday belonging and exclusion without locating any household for visitors. [4]

**A distinct community-history voice:** **Dr. Natasha Howard**, author of *Black Placemaking*, explains her project as an effort to document South Broadway's Black community through oral histories and digital interpretation. She presents a racially mixed neighborhood with Black businesses, organizations and civic life, shaped partly by housing restrictions. This is Howard's published research and project rationale, not a substitute for Waites's different neighborhood memories. [5]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Lead with the social world people built, not just with what discrimination denied them. The record can hold food, affection and agency alongside exclusion. Neither one narrator nor one neighborhood stands for every Black Albuquerque experience.

## 3. Then / now

The comparison is between community memory and opportunities to encounter it responsibly today, not a photographic match between a historic house and its current residents.

The municipal museum's present exhibition addresses racist imagery and resistance through contextualized material. It is a broader interpretive setting, not proof that any displayed object belongs to the narrator or represents his childhood. [2] The published oral history supplies the specifically local voice; the digital placemaking project supplies another locally grounded framework. [4,5]

Visitors should be free to avoid or leave distressing imagery. The story remains intelligible through the published account without reproducing degrading objects as attention-grabbing graphics.
