## 2. The story
**The survivors spoke for themselves.** On **September 18, 1885**, Chinese laborers at Rock Springs submitted a memorial to **Huang Sih Chuen**, the Chinese consul at New York. Here “memorial” means a petition, not a statue. The published English translation identifies **559 signatories** and describes labor, lost homes and possessions, continuing fear and a demand for protection and redress. Its appeal includes **“the relief of the wounded.”** [5–6]

Read this first, before an account that treats Chinese workers merely as the object of somebody else's labor dispute. The petition explains that some survivors no longer wanted to resume mining but lacked the money and clothing to move elsewhere. Returning therefore cannot automatically be read as freely choosing reconciliation. It asks for punishment of perpetrators as well as relief and property compensation. [5–6]

The petition was preserved in **House Report 2044, 49th Congress, first session, 1886**, printed pages 28–31. The scan's final page states that signatures follow, but does not reproduce an autograph list there. This is a collective, translated survivor document, not a quotation assigned to an invented individual speaker. The translator and Chinese-language original were not established in this review. [6]

Named descendants challenge another kind of disappearance. In **Jenna McMurtry's August 6, 2025 report**, **Ricky and Grace Leo** describe learning late in life about the massacre and their family connections. Ricky says, **“We have to let them know that there are still descendants here.”** The report describes his great-uncle as a survivor and documents the couple's efforts to correct incomplete public interpretation. Their account is their own published perspective, not permission to visit a family house or a claim that all descendants agree about commemoration. [7]

**Interpretive conclusion:** the story lands hardest when the petition's demand to be heard meets the descendants' insistence that they are not gone. A monument can acknowledge a wrong, but it cannot substitute for listening to the people whose lives and family histories are at issue. Do not turn violence into a dramatic sequence, visitor simulation or contest.

## 3. Then / now
- **September 2, 1885:** the massacre; the survivor petition followed sixteen days later. Do not confuse the event date with the document date. [5–6]
- **1886:** congressional publication of the report containing the translated petition. Publication is not proof that all requested remedies occurred. [6]
- **1894:** the city dates the later museum building to this year. It is not a surviving 1885 Chinese home or the location of the petition's preparation. [1]
- **2025:** Requiem was unveiled on the 140th anniversary. This is a modern artwork and commemorative act, not an original likeness of a named 1885 victim. [4]
- **Today:** indoor museum admission, a commemorative park and archaeological/historical ground have different permissions. Their proximity does not join them into one open visitor area.

## 4. What's still there
**Public museum:** the former city hall houses local-history interpretation, a research library and rotating exhibits. Its general exhibit description does not verify the current wording of a massacre label or guarantee that the petition is on display. [1]

**Published primary record:** GovInfo provides the congressional report scan. The University of Nebraska teaching edition supplies a readable transcription and bibliographic context. Both were inspected; the final petition page was also visually checked. [5–6]

**Modern commemoration:** the city describes the memorial location and Requiem project. Hannah Romero's reported ceremony account confirms unveiling and names participants. Her account records that Ricky and Grace Leo helped gather descendants; it does not make a past event's archaeological access invitation permanent. [2–4]

**Excluded:** all rail rights-of-way, former private housing sites, family homes, church or school interiors/grounds without independent permission, excavation areas, burial locations and artifact collection. No following attackers' or survivors' paths. No grave search, names-as-clues or casualty counting task.

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Before travel:** read the petition's closing appeal and the descendant account. The primary text contains racial violence; readers may skip its graphic passages without losing this stop's meaning.
2. **At the admitted museum:** establish which interpretation is actually available. Keep the survivor petition as an identified outside source, not an imagined exhibit object.
3. **Reflect:** contrast the petitioners' requested remedies with what public memory chooses to emphasize. Distinguish a compensation claim, criminal accountability and later commemoration rather than treating them as equivalents.
4. **End with the living community:** return to Ricky Leo's statement about descendants. This is the strongest ending; it does not require an outdoor stop.

A future memorial visit must first resolve the public approach, winter condition and current restrictions. No route through adjacent historical ground is proposed. No reenactment, role assignment, scavenger hunt, score, stamp or completion reward. Quiet reading is sufficient.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- The survivor petition reports 28 killed and 15 wounded; the 2025 radio article says 28 killed and 14 injured. These are different source figures, not silently harmonized. No precise injury total is required for the visitor reflection. [5–7]
- The city Requiem page still asks readers to help make the project real; September 2025 reporting documents the unveiling. Treat the city page as project/background evidence, not proof that the sculpture remains unbuilt. [3–4]
- “Memorial” can mean the 1885 petition, an older memorial marker or the newer sculpture. Label which one is meant every time.
- A state tourism listing gives **211 B Street**, whereas the city operator gives **201 B Street**. Use the operator's 201 address; the discrepancy is not a reason to infer a second entrance. [1, 8]

- The printed translation is a mediated primary source. No autograph list or Chinese original was read, and no individual is claimed to have composed its exact wording. [6]
