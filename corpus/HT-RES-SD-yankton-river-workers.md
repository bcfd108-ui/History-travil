## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** River history remembers captains. A contemporary labor return shows a much larger workforce behind the vessel.

**Primary record actually read:** the **1880 Census, Volume 4 transportation report**, “Steam Navigation,” printed page **700**, Group X, identifies Upper Missouri traffic with headquarters at **Yankton and Sioux City**. Its grouping covers more than Yankton: Missouri, Platte and Kansas rivers and the Red River of the North, representing Nebraska, western Iowa and Dakota Territory. It reports **44 steamers**, **661 officers and crew**, and **386 roustabouts**, with a season averaging no more than seven months. These are regional categories and reported counts, not the population or payroll of Yankton alone. [3]

The original short phrase **“for a short season”** is the interpretive hinge. A boat could be visible for a season while the people depending on its work needed a living beyond it. That household implication is interpretation, not a fact proved about a named worker by this table.

**Evidence boundary:** this pass read the actual published report text, not merely a catalog description. It did not recover a named laborer's diary, employment contract or complete payroll. No worker's race, migration history, family situation, wage or working conditions are inferred from the category alone.

**Original interpretation:** The record makes labor visible while still flattening people into totals. Let that tension guide the encounter. A heroic captain story should not be allowed to erase the crew, and a census table should not be mistaken for having heard the crew speak.

## 3. Then / now

Then: Yankton was one headquarters within a larger river-transport system. The census's regional scope is essential to using it honestly; a city label cannot absorb all the workers it counted. [3]

Now: the museum publishes **A Dakota Story** as open. Its gallery descriptions specifically include Yankton's riverboat-port era, alongside other local histories. This establishes an independently relevant interpretive venue, not proof that the selected census page, an identified worker artifact or a particular vessel is displayed. [4]

A visitor need not stand on a hazardous shore or enter a working river facility to understand historical labor. The museum and primary document provide the context without converting the modern river into an operational exercise.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

1. **Counts:** all quoted workforce and vessel figures belong to the report's regional group, not solely Yankton. No local employment total is calculated from them.
2. **Primary but not personal:** this is a contemporary official compilation. No claim of a named worker's firsthand voice is made.
3. **OCR caution:** the downloaded text has visible recognition errors, including in the payroll line. No payroll total or calculated personal wage is used. A scan comparison is required before using those monetary figures.
4. **Museum weekend conflict:** “Closed Sundays” contradicts the November Sunday entry. Saturday is missing. This is unresolved, not silently corrected using a tourism listing.
5. **Dated closure:** November 22–26 is explicitly closed. Later holiday-event hours are not generalized to all winter dates. Weather closures were not checked for a particular arrival.
6. **Exhibit wording:** the dedicated page contains both an open banner and older future-tense copy. The homepage also announces the exhibit open; no individual object availability is inferred. [1,4]
7. **Living-history caution:** NPS publishes performed historical accounts. A modern portrayal is not an original river worker's recording. None is used as firsthand evidence here. [5]
8. **Perspective:** the river's Indigenous histories are not fully represented by a federal commercial-transport table. This draft does not substitute that source for sovereign accounts or turn colonial celebration into neutral narrator language.
