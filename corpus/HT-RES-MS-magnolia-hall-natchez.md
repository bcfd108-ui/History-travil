# Magnolia Hall — the brownstone finish the paint concealed

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP03 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Start at the Magnolia Hall gift shop, 215 South Pearl Street at Washington Street. The Natchez Garden Club lists public tours seven days a week at 11 a.m., noon, 1 p.m. and 2 p.m. Use the date-specific schedule during pilgrimage, holidays or weather changes. The operating tour areas are the visitor boundary. [1]

## 2. Story

The large house at Pearl and Washington was meant to look as if someone had cut its walls out of stone. Natchez cotton broker Thomas Henderson acquired the lot in 1858. Architectural historians identify J. Edwards Smith as the designer on the strength of a contemporary newspaper attribution. The work that achieved the effect was performed on brick: workers applied stucco, cut lines to suggest block joints, and finished the surface to look like brownstone. Two kinds of skilled work can be seen on the same front. The columns were shaped to give the portico its proportions; the finishers gave the wall its apparent material. [2–3]

In the restoration begun in 2016, the Natchez Garden Club sampled successive layers from the walls, shutters, doors, columns and galleries. Those samples supplied a palette independent of anyone’s memory of a familiar white house. The club records the result in its own project account. The interior also carried a record of later use: when the house was adapted as a school, walls changed, marble mantels were removed and suspended ceilings went in. A restored house can preserve evidence recovered after one generation covered over the work of another. [2–3]

## 3. Then and now

The house passed to banker Audley Britton in 1870. The Armstrong family later called it Magnolia Hall for flowers worked into the parlor plaster. The house served as a school before the family conveyed it for preservation. Henderson’s cotton wealth also depended on a society organized around enslavement. In a 2021 account the Historic Natchez Foundation identified this house as the first of its local house-museum exhibits devoted to enslaved people. The researchers described reconstructing people’s lives from estate lists written by those who claimed them as property. The names documented at other Natchez houses cannot fill the gaps at this one. [3–4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Operator-open gift shop and guided tour rooms. The exterior finish can be read on the approach. The attached service wing helps explain the relationship between the kitchen and the main house. Its presence is not a separate permission to enter. [1–3]

## 5. Public progression

Begin outside with a single apparent joint between blocks. On the tour, compare that deliberately constructed surface with the floral plaster that supplied the later name. **Strongest story moment:** the restorers found the early color buried in the building itself. It was the samples that led them back to brown. [2–3]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

1858 is the land acquisition and probable start of construction. The sale price gives some support to a family tradition that an earlier house was moved down the block, but does not document the move. The underlying newspaper issue naming Smith and original estate records naming individual household workers still need inspection. The exact rooms and exhibits open on a particular day are operator-controlled. [1,3–4]
