# HT-RES-MI-fort-holmes — A blockhouse on the island's highest point, built by one army and named by the other
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

The highest ground on Mackinac Island holds a small fort with two names and no original buildings. The British built it during the War of 1812 as Fort George, a blockhouse and stockade watching over the undefended rear of Fort Mackinac below; the Americans, taking it back in 1815, renamed it Fort Holmes for the major killed in their failed assault the year before, held it for two more summers, and let it fall down. What a visitor climbs to today is the park's reconstruction of that redoubt — earthen walls, a cedar-log face, a gate, and a two-story blockhouse — rebuilt on the 1817 Army plans and standing where the original stood, open ground with a view over the Straits.

## 1. Trip gate
The operator's terms are the lightest in this catalog: "The Fort Holmes grounds are always open. The blockhouse is open May-October" (Mackinac State Historic Parks, Historic Sites and Monuments page, checked November 26, 2026). Mackinac Island State Park itself is open 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, and Fort Holmes sits inside it. For the blockhouse interior the operator's seasonal hours page gives the 2027 season as May 7 to October 24, 10:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. The operator's published admission rates ticket Fort Mackinac and the island's other staffed attractions; no admission charge for Fort Holmes appears in the operator's rates checked, and none is printed here — the fort is unstaffed ground.

The island shapes the visit before the fort does. Mackinac Island is reachable only by ferry or airplane, and its historic sites on foot or by bike (MSHP). Fort Holmes stands on the island's highest point, above and behind Fort Mackinac; the climb is the trip. Nothing in the operator sources checked promises more than the ground, the reconstruction, the marker, and the view — a visitor expecting a staffed fort with programming is expecting a different site, and this entry says so plainly.

## 2. The story
Mackinac Island changed hands at the start of the War of 1812 without a fight at the harbor fort: a British and Canadian force landed on the island's north side on the night of July 16–17, 1812, and Fort Mackinac, its garrison unaware war had been declared, surrendered (MSHP). The island's height was the fort's weakness — the high ground behind and above it was undefended — and the British remedied it with a small fort of their own on the island's highest point: a blockhouse and stockade named Fort George, for the king. The Michigan historical marker at the site (Michigan Historical Commission, Registered Site No. 79) gives its construction to 1812 and calls it "the bulwark of British defenses in 1814 when the American attack was repulsed." The park's published histories date the redoubt's building to 1814; the marker's date and the histories' date are printed against each other in §6 and not reconciled here.

On August 4, 1814, the Americans came back by water to retake the island and were beaten in the island's interior, in the battle fought on the farm fields below the heights. Major Andrew Holmes was killed in that assault (marker; MSHP). The high fort was never itself stormed — its work was to exist above Fort Mackinac's rear, and in 1814 the British kept both forts.

Peace gave the post its second name. Under the Treaty of Ghent the island returned to the United States, and American troops came back on July 18, 1815 (MSHP, "1815: The Americans Return to Mackinac Island"). By at least early September 1815 the post on the height had been renamed Fort Holmes, for the major killed the year before. What the Americans found, as their commander Lt. Col. Chambers recorded it, was a horseshoe-shaped fort ringed by a defensive ditch: earthen walls topped with a fraise — sharpened logs laid horizontally — a two-story blockhouse he called "tolerable strong" and capable of mounting two 12-pound cannons, unfinished gun platforms meant for eleven artillery pieces, two bombproof shelters just outside the gate, and an advanced battery for two 24-pound guns overlooking Fort Mackinac and the harbor below (MSHP). The Americans finished the platforms and armed the place properly: two brass 12-pound guns, two howitzers, a long 24-pound gun, and two 6-pound guns, with ammunition in one bombproof and provisions in the other.

The fort's American life was a summer routine, and the park's own history of it is the best part of the record. From the summer of 1815 a guard detachment of ten men marched up the hill every morning to relieve the night watch. In 1816 a "Piquet Guard" — an officer, a sergeant, a corporal, and twenty privates — joined them each night. Their orders in an attack were a small fort's whole doctrine: fire a cannon to warn Fort Mackinac below, then defend the post with muskets and its artillery until reinforcements arrived (MSHP, "Small Fort Plays Big Role in Mackinac History"). Every autumn the soldiers closed the post and went down to winter quarters. In 1817 Major Charles Gratiot visited and recorded the fort's design in detailed plans — blockhouse, walls, and gun platforms clearly drawn (MSHP). That survey is the document the fort's later life hangs on. The Americans closed Fort Holmes for the last time in December 1817 and never reopened it; the artillery and equipment were removed, and the post was left to fall into ruin (MSHP).

For more than a century the height held only the earthworks and the memory. The state marked the ground (marker, Registered Site No. 79), and the island around it became a National Historic Landmark district on October 15, 1966 (NRIS record 66000397). The fort itself came back in the 1930s: the Works Progress Administration rebuilt it from the 1817 engineer's drawing and elevation — a new blockhouse, the ditch re-dug, the embankment rebuilt, the outward walls lined with cedar logs (Living New Deal, carrying the park superintendent's account). The park's own pages give the reconstruction's completion as both 1934 and 1936 (see §6). The rebuilt fort decayed in its turn; by the time the park's historian Brian Leigh Dunnigan published his report *Fort Holmes* (Mackinac State Historic Parks, Reports in Mackinac History and Archeology No. 10, 1984), most of the reconstructed buildings had again disappeared, leaving the earthen walls.

The present fort is the newest layer. After three earlier reconstructions, the park rebuilt Fort Holmes again, completing the current version in 2015 (MSHP Historic Sites page; MSHP blog). The half-million-dollar project was funded by $250,000 in private donations and a Michigan State Legislature appropriation, and its plans were based on the 1817 United States Army drawings of the original fort, prepared with architect Rick Neuman (Mackinac Associates newsletter, 2015). The state marker at the gate carries the essential honesty of the place in its last line: "The present blockhouse is not the original building."

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a horseshoe of earthworks on the island's roof — ditch, earthen walls topped with sharpened logs, a two-story blockhouse, gun platforms for eleven pieces, bombproofs outside the gate — held by a guard of ten men by day and a piquet by night, closed every winter, abandoned for good in December 1817. **Now:** the same outline rebuilt in 2015 from the 1817 plans, its grounds always open, its blockhouse open May to October, standing over an island state park that is open around the clock.

**Then:** the fort's purpose was the view: it watched the harbor, the Straits, and the rear of Fort Mackinac, and its one shot in anger was to be a cannon fired as a warning down the hill. **Now:** the view is the visitor's — the reconstruction is the island's high overlook, and the Straits, Round Island and its light, and the town and harbor below are what the ground gives. The photograph in §7 was taken looking back at the walls; the fort's own outlook is the other direction.

**Then:** Fort George, a British post named for a king, taken over and renamed by the enemy for a dead American major. **Now:** Fort Holmes on every map and marker — the American name outlasted the British fort, and the marker tells both halves of the story on one plate.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish:
- The reconstructed redoubt of 2015: earthen walls with a cedar-log face, the surrounding ditch's ground, a gate, and a two-story wooden blockhouse, built on the 1817 U.S. Army drawings (MSHP; Mackinac Associates newsletter, 2015)
- Six exhibit panels inside the blockhouse (Mackinac Associates newsletter, 2015); the blockhouse is open May–October, grounds always open (MSHP)
- The Michigan historical marker for Fort Holmes, Registered Site No. 79, at the approach to the gate — its text names the British building of Fort George, the 1814 repulse, the renaming for Maj. Holmes, and states that the present blockhouse is not the original building (marker, read from the marker itself)
- The island's highest point itself, and the outlook over the harbor and the Straits that was the post's reason for standing there (MSHP)
- Fort Mackinac below, the fort this one was built to protect — a separate, ticketed attraction of the same operator (MSHP)

Not there: any fabric of 1812–1817. The marker says so and the park says so ("replaced by the current version in 2015"); this entry does not let the reconstruction's weathering blur the point.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the progression follows the ground as the operator keeps it:

1. **At the marker by the gate:** Read the state's plate first. It gives the whole two-name story in eight sentences and ends with the warning that governs everything after: the present blockhouse is not the original building.
2. **Through the gate, on the wall walk:** The horseshoe as Chambers described it — ditch outside, earthen wall, the fraise line the 1817 drawing shows. The 2016 photograph in §7 is this approach: wall, gate, and blockhouse behind.
3. **In the blockhouse:** The two-story post of the guard — ten men by day, the piquet by night — with the six exhibit panels the 2015 rebuild installed. If the blockhouse is closed (it is open May–October only), the ground still carries the story; the operator opens the grounds always.
4. **At the outlook:** End where the fort's purpose was: looking down on Fort Mackinac's rear, the harbor, and the Straits. The advanced battery stood to command exactly this view (MSHP); a warning cannon fired here was the whole garrison's first duty in an attack.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The building year of the original fort prints two ways, and this entry does not settle it. The Michigan historical marker at the site states: "Here in 1812, on the island's highest point, a blockhouse and stockade were built by the British and named Fort George." The park's published histories, following Dunnigan's 1984 park report, date the fort's construction to 1814. Section 2 therefore carries the building as the British occupation's work and gives 1814 as the year of the battle in which the fort served as the defense's bulwark — the point both accounts agree on — while the construction year stands in conflict: 1812 on the state's marker, 1814 in the park's histories.

The first reconstruction's year also prints two ways, inside the operator's own publications. The Historic Sites and Monuments page states that a reconstruction "carried out by the Works Progress Administration" was completed in 1934. The park's own blog illustrates the standing reconstruction with a caption reading "1936-2015," its Associates newsletter treats the 1930s fort as the predecessor demolished for the 2015 project, and the Living New Deal's account of the park superintendent's report dates the WPA rebuilding to the summer of 1936. The discrepancy is noted, not resolved: the WPA itself was created in 1935, which sits awkwardly with the 1934 date — but awkwardness is not a correction, and both years stand as printed.

Two counts differ quietly. The park's blog says "three earlier reconstructions" preceded the 2015 version; its Historic Sites page describes the 1930s reconstruction as the one the 2015 version "replaced." The sources checked name the 1930s WPA fort and the 2015 fort, and no others — whether the intermediate rebuilds the blog counts are documented anywhere in the operator sources, this pass did not establish. And the fort's height prints in feet in secondary sources in at least three forms (about 320 feet above Lake Huron; 890 feet above sea level; 325 feet above sea level in a tourism source that appears to misplace the figure) — no elevation figure appears in the primary sources checked, so none prints in §2: the park's own statement, that this is the island's highest point, is what the entry carries. Finally, the 1817 survey's survival is carried by the operator's own accounts; the original drawing set itself was not read for this entry, and the claim is attributed to the park wherever it appears above.
