## 2. The story
The useful starting point is labor, not a superlative about the building. The 1972 nomination names **Jacob Tueller and his three sons**, Swiss masons who moved to Paris and spent four years on the work beginning in 1884. It names **James Nye** in connection with the pulpit and choir-ceiling woodwork. Its account describes local timber and stone carried by animal teams, with some winter transport across the lake. These are retrospective architectural documentation, not payroll sheets or the workers' own testimony. [5]

The register identifies **Joseph Don Carlos Young** and **Thomas G. Lowe** among the building's architects/design-and-construction figures. [6] A name on the register does not turn one designer into the whole workforce.  The interpretive question is how individual work became an assembly room: material extraction, hauling, shaping, finishing and gathering, rather than a story of a building appearing through one man's vision.

A named contemporary voice survives in **George Q. Cannon's journal**, published by the Church Historian's Press. Under September 15, 1889, he records: **“at President Woodruff’s request I offered the dedicatory prayer.”** Cannon then describes speeches, afternoon testimonies and an evening Sunday School gathering. The record brings the finished hall into use as a whole day's religious and educational meeting, rather than merely an architectural opening. [7] This is a transcription of a contemporary journal, not an audio recording; no recorded laborer's voice was found.

Context must not turn settler construction into a claim that the region had been empty. ISHS's regional account identifies Bear Lake Shoshoni territory extending through this area. [8] The Northwestern Band of the Shoshone Nation's own published history describes dispossession and coercive change in the wider region. [9] Those accounts are not a documented encounter at this particular construction site; no Paris-specific Tribal quotation or agreement has been established here. Do not invent one, or collapse different Shoshone communities into a single interchangeable voice.

## 3. Then / now
Then: a regional congregation undertook a lengthy building project and used the completed meetinghouse for collective worship and instruction. Now: the Church retains a historic religious building with public interpretation when tours operate. The old nomination's condition assessment is historical, not a present-day conservation certificate. [5]

The narrative comparison is labor made visible in masonry and woodwork, then community activity filling the room. It is not a reenactment of lake hauling. Historic ice travel supplies no present safe route. Neither a modern visit nor an archive photograph demonstrates that every visible fitting is original.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Check the admission gate before making this an interior anchor.** For a summer visit, verify that day's actual public-tour availability. For a winter visit, maintain an exterior-only/defer alternative; a seasonal directory is not a booking.
2. **Start with material and hands.** From a legal public position, consider the stonework against the named Tueller family and the hauling account. Observe without touching or collecting.
3. **If a guide admits visitors, move from shell to assembly.** Stay on the authorized main-floor route. Look for how the room supports a gathered audience rather than chasing inaccessible architectural details.
4. **Finish with the strongest story moment: a day's life entering the new room.** Read Cannon's short dated observation and consider the transition from years of construction to people speaking, listening and teaching. This is an interpretive sequence, not a claim to stand where Cannon stood.

Onsite question, only during an offered tour: **Which surviving detail can you connect to a named local maker, and what record establishes that attribution?** An answer should be logged with its source rather than automatically promoted to fact. Do not interrupt worship or request demonstrations of sacred practice.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- The current Church page places settlement in the late 1860s; ISHS's nomination gives **September 26, 1863**. Record the discrepancy instead of silently blending it. [1,5]
- Quarry distance differs: the Church gives about **24 miles southeast**, while the nomination gives **18 miles**. A straight-line distance versus historic route might explain it, but that explanation is unverified. No quarry locator is assigned. [1,5]
- Cannon's manuscript transcription retains “Sept. 14th [15th]” under the editor's September 15 heading. The bracketed correction matters; do not manufacture two dedications. [7]
- The operator rejects the familiar shipbuilding explanation of the roof. Do not repeat that attractive story as established engineering history. [1]
- No contemporary workers' ledger, individual donation register or women's firsthand account was retrieved. The named masons are documented, but the full labor community is not reconstructed.
- Current November interior access, winter sidewalk condition, photography permission and private-event exclusions remain unverified. No church service has been designated a tourism opening.
