## 2. The story
**Cinnamon Spear Kills First** publishes her own family connection in *My Relatives at Sand Creek* (November 29, 2014). She names **Ellen and Arthur Brady** as survivors and explains why writing this history matters to her as their descendant. Her essay remembers learning with elders in 2004 and challenges the suggestion that the past should simply be forgotten. Her concise declaration is the opening: **“It’s time for us to tell our stories.”** This is a descendant's self-published account, not a transcript recorded in 1864. Graphic passages and physical directions are deliberately not reproduced. [5]

A separate Tribal publication puts remembrance in community hands. Rosemary Stephens's May 15, 2021 *Cheyenne & Arapaho Tribal Tribune* report describes **Fred Mosqueda and Chester Whiteman** gathering names through oral history, research and interviews for a monument. Mosqueda explicitly cautions that its list cannot include everyone who died. That makes the work of remembering visible without turning a list into a final casualty census. The monument discussed is in Oklahoma, not a feature promised at the Colorado visitor center. [6]

In a published 2023 interview, **Kaden Walksnice**, who identifies himself with the Northern Cheyenne Nation, describes reconnecting with identity and supporting younger generations. **Cinnamon Kills First** connects remembrance to land, resources and a voice in present-day policy. These are their stated experiences and priorities, not an invitation to participate in ceremony or a statement on behalf of every Tribal citizen. The edited text was read; no video/audio review is claimed. [7]

Contemporary sovereignty is not merely an epilogue. The Tribal Tribune's September 2022 legislative report documents a vote concerning **Chris Tallbear's** park-representative nomination and discussion of engagement across multiple federal sites. This is evidence of a government conducting its own business, not a current 2026 officeholder directory. [8]

## 3. Then / now
- **November 29, 1864:** the massacre of Cheyenne and Arapaho people anchors these accounts. This draft does not reproduce tactics, graphic descriptions or competing numerical totals. [5–7]
- **2014:** a descendant publishes family memory and her own reason for telling it. [5]
- **2021:** Tribal reporting documents community remembrance and the limits of the surviving name record. [6]
- **2022–2023:** legislative reporting and published descendant interviews demonstrate ongoing government and community concerns. [7,8]

In a 2023 Smithsonian article by Dennis Zotigh, Reggie Wassana, identified there as Cheyenne and Arapaho governor, states that treaty promises remain unfulfilled and accountability remains necessary. **Excluded from this product:** burial locations, archaeological evidence locations, ceremonial spaces, family property, operational areas and historical escape/attack routes. No ceremonial practice is adapted as entertainment, and no graves or sensitive cultural places become stops.
