# Texas City 1947 — the people who came to help
Research draft · Texas OP03 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

### People near the ship

On April 16, 1947, the French freighter Grandcamp caught fire and exploded at the port. A second ship, the High Flyer, exploded early on April 17. The Rice University disaster collection was created or maintained by survivor Robert M. Morris. His collection preserves reports, images and statements given in the years immediately following the disaster. The records describe a workplace disaster whose reach extended into ships, streets, factories, homes and families [2, 4–5].

### The missing fire truck

In the Texas City World War II Oral History Project, Clarence Wood recalled his father with a fire truck beside the ship. At 28:40 in the published transcript he explained, “they did not find him or his fire truck at all.” This is a son’s later recollection in a city-published transcript. Wood was speaking about what happened to his father and revisiting the city for the fiftieth anniversary, rather than presenting an eyewitness report of the waterfront itself [6].

### Two firefighting groups appear in the record

Cheryl Lauersdorf Ross’s published account interviews Clifford C. Reed Sr., who responded as part of the Republic Oil Refinery team and survived. He recalled trying to help people at the offices after the blast. The city volunteers and the refinery crew have separate histories. Do not apply Reed’s survival to the city crew at the ship. The visitor gallery retains films, photographs and artifacts that help give the collective loss names and places [1, 5–6].

## 3 · Then / now

**Historic place.** The industrial docks and surrounding affected workplaces and neighborhoods. Port property and operational industries are outside the visit [2, 4–5].

**Lawful physical anchor.** Texas City Museum, 409 6th Street North. Drive-up with short public frontage approach and ordinary museum entrance. Use the designated museum gallery during posted hours. The museum occupies the former J.C. Penney building; its address serves as an interpretation anchor [1].

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Begin in the museum’s disaster gallery, with a quiet reading of which port workers and responders were at risk [1–2, 5].
2. Read the city’s oral-history transcript and identify its speaker and relationship to the event [6].
3. **Strongest story moment — The fire truck that never came home.** In the public gallery read Wood’s brief transcript line about his father and the truck, then leave a quiet moment for those whose families did not recover them [1, 6].

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Rice’s collection summary gives at least 576 killed and 4,000 injured [4]. Texas City’s published historical summary gives 581 killed and over 5,000 injured [7]. Both figures are reported with their source and counting language. No independent case-level reconciliation was completed. The state-marker print page returned a TimeoutError [9]. The city library’s separate holiday calendar and standard hours cannot establish a specific museum reopening on Nov. 28, so check with the museum if planning that day [1, 3].
