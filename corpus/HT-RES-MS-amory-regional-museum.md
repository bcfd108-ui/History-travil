# Amory’s former sanitarium and the work that made a railroad town

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP17*

## 2. Story

The City of Amory traces the town’s layout to an 1887 railroad survey. Look at that statement together with the workers that a company magazine named four decades later. The September 1928 *Frisco Employes’ Magazine* lists “Engineer E. JACOBS, fireman SPARKS” on a Birmingham–Amory extra with engine 4141. The item goes on to tally the work in ton-miles and the fuel in tons of coal. It is a railroad company record measuring performance, with the two employees’ names attached. It preserves who was responsible on one run but offers neither worker’s own account. The same issue groups runs by subdivision and shows Amory among several routes worked by distinct crews. [4][5]

In 1916 E. D. Gilmore financed the Gilmore Sanitarium. The state designation and the city’s museum history agree that the current building housed it. The hospital successor opened in 1961. The old facility then housed nursing-home residents for several years. “Sanitarium” is the historic institution’s title in both sources, and the cited files do not establish a diagnosis, a facility for involuntary confinement, or a precise patient admission rule. No patient is identified without a verified public first-person record. The train records and the medical institution describe different kinds of work in the same growing town. Neither is a substitute for the other’s records. [1][3][5]

Before the museum opened, the Bicentennial Committee took up a city museum as part of Project ’76. The City says the Gilmore Foundation deed went to the Amory Arts Council in February 1976 and the museum dedication followed June 27. A later gallery annex opened in 2007. That sequence shows where to draw a line between the 1916 facility, the adaptive reuse and the added display space. The civic effort preserved a local building and turned it into a place that could hold the town’s material evidence. [1][3]

## 3. Then and now

The state record places the sanitarium and the current museum in the same building. Today it holds exhibits chosen by city staff. The civic railroad story is interpreted here, although historical locomotive runs and railroad parcels are different places. The city’s mention of the annex means some of today’s visitor rooms occupy added fabric. [1–5]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Staffed museum interior.** The municipal museum and the designated public entrance at 801 South Third Street. Confirm live hours. Stay in marked public display areas. [1][2]
- **Documented original building.** The former Gilmore Sanitarium’s massing and surviving exterior fabric form the historical shell of the museum. Its current floor plan reflects later uses. [1][3]
- **Off-site historical evidence.** Railroad corridors and historic hospital records have no visitor pin or route here. The archives provide research sources remotely. [3–5]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

Start at the museum’s signed public entrance when open. Ask what exhibits are on view and read any railway or local household object with its label. **Strongest story moment — Two names in a column of numbers.** Bring the Jacobs and Sparks entry into the museum and read it as a trace of the labor ordinarily compressed into the words “railroad town.” [1][2][5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The city places nursing-home use in 1961–1965, while the historic property record describes approximately five years. Dates agree in general and the precise opening or closure date for the nursing home is unverified. [1][3]
- The city copy for the museum does not establish a present-day recurring admission schedule. Its related PDF on town history was available via its indexed text, but the PDF open request returned HTTP 403. The magazine’s readable indexed PDF yielded the named crew entry; a later retrieval attempt returned 404. [1][4][5]
- Clinical authority, who was treated, staffing records, original patient voices and details of the 1916 opening remain open. No claims of a particular treatment program, confinement policy, or rail collection object are made from those missing records.
