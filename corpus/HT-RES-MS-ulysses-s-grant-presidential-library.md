# The Grant gallery and the writing of a public memory

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP16*

## 2. Story

At Mount McGregor, New York, Ulysses S. Grant wrote the preface to his Civil War memoirs. He dated it July 1, 1885. He traced his turn toward writing to a fall that temporarily confined him to the house, then the financial losses that shrank his family’s income. He described a “sincere desire to avoid doing injustice” to people on either side of the war and acknowledged how even leaving someone unmentioned could be an injustice. This is Grant’s own explanation in a digital transcription of the printed memoir. His account of wanting to be fair is a way into testing what his own narrative could see. [4]

Mark Twain, the pen name of Samuel Clemens, helped change the publishing arrangement. The National Park Service reconstructs a moment when Grant was close to accepting an agreement with the *Century* company. Grant’s son Fred persuaded him to wait long enough to hear a proposal from Twain. The writer’s family, a competing publisher, author-publisher Twain and the people who would prepare and distribute a book were all engaged in turning a dying man’s writing into a printed work. The photograph here records Grant at the writing, with a board or book resting on his lap. Grant’s words and the publishing arrangement concern different kinds of authorship and work. [4–6]

The public museum is in Mississippi because this is where another group of people took on the work of maintaining the record. The Grant Association says the collection moved from Southern Illinois University Carbondale to Mississippi State in December 2008 after historian John F. Marszalek succeeded the late John Y. Simon as editor and executive director. It dates the written agreement January 30, 2009. The Association retained ownership of the papers and identifies Mississippi State as responsible for care and research access. Visitors can see a curated public gallery without entering the archive or arranging research access. [1][7][8]

## 3. Then and now

Grant’s last writing location was in New York. The Starkville gallery stands on the campus that later accepted custody of an association’s collection. The Association’s papers, the Library of Congress’s separate manuscript holdings and the museum’s selected public exhibits have distinct scopes. What is on view can change. [1][4][6–9]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Public interior.** Fourth-floor Grant museum gallery, an operator-designated self-guided visit. Use marked public circulation during gallery hours. [1][2]
- **Public approach.** Normal campus visitor parking and the public library entrance according to the operator’s directions. The direct GPS route to Hardy Road ends at a gate. [1][3]
- **Research reading only.** Archive stacks and the consultation or reading room have their own rules and carry no visitor progression here. The separate Abraham Lincoln gallery listed by the university is a separate public exhibit. [1][2][8]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

Use the university’s designated visitor approach and go to the fourth-floor public museum. Read the labels to identify a printed reproduction, a period object, or an original document when one is shown. **Strongest story moment — The manuscript becomes a public account.** Read Grant’s one-line statement about doing justice and consider the difference between the writer’s purpose, the edition made from his pages and the evidence a curator can put before you. The interpretation works with a label or facsimile when an original is off display. [1][4][8]
