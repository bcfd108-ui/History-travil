## 2. Story

**Castillo de San Marcos National Monument · Reflection · Research draft · Fred**

In 1671 the castle was still a supply problem. The NPS historic resource study records blacksmiths and carpenters preparing tools for quarrying and shipping stone. On October 2, 1672 Governor Manuel de Cendoya and other officials broke ground. Workers cut shell limestone from Anastasia Island, and lime kilns turned oyster shells into mortar ingredients. The work drew on Indigenous workers, enslaved African Americans, convicts and skilled labor from Cuba. The resource study describes the workforce collectively. It does not establish the terms under which each individual labored. [4]

In 1675 the colony’s governor called the fort “not surpassed by any of those with greater character,” in a translation printed by NPS. The words come from a governor reporting to the Crown. The workers who quarried, carried and fitted the stone do not speak through that report. The first phase was complete by 1695. When Carolina governor James Moore attacked in 1702, Spanish authorities sheltered roughly 1,500 people in the fort, according to NPS. The courtyard was larger then. The NPS account names Governor José de Zúñiga y Zerda, and credits an Indigenous woman from the Chacato community with warning Spanish officials. It gives no personal name for her. [4–6]

The same walls acquired different meanings under successive governments. Britain acquired Florida in 1763, and St. Augustine remained loyal to Britain during the American Revolution. Under the United States this was Fort Marion. In 1875 the Army confined people from several Native nations there. NPS distinguishes 72 prisoners and two accompanying family members. Among the people held were Mochi and Southern Cheyenne artist Howling Wolf. The Allen Memorial Art Museum links him to a body of ledger drawings. Many depict experiences before imprisonment. His work lets readers encounter a named artist’s view of his own world, beyond the Army’s confinement record. [1, 7–8]

## 3. Then and now

The work still visible in the historic fort includes the shell-stone bastions and the arrangement of enclosed rooms around the court. The NPS resource study distinguishes the reconstructed drawbridge from earlier fabric and records filling the east side of the moat in 1842. The museum on King Street is a separate indoor interpretation stop. Its Florida At War exhibit concerns British St. Augustine in the Revolution. It does not establish the story of the 1702 siege or the Fort Marion incarcerations by itself. Use the open records below for those episodes. [1, 3–4, 7]

## 5. Public progression

At the independent museum, start with the posted exhibit on British-period St. Augustine. Think about how a change in sovereignty turned a Spanish fortress into a British post. End the visit indoors with **One Door, Many Governments**. Use the stereograph and the recorded section of the sally port to connect that change in government to the later use of the fort as a military prison. [1, 4, 7, 9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- HABS describes an overall construction span of 1671–1756. The NPS study uses 1672–1756 for the fort and differentiates tool preparation in 1671, ground-breaking in 1672, the first phase and later improvements. [4, 10]
- The LOC historical stereograph summary loosely treats Fort Marion as a later name. National Park Service records already associate that name with the American period before this exposure range. Treat the LOC item title as its historical caption and the catalog’s date as a range. [7, 9]
- The NPS confinement account counts multiple Native nations. Oberlin’s biography describes Howling Wolf’s removal with seventy other Southern Cheyenne. Its artist account is not the source for the total or nations represented. The fort’s reconstruction dates and the city’s indoor gallery hours describe different grounds. [2–3, 7–8]
- The standard web request to the LOC stereograph detail returned a retrieval error. The Library’s same record opened through a second retriever, and the repository JPEG was inspected. A scan of the governor’s 1675 original, named construction workers and two independent agreeing coordinates remain open. [4–5, 9]
