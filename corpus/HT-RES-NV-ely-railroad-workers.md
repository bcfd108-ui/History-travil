## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** a railway can be remembered through the labor that made other labor count. A pay record connects the spectacular machine to an ordinary person's household. Preservation later required another kind of service work, often less visible than the equipment it kept in public memory. These are questions for interpretation, not claims that every worker experienced the same history.

The museum's Secretary's Office account identifies **Pearl Brown**, hired in **1924** as bookkeeper and paymaster, working for more than two decades. It identifies her desk and original payroll records as exhibited material. This is institutionally attributed worker evidence, **not a retrieved first-person statement by Brown**. No quotation has been invented for her, and her personnel record or an item-level ledger accession has not been retrieved. [5]

A separate named first-person voice is **Rae Nell O'Donnell**, whose essay is bylined to her on the railway museum's website, posted September 5, 2007. She describes growing up in Ely and later volunteering with her husband Jerry after the railroad's museum transition. Her stated work included concessions, ticket selling and narration over eighteen years. Her phrase **“helped to sell tickets”** anchors this dossier in actual public-facing labor rather than an imagined paid railway career. Her account also recalls a gendered limit on her earlier ambitions. It is a retrospective personal essay, hosted by the institution, not a payroll record or recorded oral-history transcript. [6]

The essay's later excursion narrative is **not** used as a visitor program, instruction, access precedent or operational explanation. Nor is its relative eighteen-year interval converted into an unsupported exact hiring date.

**One-minute hook, original:** Who made sure the people behind the trains were counted? Begin with the paymaster, then listen to a woman whose later work helped the town tell its railway story to visitors.
