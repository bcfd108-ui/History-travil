# Graue Mill and Museum — a working Salt Creek gristmill and an Underground Railroad station
HT-RES-IL-graue-mill-and-museum · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The mill begins with a burned sawmill site and materials gathered close at hand. Frederick Graue — Friedrich in Germany — emigrated in the 1840s and settled at Fullersburg, the Salt Creek settlement now divided between Oak Brook and Hinsdale. In 1849 he and William Asche bought land along the creek where a sawmill had stood. What they raised over the next years was built largely from the place itself: limestone for the basement walls quarried near Lemont, bricks formed from clay dug on the Graue farm and fired in kilns near the mill, floor and beam timbers of white oak cut along the Illinois & Michigan Canal, and four grinding stones, each a ton, imported from France because no local stone would do that work. The mill opened in April 1852 in the operator's account, and local farmers brought wheat, corn, and other grain to it as a matter of routine. A working mill of that kind was not one business among many; it was where a farm neighborhood turned crops into food, and it made the miller a man every household dealt with. [1, 3]

The same building carried a second, quieter use. The Graue family were Pietist Germans who opposed slavery, and the mill is repeatedly identified — in the village history tradition, the county's Underground Railroad accounts, and the museum's own basement interpretation — as a station where freedom seekers moving north toward Canada were sheltered, in the cellar of the mill. The museum's basement exhibit, recorded in visitor accounts as "Graue Mill and the Road to Freedom," presents that history with photographs, documents including slave-sale advertisements, and period objects. Secrecy was the operating condition of that work, and the surviving record is correspondingly thin: no passenger list, no count, and no named freedom seeker tied to a specific night at this mill was retrieved in the sources checked this run. What is documented is the building, the family, their stated opposition to slavery, and a local tradition consistent enough to be carried by the museum itself. The dossier keeps those two layers separate: the sheltering is the site's received and interpreted history; the names of the sheltered do not survive here. [3, 5, 6, 8]

The mill outlived its second use by decades. The Graue family operated it for 70 years by the operator's count — three generations in other summaries, which give roughly 60 years — until modern milling methods made a small water-powered gristmill obsolete and the building was abandoned, windows and door boarded, exterior brick crumbling, machinery stopped. The Forest Preserve District acquired it in 1933. In 1934 the district chose restoration to mid-1800s operation, and men of Civilian Conservation Corps Camp V-1668, based at Fullersburg Woods, replaced windows and door, cleaned the interior, and built a new millrace; periodic work since the 1940s has kept it in working order. The mill joined the National Register of Historic Places in May 1975, and in 1981 the American Society of Mechanical Engineers recognized it as an Illinois Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark. It still grinds: the district's season programs demonstrate milling, spinning, and weaving, with interpreter demonstrations on Saturdays and Sundays at 2 p.m. in the 2025 season release. [1, 2]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The mill stands on its original Salt Creek ground at Fullersburg — earlier called Brush Hill — on the east side of DuPage County. The settlement around it has largely been absorbed into Oak Brook and Hinsdale suburbs; the mill, the nearby Frederick Graue House, the Ben Fuller House (moved to its present preserve site in 1981 after a 1980 fire), and Fullersburg Cemetery are the surviving public fabric of the village. The dam and millrace on Salt Creek are part of the historic works: the first dam here was a brush-and-log structure of 1837 serving the earlier sawmill, the Graue-era dam was rebuilt in stone and log and later in crib-and-plank form, a turbine supplemented the undershot wheel from 1868, the crib dam washed out in 1916, and the present concrete-and-stone dam dates to the restoration era after a 16-year gap with no dam, per the Fullersburg Historic Foundation's dam timetable, which also records a current proposal to remove the dam — printed here as an unresolved live question for the site, not a settled fact. [5, 6, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Graue Mill and Museum, 3800 York Road, Oak Brook, IL — staffed museum interior in season, in Fullersburg Woods Forest Preserve, reached by the preserve's public parking at York and Spring roads. Outside the mill's season, the lawful visit is the preserve itself — trails, the mill exterior, the creek and dam, and the 0.6-mile Salt Creek walk to the Fullersburg Woods Nature Education Center — on preserve hours only. No interior is promised outside the published season. [1, 2]

## 5. Public progression

Start in season, at opening if possible, and start outside: read the creek, the dam and the millrace first, so the wheel inside is understood as a machine driven by this water and not as a display. Enter and take the milling floors in working order — grain in, stones, meal out — with the interpreter demonstrations as the center of the visit; the district frames its programs around life between 1850 and 1860. Then go down to the basement interpretation last and take it slowly, in reflection posture: the same cellar that served a mill's ordinary storage is the space the site's tradition names as shelter for people moving toward Canada, and the exhibit's documents include the sale advertisements of the system they were escaping. **Strongest story moment · One building, two kinds of work.** By day the mill ground the neighborhood's grain in the open; by the site's documented tradition, its cellar hid the neighborhood's most dangerous secret — and both kinds of work depended on the same trusted miller. Finish on the Salt Creek trail to the nature education center, which returns the visit to the water that powered everything. Out of season, do the exterior progression only — preserve, creek, dam, mill walls — and treat the interior as a return trip, not a disappointment. [1, 2, 5, 8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Season vs. checked date: the operator's standing hours end in mid-November and this check falls on Nov. 21. No 2026 season dates were published in the pages checked this run. The trip gate is therefore confirm-before-driving for any visit until the district publishes the next opening date. [1, 2]
- Phone conflict printed in full: the operator's mill page lists 630-451-3430 as the mill contact; the district's 2025 season-opening release directs details calls to 630-850-8112. Both are district numbers; neither page reconciles the other. [1, 2]
- Operating-span conflict: the operator page says the Graue family operated the mill for 70 years; widely repeated summaries say three generations for approximately 60 years (some give "nearly 60," others "almost seventy"). This dossier prints the operator's 70 in the Story and records the variance here rather than averaging it. [1, 3]
- Opening-month variance: the operator page and the Oak Brook visitor page give April 1852; other summaries give summer 1852, and construction-start dates divide between 1849 (purchase and start, operator) and a five-year build in the marker tradition. The purchase in 1849 and an 1852 opening are common to all accounts checked. [1, 3, 5]
- Arrival-date variance for Graue himself: the operator page places his emigration in the 1840s and settlement at Fullersburg without a year; local accounts give 1842, and the Fullersburg dam timetable gives an 1838 arrival working at the Torode sawmill. No account checked this run resolves the three. [1, 6]
- "Only mill" framing varies by source: the 2025 district release calls Graue Mill the only operating waterwheel gristmill in the Chicago area; district-derived listings widen that to northeastern Illinois, and other summaries to Illinois with one other operating water-powered gristmill (Franklin Creek) as the exception. The narrowest, operator-published form — Chicago area — is the one this dossier relies on. [2]
- Underground Railroad documentation gap, printed plainly: the operator page checked this run tells the mill's economic and restoration history and does not mention the Underground Railroad at all. The station tradition is carried by the museum's basement exhibit as described by visitors, by DuPage County Underground Railroad accounts naming Graue a conductor who hid freedom seekers in the cellar, and by the Fullersburg tradition. The often-repeated "one of three authenticated Illinois stops" formulation appears in secondary summaries without, in the pages checked this run, a named authenticating record. No NPS Network to Freedom listing page for this site was retrieved this run. The dossier therefore presents the sheltering as the site's interpreted tradition, strongly and consistently held, and does not upgrade it to a separately verified federal listing. [3, 5, 8]
- Tunnel lore, labeled lore: a 1929 newspaper account, repeated by the Fullersburg Foundation, held that the Fuller inn and tavern were connected by an underground tunnel forming a link in the Underground Railroad. No physical tunnel evidence is presented in the sources checked this run. Filed as lore — a period telling about the settlement — not as documented fabric, and no tunnel is promised on the visit. [5, 6]
- Lincoln-visit tradition: local accounts state Abraham Lincoln visited the mill area en route between Chicago and Springfield. No primary record of a specific visit was retrieved this run; the claim is carried here as local tradition only. [5]
- Dam-removal proposal: the Fullersburg Historic Foundation records a plan being formulated for presentation to the Forest Preserve District to remove the Graue Mill Dam, and the Foundation's stated opposition to removal. The proposal's current status was not established in the pages checked this run. [7]
