# Washburne House State Historic Site — the Galena library where Grant learned he was president
HT-RES-IL-washburne-house · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Elihu Benjamin Washburne arrived in Galena in 1840, a Maine-born, Harvard Law–trained Whig lawyer who had worked as a printer's apprentice and assistant editor before the law. The site's own history explains why Galena: it was a thriving lead-mining commercial center, its residents were, in Washburne's word, a "litigious set," and most of its lawyers were Democrats, so a Whig attorney could find work. He opened an office in the good part of town, boarded for several years with the leading Whig lawyer Charles S. Hempstead, and there met Hempstead's niece Adele Gratiot, whom he married in 1845. Adele was Galena-born, daughter of Henry Gratiot, a lead prospector and Indian agent whose standing with the Winnebago was such that, on the site's account, they visited Adele in his honor for more than twenty years after his death in 1836; she spoke fluent French, which later served the household in Paris. The Washburnes had seven children. [2, 5, 6]

The house came with the rise. Construction began in 1843 in the Greek Revival style — a two-story brick home in the state site's description, its temple-like front portico carried on large pillars — and it was completed in 1845 on the operator's account, enlarged sixteen years later, in 1859 and 1860, to its present size, with Washburne following the enlargement work in numerous letters while he was away in Washington. Inside, the plan sorted the household plainly: a hallway separating public and private rooms at the front, guests received in the parlor, the sitting room reserved for family reading, study and sewing, and the servants' work confined to the rear, where cooking and laundry were done. The Register record carries the house under both architecture and politics/government, with significant years 1844 and 1882 — the build and the end of the Washburne tenure. [1, 2, 4]

Washburne's public career is the reason the house matters beyond its architecture, and the federal biographies agree on its spine while disagreeing on details printed in Gaps. Elected to Congress in 1853 — first as a Whig, then as a founder, with his three brothers, of the new Republican Party in four different states — he served until 1869, chaired the Commerce and Appropriations committees, and earned the name "Watchdog of the Treasury" for fiscal discipline. He was an anti-slavery man, a leader of the Radical Republicans, a member of the Joint Committee on Reconstruction that drafted the Fourteenth Amendment, and, in the National Park Service's summary, a proponent of legal racial equality who believed large Southern plantations should be divided to provide compensatory property for freedmen. He backed Abraham Lincoln's failed 1858 Senate bid, then devoted himself, in his own phrase, his "whole soul and energies" to Lincoln's 1860 presidential campaign, advising Lincoln before and after the election. And he promoted the other Galenian: Ulysses S. Grant, whose military and political career Washburne sponsored from inside Congress, serving as a conduit between Lincoln and Grant through the war. [2, 5, 6, 7]

The house's single most famous hour belongs to that sponsorship. On the evening of November 3, 1868, Grant followed the election returns in the Washburne library and there learned he had been elected President. The room is still interpreted on the tour, and it is the correct center of the visit: a congressman's library in a lead town functioning, for one night, as the place where a general became a president-elect, because the man who owned the library had spent a decade making both men's careers possible. [1, 2]

What followed was stranger than the library night. Grant made Washburne Secretary of State in March 1869 — an appointment the State Department's own historian describes as personal and temporary, a means to honor Washburne while Grant restructured his cabinet after the Senate balked at another appointment. Washburne's commission issued March 5, 1869; he resigned March 10 and remained until his successor Hamilton Fish arrived March 16, on the understanding he would make no appointments or key decisions. Grant then appointed him Minister to France, where he served until 1877 through the Franco-Prussian War, the siege of Paris, and the Paris Commune. The Congressional biography records that on the declaration of war he protected the Paris legations of the German states under the American flag, remained in Paris during the siege, and was the only foreign minister who continued at his post during the Commune, protecting not only Germans but all the foreigners left by their own ministers. The State Department's account adds the harder detail: he facilitated foreigners' departure, visited imprisoned Americans, identified American bodies, and tried, as intermediary between the Vatican and the Commune, to exchange the imprisoned archbishop of Paris, who was executed on May 23 as the Commune fell. [6, 7]

Washburne never really came home to the house. Recalled when Grant's second term ended in 1877, he found Galena, in the words the site preserves, "terribly dead and dull," and settled in Chicago instead. The family occupied the house until 1882; only one other family, the Sheehans, owned it afterward, until the State of Illinois purchased it in 1968. Washburne served as president of the Chicago Historical Society from 1884 until his death in October 1887 — the day itself is in conflict across the sources, printed in Gaps — and he is buried beside Adele in Galena's Greenwood Cemetery. The house was added to the National Register of Historic Places on July 5, 1973. Its exterior is restored to its appearance as sketched in *Harper's New Monthly Magazine* in 1866; inside, the first floor interprets an entry hall, parlor, sitting room, library, dining room and kitchen with adjoining pantries, furnished to depict the lifestyle of a well-to-do midwestern professional, with two bedrooms shown upstairs. [1, 2, 4, 6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The house stands at 908 Third Street in Galena, Jo Daviess County, south of the Galena River and a short walk east of U.S. Route 20, inside the Galena Historic District. It is administered as part of the U.S. Grant Historic Sites with the Grant Home and the Old Market House — the congressman's house and the general's house kept, correctly, as one story told at two addresses. The Register record gives the same 908 3rd St. address; there is no address conflict for this property. [1, 2, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Washburne House State Historic Site, 908 Third St., Galena, IL 61036 — entered only on the Questers' guided tour, in season (May–October, Fridays and Saturdays), checking the Friday hours by phone at (815) 777-3310 before driving because the operator's own listings disagree. Outside the season — including on the checked date — the lawful visit is the exterior from the public sidewalk and street, the rear parking lot, and this reading. The Grant Home, a separate state site a few blocks away, has its own gate and is not part of this anchor, though the two belong to the same administered group. [1, 2, 3]

## 5. Public progression

In season, time the Galena day around this house, not the other way round — two short windows a week is the whole offer. Call (815) 777-3310 first and settle which Friday hours govern. Begin on Third Street and read the exterior as the operator restored it: the 1866 *Harper's* Greek Revival front, portico and pillars announcing a lead-town lawyer's success in the classical language every ambitious American town spoke in the 1840s. Take the tour in the house's own order. On the first floor, hold the plan's sorting in mind — parlor for the public man, sitting room for the family, servants' work kept at the rear — and let the library carry the visit: on November 3, 1868, the man who had sponsored Grant from a Galena law office watched the returns with him here, and the clerk of 1860 became president-elect in his congressman's front rooms. Hear Adele's half of the house properly: a Galena-born, French-speaking Gratiot, visited by the Winnebago in her father's honor, whose background the state site itself uses to open the region's French and Native American cultures — the house is not only a Washington story. Go up to the two bedrooms if you can climb, or take the photo board below if you cannot, and register the ending the tour may hurry past: the family left in 1882, Washburne chose Chicago over a Galena he called dead and dull, and the house survived because one other family kept it until the state bought it in 1968. **Strongest story moment · The sponsor's library.** Grant has the monument and the gift house on the hill; Washburne has this library, where the election was actually learned. Galena's Civil War was run, in no small part, from these rooms — Lincoln advised, Grant promoted, a cabinet post held for eleven days, a legation held through a siege. Leave by Third Street in site hours; out of season, the lawful visit is the sidewalk, the portico, and this reading — nothing more. [1, 2, 5, 6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Friday-hours conflict, printed in full: the Grant Home sites pages (both the Washburne House page and the sites index page, checked this run) state the house is open every Friday, 10:00 a.m.–1:00 p.m., and Saturday, 10:00 a.m.–1:00 p.m., May through October. Current venue listings derived from the site's business profile state Friday 1:00 p.m.–4:00 p.m. and Saturday 10:00 a.m.–1:00 p.m. The Saturday window agrees everywhere; the Friday window does not. The gate therefore requires a phone confirmation at (815) 777-3310 and asserts no Friday hours as settled. [2, 3]
- Season status on the checked date: all operator listings agree the house opens May through October only. On November 21, 2026 it is closed for the season; the dossier's in-season tour gate is prospective and its present-tense anchor is exterior-only. No source checked this run gives a 2027 reopening date.
- Build-date variance, printed in full: the state site states the original portion was built beginning in 1843, with additions in 1859 and 1860. The operator history states the house was constructed in 1843, enlarged sixteen years later, and elsewhere that it was completed in 1845. The Historic American Buildings Survey account, as reproduced in *Old Illinois Houses*, gives a build range of about 1845–1850. The Register record lists 1844 as a significant year. The dossier uses "begun 1843, completed 1845, enlarged 1859–60" as the working form and prints the HABS range here rather than merging it. [1, 2, 4, 8]
- Story-count variance: the state site describes a two-story brick home; other descriptions of the same house call it a 1½-story Greek Revival house. The dossier follows the state site's two-story form for the main block and notes the variance here; no source checked this run reconciles the two by roof or dormer description.
- Death-date conflict, printed in full: the operator history states Washburne served as president of the Chicago Historical Society from 1884 until his death on October 22, 1887. The Congressional biography and the State Department biography both give October 23, 1887, in Chicago, with interment in Greenwood Cemetery, Galena. The dossier prints both days and asserts neither. [2, 6, 7]
- Congressional-start errors in secondary federal summaries, flagged rather than followed: the National Park Service biography states Washburne "was elected to Congress in 1858 as a Whig" and spells his surname "Washburn" in two sentences. The Congressional biography, the State Department biography, the state site and the operator history all give election in 1852 and service from March 4, 1853, first as a Whig. The dossier follows 1853 and prints the NPS form here as a known error in a federal source. [1, 2, 5, 6, 7]
- Secretary of State tenure, reconciled only in part: the State Department gives commission issued March 5, 1869, resignation March 10, service until successor Hamilton Fish arrived March 16. The NPS biography says he "remained at State for 12 days." March 5 to March 16 is eleven to twelve days depending on counting; the dossier states the dated State Department sequence and does not adopt the NPS day-count. The Congressional biography's explanation — that he resigned to accept the France mission — and the State Department's — that the appointment was a temporary honor while the cabinet was restructured, with an agreement to make no appointments or key decisions — are both printed in the Story as the complementary accounts they appear to be; no source checked this run ranks them. [5, 6, 7]
- Tour-length variance: the state site's main text gives 25-minute guided tours; its own accessibility text gives approximately 30 minutes. The dossier prints both.
- Admission gap: no page checked this run prints an admission charge or a free-admission statement for the Washburne House tours. The gap is left blank rather than filled from the Grant Home's separate $5/$3 listing, which belongs to a different property's gate.
