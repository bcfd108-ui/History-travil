## 2. The story
The story begins inside a family rather than underground in a mine.

**Joyce Chinn**, born in Butte in 1952, described connections to both the Hum and Chinn families in her **January 23, 1998** oral history. Her published, interviewee-reviewed transcript remembers a bilingual childhood. On printed page 13, she recalls school authorities telling her mother to stop speaking Chinese at home when her older sister entered school. She describes the resulting loss of language and the pressure of having to explain it later. Her plain question, **“Do you speak Chinese?”**, carries that double bind. This is her recollection, not a recovered school directive or a claim about every Chinese American child. [4–5]

The public transcript contains corrections and interviewee-requested restrictions. Those boundaries are part of the record. Only released material is used; no attempt was made to recover omitted passages. [4–5]

The **Mai Wah Society's Chinn Family account** provides the business setting. **Chin Hin Doon** was a Wah Chong Tai Company partner by 1894; his son **Chin Yee Fong**, later **Albert Chinn**, attended Butte schools and worked as an assistant bookkeeper around 1910. The family operated the mercantile and noodle parlor, rented space to other businesses, and also lived in the buildings. This was commerce, domestic life and community infrastructure, not merely a service appendage to mining. [6]

A second locally published story names organized resistance. The Society describes **Hum Fay, Quon Loy and Dr. Huie Pock** opposing the 1896 boycott through petitions and litigation, obtaining an injunction. The underlying affidavit is identified as a National Archives record, but it was not retrieved here; no supposed courtroom speech or direct affidavit quotation is supplied. Winning an injunction did not establish that losses were repaired or hostility disappeared. [7]

**Interpretive conclusion:** pair the family's capacity to build a life with the pressures exerted on that life. A shop, a family language and a legal challenge are different kinds of community work. The endpoint is not an exotic “Chinatown” atmosphere. It is a named person's account of what belonging could demand.

## 3. Then / now
- **1894 / circa 1910:** the Society's family account places partnership and bookkeeping respectively. These are different generations and business milestones. [6]
- **1909:** the Society credits architect **George DeSnell** with the adjoining Mai Wah building. It describes commercial spaces below and the noodle parlor upstairs. Do not treat that construction date as the beginning of the community. [8]
- **1940s onward:** closure of family businesses did not mean the buildings immediately became empty; family residence and later other commercial use followed. [6, 8]
- **1998:** Joyce's interview is retrospective. It preserves personal experience and remembered family stories, not contemporaneous proof of every ancestral detail. [4–5]
- **Present winter:** the museum's seasonal gate and the archives' public research function are different offers. An open archive is not a replacement museum interior or a reopened historic restaurant.

## 4. What's still there
**Museum buildings:** the Mai Wah Society interprets the surviving mercantile/noodle-parlor complex. Its noodle-parlor account describes William Chinn's ownership by 1949 and Paul Eno's later repair/secondhand business. Those later layers matter; not everything visible should be labeled untouched Chinese restaurant fabric. [8]

**Operator-described interpretation:** the family page describes family artifacts and a digital exhibit. That publication is not a field receipt proving a screen works, a particular object is presently displayed or an upper floor is available. [6]

**Public released oral record:** University of Montana holds **OH 436-001**, with an interviewee-reviewed transcript. That record is usable for reading within its published boundaries; it is not in the Butte archives merely because the subject lived in Butte. [4–5]

**Public archives:** a lawful research setting with immigration and community collections and public computers. The specific requested originals may require staff retrieval or may be held elsewhere. [2]

**Excluded:** private homes, temple or devotional spaces, archaeological excavations, adjoining vacant land, alleys or interiors without independently verified public permission, and all closed museum areas. No artifact search, collecting, ritual imitation or camera task aimed at cultural objects.

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

- Joyce's transcript places her sister's school entry in 1955, while the Society describes Yvonne as born in 1950. Do not silently reconcile ages or dates; the remembered incident is not a school enrollment record. [5–6]

- The local boycott narrative reproduces a later newspaper claim that Chinese residents never sued, yet describes their lawsuit. This is an example of contradictory outside characterization, not evidence to negate the litigation. [7]
- No actual Hum Fay affidavit, complete case file, archaeological report or item-level museum accession record was inspected. No claim of court-file verification is made.
