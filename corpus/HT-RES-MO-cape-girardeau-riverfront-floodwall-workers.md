# Cape Girardeau — Somebody had to keep the pumps running

Missouri OP04 · Draft for independent Dash review · Quiet witness for flood loss

## 2. Story

The early record of flood work is a set of 1922 photographic postcards. The Southeast Missouri State University finding aid describes the Frisco passenger station, buildings along Water Street, sandbagging and a train on inundated tracks. The writer of the postcard descriptions is unidentified. The archive dates the group April 21–22, 1922 and attributes two scenes to Kassel Studio. Those catalog descriptions give subjects for research. The prints themselves require separate inspection and item identification. [4]

The downtown wall took more than a decision to pour concrete. The Corps history records authorization through the Flood Control Act of 1950, local financial assurances in 1955, the start of downtown work in February 1956 and a right-of-way delay that held one section until 1964. It quotes the Board’s judgment that “a higher degree of protection is desirable.” This is the Corps historian quoting a federal report. The historical cost figures cover different proposals and scopes. The wall created a line along which people would close gates, watch the river and maintain the works. [5]

The long operating record appears in a city naming memorandum. It says Andy Juden Jr. served on the Main Street Levee District board from 1958 until the city's 2008 takeover. It records his responsibility to “maintain the operation of the Merriwether Pumping Station.” The words come from the city's memorandum, not from Juden's own testimony. His role is verified as a board officer and steward of operation. A name on a station cannot account for everyone who staffed it. City Public Works explains that crews must staff pumps through high water as gravity drainage stops working, and must keep watching the whole levee. [2, 6]

The 2021 Corps notice records the completion of a later repair series involving settlement and stabilization, joint and seal repairs, renewed drainage and new pumping stations. The line downtown is a structure made in the mid-twentieth century and repeatedly maintained by people after it opened. [5, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historical location.** The lower downtown business and rail frontage along the Mississippi was exposed to recurrent flooding. The earlier streets and existing flood-control line overlap in broad reach. Specific scenes, individual stations and the downtown-project reach need separate mapping. [2, 4–5]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Use legal downtown parking and the public downtown sidewalk near the Port Cape building at Water and Themis streets, before the gate. It is a drive-up and short public walk. Use sidewalks and open crossings only. Riverfront Park is a separate optional public exterior beyond the wall. Enter it only via open public pedestrian access and stay on its walkway. Active tracks, gate works, pump compounds and any waterside ground beyond a posted closure are outside the trip. [1–3]

## 5. A short visit

Approach on downtown sidewalks and read the wall from the city side near Water and Themis. If the riverfront is open, use the marked public walkway briefly and return to the city side. Finish at the landward wall with **The work that begins when gravity stops**, reading the city’s description of pump staffing and the memorandum’s note on Juden. The tourism page describes the named Mississippi River Tales panels as outside the floodwall. Those panels are outside the landward fallback and can be seen only from an open lawful public approach. The closure gate determines what part of the walk is available. If streets or sidewalks are wet, icy or closed, read the public records remotely. [1–7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

The SEMO finding aid gives 7,210 feet for the historical combined wall and earthen levees and 4,000 feet for the wall. The city describes an 8,460-foot maintained system in its current scope. Retain all three quantities with their source scope. The earlier Corps history includes a 51-foot design revision under discussion. Current Public Works describes protection to a 54-foot flood stage, and the city’s published gate closures start well below that line. These figures do not predict your access on any particular day. [2, 4–5]

The city’s hearing packet records Juden’s role and a naming request. The outcome of the naming action, the newspaper's original page, crew shift logs, individual construction workers and the 1950 drawings remain open.
