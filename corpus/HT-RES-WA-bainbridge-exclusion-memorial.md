## 2. The story

On **March 30, 1942**, Japanese American islanders were forcibly removed from their community through the Eagledale ferry landing. Survivor **Fumiko Hayashida**, in her **September 28, 2006 testimony to the U.S. House Subcommittee on National Parks**, described caring for two young children while pregnant, hurriedly arranging affairs, and departing with little certainty about what came next. She recalled **227 people** boarding that day. Her testimony follows incarceration at Manzanar and Minidoka and the difficulty of rebuilding a livelihood afterward. This is her account, not a universal experience imposed on every family. [5]

**Direct published survivor voice:** In her **February 25, 2006** interview with **Debra Grindeland**, Hayashida explained why remembrance mattered: **“We all want to remember what we went through and we don't want it to go through again like that.”** The segment also recognizes differing experiences and burdens, particularly those of parents with small children. [6]

The transcript belongs to the **Bainbridge Island Japanese American Community Collection**, hosted by Densho, segment **ddr-densho-1001-1-19**, legacy **denshovh-hfumiko-02-0019**. Transcript read; video/audio not played. [6–7] The memorial's purpose is not to invite a visitor to pretend to understand incarceration by taking a short walk. It is to make room for survivors' testimony and responsibility in the present.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a functioning ferry landing became a point of enforced departure. Hayashida's testimony recalls the image made there of her carrying Natalie. She later spoke publicly for a memorial, turning a photograph's anonymous symbol back into an individual voice. [5]

**Now:** BIJAEMA identifies the modern **Departure Deck** with the former Eagledale dock location. Dedicated in 2021, with artwork dedicated in 2022, it is a commemorative structure, not a surviving 1942 ferry dock. The association identifies an old western red cedar beside it as present at the historical departure. [8] That is an institutional identification, not a survey or tree-age verification performed here.

The cedar-and-granite Story Wall names **276 residents** with their ages, grouped by surname. The operator identifies its length as 276 feet and its public opening as July 30, 2011. [9] The 276 commemorated residents and the 227 departure-day figure are differently scoped counts. Do not call the wall a count of everyone boarding that particular ferry.

## 4. What's still there

- **Public outdoor memorial:** Story Wall, interpretive art and signed visitor paths; daylight public access is documented. Stay within presently open areas. [1,9]
- **Departure Deck:** modern memorial at the historical landing location, subject to actual site conditions and signs. No claim that original dock timbers survive. [8]
- **Visitor center:** current operator and NPS pages establish opening and regular weekend hours; they do not establish staffing every winter weekday or every holiday. [1–2]
- **Memorial landscape:** the memorial is not permission to search the beach. The construction bulletin explicitly says there is no beach access from the memorial grounds. [4]
- **Neighboring properties and former homes/farms:** excluded from this dossier's public visit. Their historical relevance does not create public access.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**A proposed reflection sequence, not an activated itinerary.** Resolve the approach before departure; finish outdoors before dusk. No promised travel time or ranger availability.

1. Begin in the currently designated public orientation area or visitor center when open. Establish the distinction between forced exclusion from home, incarceration elsewhere and the later creation of this memorial.
2. Move slowly along the Story Wall if the signed path is open. Names and ages restore individuals where a mass count can obscure them. Do not turn name-reading into completion checking or ask children to collect a specified number of names.
3. Read the interpretive artwork as later remembrance informed by oral histories, not a contemporaneous photograph or unmediated reconstruction. The operator identifies **Steven Gardner** as the wall's terracotta-frieze artist. [9]
4. End at the public Departure Deck, **only if currently accessible**, or stop earlier at an open interpretive point. Read Hayashida's own statement about remembering and preventing recurrence. The strongest moment is the contrast between an ordinary journey across water and a departure whose destination and duration were not chosen. This reflection is editorial framing, not an instruction to reenact the removal. [6,8]

Allow silence. Do not stage luggage, numbered tags, costumes or forced-removal poses. A visitor's freedom to leave the memorial is part of the contrast, not an equivalent experience.
