## 2 · The story

### The meeting that started at 8:20

Florence Rogers ran the federal employees’ credit union in the Alfred P. Murrah Federal Building. In her account she got to work early with pictures from a recent cruise to show her coworkers. The meeting started around 8:20. As the group worked through audit preparations, she would turn toward her computer for the next item and lean back while her coworkers divided the copying and other jobs. Her recollection preserves a morning in which people expected to get through an agenda and share vacation photographs. [3]

When she could no longer see the others, Rogers remembered calling, “Where are you guys? Where are you guys?” The FBI’s published audio transcript supplies her words. It records her first impression that they had escaped and her later understanding that the collapse had taken them. Read the ordinary beginning and this question together. The question belongs to a person who had spent the morning talking to people she expected to see again. [3]

### What the rescuers’ trees remember

Richard Kelley was off duty and responded as a paramedic. The Memorial’s First Person listing identifies him by name and role. In the public landscape, the Rescuers’ Orchard acknowledges volunteers as well as formal responders. Across the old street, the Field of Empty Chairs commemorates the 168 people killed. Each chair stands for a person, and the nineteen smaller chairs mark children. Names, the survivor wall and the orchard let three groups be remembered at once: the people killed, those who lived through it, and those who came to help. [4–5]

The American elm nearby had been a source of shade in a downtown parking lot. The operator’s research dates its planting to around 1920 and shows it already established behind a house in 1947. The site’s successive uses were residential, commercial, and commemorative. The tree was in ordinary use long before it was a memorial. [5–6]

## 3 · Then / now

- **Historic ground.** The former federal building footprint is the Field of Empty Chairs. The reflecting pool occupies part of former NW Fifth Street. The Memorial identifies the east-end walls as the remaining parts of the federal building and uses lobby granite for the survivor names. [5]
- **Lawful physical anchor.** Outdoor Symbolic Memorial, NW Sixth and North Harvey vicinity, with the museum entrance at 620 N Harvey Ave. Drive-up to the signed public arrival area, then a short walk along designated memorial paths. The separate ticketed museum uses its posted visitor entrance and galleries. Visit the field from its public paths. [1–2,5]
- **Weather option.** During regular museum hours use the galleries or, from anywhere, Rogers’s published account. [1,3]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Enter by the Gates of Time. Pause by the reflecting pool and picture the former street running between the two sides. [5]
2. Take the designated path to the Survivor Tree and the orchard. Give a moment to the volunteers and off-duty responders, including Kelley. [4–6]
3. **Strongest story moment — A question at the edge of the field.** From the public path overlooking the chairs, read the first paragraph of Rogers’s transcript and her repeated question. Put the page away and look at the chairs. [3,5]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The operator’s museum page lists year-round 5 p.m. closing. The NPS seasons page lists 6 p.m. closing between Memorial Day and Labor Day and regular interpretation in that period. The operator governs museum planning, with Thanksgiving Day specifically closed. Outdoor access is 24 hours in both sources. [1–2,7]
- The museum lists group First Person programs by appointment. Kelley’s video is linked, but its spoken audio was not reviewed in this batch. His role comes from the Memorial’s published caption. Rogers’s words were read as the FBI’s transcript, without a separate audio comparison. [3–4]
- Individual archival identifiers and photographer/rights documentation for the operator’s historic images remain open. [6,8]
