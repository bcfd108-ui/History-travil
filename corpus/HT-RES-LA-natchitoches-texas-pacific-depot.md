# Texas and Pacific Railway Depot, Natchitoches

## 1. Trip gate
Use the National Park Service visitor entrance at 1927 Remembrance Way Wednesday–Sunday, 9 a.m.–3:30 p.m. The depot closes Monday, Tuesday and federal holidays. Use the marked street-side approach in passable weather and skip the grounds if closed. Checked November 20, 2026. [1–2]

## 2. Story
One Natchitoches resident recalled, “Our mothers went ahead and got jobs” before sending for the children. The National Park Service publishes the excerpt as an unattributed recollection about family migration to San Francisco. The sentence stops being abstract when you see what the station asked of a passenger before boarding. Race determined the door and room through which a traveler passed. The family account records what the journey meant, and the surviving floor plan shows the physical rules that preceded it. The speaker’s name and original recording remain unverified. [2, 4]

When Natchitoches gained local rail service in 1887, the Tap Line connected the town to Cypress. Texas and Pacific acquired that line in 1901, and by 1927 the railway could dedicate a much more imposing passenger building. The operator names designer F. G. Shaw and railroad president J. L. Lancaster with different roles in choosing the design. The 1987 nomination calls out an exterior loggia, paired towers and colored terra cotta. A passenger could see the railway’s civic ambitions from the street. Inside, separate entrances and unequal facilities expressed the racial order that governed passenger service. The structure carries those two histories simultaneously. [2–3]

The segregated doors belong to a broader legal system. The U.S. Supreme Court’s 1896 opinion in *Plessy v. Ferguson* reproduces the Louisiana statute’s phrase “equal but separate accommodations.” Its language concerns railway coaches; the actual station layout comes from the depot’s site history and building evidence. NPS reports that passenger service ended here in 1965 and that the depot never operated as an integrated passenger station. NPS interprets the separate spaces today with replica signs and community input. They are objects for quiet reflection. [2, 5]

## 3. Then and now
The 1927 station is the historic location. The same building reopened for public interpretation under a different operator. Use the National Park Service street-side visitor entrance and posted visitor rooms. Historic tracks and the rail-facing working side are outside the itinerary. [1–3]

## 4. Surviving features and access
- Public visitor rooms and interpreted waiting-room layout — staffed interior as posted. Ask staff which rooms are open.
- Arched street front and paired towers — footway or authorized visitor approach only.
- Track side, platforms beyond approved visitor limits, and private operating areas — documentary only. [1–3]

## 5. Public progression
Drive up to the signed visitor entrance and take the short authorized approach. Read the facade from the street side, then ask staff about the waiting rooms and replica signage. End with **The room before the journey**. Set the family recollection beside the station’s division of travelers who were trying to make the same trip. Ordinary access follows today’s entrance, with no reenactment of a segregated path. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
The nomination calls the building Italian Renaissance, while NPS reports that Lancaster selected a Spanish design. These are assessments of the same depot, and both are retained. The National Register nomination dates the depot to 1926, while the current NPS page dates completion and dedication to 1927. No building contract was located to establish whether 1926 refers to commencement. The nomination uses Sixth Street and the HABS caption index lists Seventh Street. For the public visitor entrance use the NPS Remembrance Way address. [1, 3, 9] The NPS interview solicitation and summary were read; no attributable full passenger interview, worker roster, or E. A. Johnson letter used for this dossier was recovered. [1–4, 6]
