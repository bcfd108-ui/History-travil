## 2. The story

**A contemporary employment promise.** *Commercial West*, **February 6, 1926, printed page 11**, reported the Armour acquisition of a cooperative-built plant it called “Fargo.” Its account dated the formal opening to **November 7, 1925**, and projected about **400 employees when fully operating**. The PDF was retrieved and its text directly read. The number is a forecast in business reporting, not a verified payroll or count of jobs already held. [4]

**A contemporary account of worker conflict.** **William Simmons**, writing *Packinghouse Workers Want Action Policy* in *Socialist Appeal*, **September 15, 1939, pages 1 and 3**, described two dismissed workers at Armour’s “Fargo, N.D.” plant and a dispute over reinstatement. The published ETOL transcription was directly read. It supplies contemporaneous reporting of a grievance, but neither dismissed worker is named and no personal interview is provided. [5]

**Perspective must remain attached.** Simmons advocates a particular labor-political position and criticizes union leadership. The article is evidence of contemporary argument, not a neutral adjudication of every allegation. Its nationwide organizing claims are not Fargo employment statistics. No tactics or operational instructions are reproduced here.

**Original interpretation.** Put the two documents beside each other: one sees prospective employment, the other workers defending their place in it. The useful tension is between a city’s hoped-for economic engine and the uncertainty experienced by the people who depended on a wage. Neither document permits inventing a worker’s home life.

## 3. Then / now

**West Fargo is the established local history.** The city’s account places Equity’s plant and worker housing in the development of West Fargo and says Armour acquired both the business and associated housing. This supports the locality while explaining why older sources’ shorter “Fargo” label should not be turned into a city-of-Fargo plant destination. [6]

**Closure was not one simple date.** The city history gives **1959** for Armour’s closure and describes a later Siouxland enterprise and subsequent operations, with a plant closure in **1999**. Prairie Public’s historical feature gives **1960** for Armour’s closure. Preserve the discrepancy; do not treat 1999 as Armour’s departure or silently choose one earlier date. [6][7]

**Job loss had household consequences.** The municipal account says residents sought different employment or relocated after Armour closed. It does not identify which individual households moved, their destinations or their private circumstances. Those missing lives should not be filled with generic packinghouse stories from another state. [6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Start with expectation.** Read the 1926 projected employment figure with its conditional wording. A proposed workforce is not a photograph of lives already secured.

**Move to the competing account.** Read the short 1939 local grievance passage, keeping author, publication and political standpoint visible. It does not establish what each unnamed worker would have said.

**Use archival space only if confirmed useful.** Any later research-room visit should be tied to an identified source request and the institution’s public procedures. Do not make an industrial stop merely to put scenery behind the reading.

**End with people, not output.** The strongest story moment is the difference between celebrating an employer’s arrival and facing the loss of a job. Close with that uncertainty rather than a factory photo hunt or a reconstruction of working conditions.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**Named worker testimony remains missing.** Simmons is the named author of a contemporary report, not a demonstrated local packinghouse employee. The two workers he discusses remain unnamed. This dossier meets a contemporary-record threshold, not a completed firsthand-worker interview threshold.

**Source format differs.** The business PDF was read through extracted text; the labor article through a later transcription identifying its original issue and pages. The original labor newspaper scan was not examined. No audio was heard.

**Municipal chronology is not perfectly tidy.** The city page pairs a 1919 start with a 1922 bankruptcy while saying four operating years. Those statements are not silently normalized into an exact duration. The 1959/1960 closure disagreement also remains open. [6][7]

**Scope held narrow.** No contemporary employer, immigration status, worker health condition, private residence or present labor practice is inferred from the historic accounts. No particular museum exhibit has been confirmed.
