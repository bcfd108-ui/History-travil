## 2. The story

In the Homeland organization's published history, **Taz E. E. Conner**, identified as the Tamkaliks Celebration founder, addresses people gathered in 1994: **“It is time once again to let the land hear the familiar sounds”**. His larger statement concerns language, people, shared responsibility and future generations. This is an attributed written reproduction of a community address, not audio heard or a manuscript examined here. [3]

That thought prevents this stop from ending with exile. The organization's account connects its work to the forced removal of the Wallowa Band in 1877 and descendants living across several reservations and regions. It describes return as rebuilding relationships, not discovering an empty landscape. [2]

A sovereign voice gives the present-day dimension. In the Nez Perce Tribe's announcement of a June 24, 2025 governmental meeting in its western homeland, then-identified chairman **Shannon F. Wheeler** states: **“It is important that the NPTEC regularly conduct sovereign governance throughout our territory”**. Vice-chairman **Ashton Picard** likewise frames the action as an exercise of sovereignty. These are government-published statements, not a tourism slogan or permission to attend a meeting now. The notice establishes a planned meeting, not independent evidence of its subsequent occurrence. [4]

**Original interpretive synthesis:** homeland is not simply a place someone remembers. It can also be a responsibility exercised through family return, institutional stewardship and governance. Those forms are related but not interchangeable.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** the operator identifies forced removal in 1877 as a rupture whose consequences continue across generations. This draft does not convert the broader 1877 history into a battlefield route or invent a single experience shared by every Nez Perce person. [2]

**Community return:** the Homeland history dates its grassroots beginnings and first celebration to 1989 and formal nonprofit organization to 1996. Its history emphasizes a place for reconnection, rather than a reconstruction of an abandoned village. [3]

**Now:** the nonprofit explicitly distinguishes itself from a Tribal government. Its board includes people associated with the Colville, Umatilla and Nez Perce communities as well as local non-Tribal participants. The separate Nez Perce Tribal Executive Committee announcement is a sovereign-government source, not a statement issued by the nonprofit. [2,5,4]

No single board member represents every descendant, and one Tribe's government must not be substituted for another's. No historic/modern photo pair verified.

## Connected ground — The Flight of 1877

In the spring of 1877 the Wallowa Band was ordered to leave this homeland within thirty days; the journey that followed reached its first battle at White Bird Canyon on June 17, 1877, and its end at Bear Paw on October 5, 1877 — and this ground's present work is the return that journey was meant to prevent.
