# HT-RES-MD-annapolis-maryland-state-house — Annapolis: the room where Washington handed the power back
Division MD seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

For nine months in 1783–84, the Maryland State House in Annapolis was the capitol of the United States. In its Old Senate Chamber, George Washington resigned his military commission on December 23, 1783, and Congress ratified the Treaty of Paris three weeks later. The building never retired: Maryland’s legislature still meets under its wooden dome.

## 1. Trip gate

The Maryland State House is lawful, staffed public ground at State Circle in Annapolis. It remains an active seat of government rather than a preserved shell. The Maryland State Archives’ visitor page reports public hours of 8:30 a.m.–5:00 p.m. daily except Christmas and New Year’s Day, with picture identification, metal-detector screening, and bag inspection. Those conditions are part of the destination: this entry points only to the public entrance and publicly opened rooms, never to legislative offices, restricted corridors, or security-controlled work areas. Hours and screening procedures must be checked again at publication and before travel.

The self-guided public visit does not require a research appointment. Group educational tours are a separate reservation program and are not the entry’s public anchor.

## 2. Story

The present State House is the third capitol built on State Circle. Construction began in 1772 under architect Joseph Horatio Anderson and builder Charles Wallace, stopped and slowed during the Revolution, and reached usable completion in 1779. The familiar wooden dome came later. Work directed by Joseph Clark and completed under John Shaw replaced an earlier roof and cupola between the mid-1780s and 1790s. The result is not one frozen moment: the 1770s brick core, later dome, nineteenth-century changes, and 1902–05 annex record a working government adapting the same ground.

From November 26, 1783, to August 13, 1784, the Confederation Congress met in the Old Senate Chamber. On December 23, 1783, George Washington returned his military commission to Congress there. His prepared address described the task entrusted to him as finished and placed his authority back in civilian hands. The significance lies in the act, not a myth of effortless unity: a victorious commander voluntarily relinquished national military power before representatives of the states.

Three weeks later, on January 14, 1784, Congress ratified the Treaty of Paris in the same chamber. The treaty had been signed in Paris on September 3, 1783; Congress’s ratification made Annapolis one of the places where the legal close of the Revolutionary War became governmental action. The Maryland State Archives preserves the ratified copy bearing the seal of Congress and the signatures of congressional president Thomas Mifflin and secretary Charles Thomson.

The building’s long public life continued after the national capital moved. Maryland’s legislature still meets here, and the governor, lieutenant governor, Senate president, and House speaker maintain offices in the complex. The State House was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960. Its national history therefore sits inside a living state institution rather than a museum reconstructed after the fact.

## 3. Then and now

In 1783–84, delegates entered a relatively new state capitol whose exterior was not yet crowned by the present dome. Congress used the Old Senate Chamber while Annapolis served as the national capital. Washington’s resignation and the treaty ratification occurred in a room that survives, though its finishes, furnishings, and interpretation have passed through restoration and reconstruction campaigns.

Today the State House remains at the center of State Circle. The 1770s section can still be read in the brick mass shown in the adopted HABS photograph. The wooden dome is the city’s dominant landmark, while the early-twentieth-century annex contains the modern legislative chambers. Public exhibits interpret the Old Senate Chamber, Washington’s resignation, the treaty, and the building’s wider Maryland history. Visitors should distinguish original fabric, restored fabric, period objects, and interpretive reproductions; the building itself does not make every displayed element an eighteenth-century survivor.

The State House Trust administers interpretation, care, maintenance, and use of public areas. Security staff, legislative operations, demonstrations, ceremonies, construction, or emergency measures may narrow ordinary access without changing the historical identity of the place.

## 5. Public progression

Begin outside on State Circle, where the compact 1770s block and the taller dome can be understood together. Enter only through the designated public security point. Inside, take the official self-guided route to the Old Senate Chamber and its exhibits. Read Washington’s resignation and the treaty ratification as consecutive acts in the same room: first the return of military authority, then the formal close of war. Continue only through rooms opened to visitors, then return to the grounds to compare the building with the HABS southeast-elevation photograph.

This is a civic and constitutional-history visit, not a scavenger hunt. Do not obstruct government work, photograph screening operations, test locked doors, or treat offices as exhibits.

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

- Official sources describe construction as beginning in 1772 and completion or first use in 1779. The LOC HABS record also lists 1771–79 as the initial-construction range. This dossier prints the narrower state interpretation while preserving the HABS range in the source record.
- Official state sources differ on the exact span used to describe Annapolis as the national capital: the Congress sat from November 26, 1783, to August 13, 1784, while an older Maryland Trust page uses a June 1784 endpoint. The current Maryland State Archives tour and HABS sources support the August date.
- Restoration has altered the Old Senate Chamber. This draft does not claim that every surface, furnishing, or displayed document is original to December 1783.
- The adopted HABS color transparency has an LOC catalog source date of 1970 and credits Jack E. Boucher, but the item-level record does not print a day or month. The photo date is therefore recorded as 1970, not made more precise.
- Coordinates are intentionally blank until two independent authoritative sources agree within the project standard.
