## 2. The story
Begin with a person who calls the place by a name that precedes its park sign.

**Ninna Piiksii (Mike Bruised Head)** speaks about this specific jump in the Galt Museum & Archives' collaboratively curated **Nitsitapiisksakoo: Nitsitapii Landscapes Part 3**. He identifies himself with the **Mamioyiiksi, Fish Eater Band**, describes ancestral use and fall gatherings of bison near the Missouri, and names the continuing Blackfeet/Blackfoot relationship to this place. His short statement, **“It still has the spirit of the old people,”** rejects the idea that protection has turned it into a culturally empty relic. The published English text was read; the embedded Blackfoot audio was not independently transcribed. [5]

That is an attributed Blackfoot account, not a statement invented by this project or a universal voice for every Nation. The exhibit is co-curated by Bruised Head, **Itsinohtss piyaki (Rebecca Many Grey Horses)** and **Akaiksims’staki (Bobbie Fox)**. It identifies its perspective explicitly. [5]

The state’s **Buffalo and the Blackfeet** teaching unit, revised January 2010, adds a useful material question: what happened after the hunt? Its reproduced Bruce Auchly archaeology account describes food processing rather than only a dramatic fall. Pemmican combined dried meat, fruit and fat; the teaching material also describes hide boats carrying goods across rivers. Read this as an account of skilled collective work and provisioning, not merely a dangerous spectacle. The unit explicitly recognizes continuing Tribal cultures and warns that some knowledge remains private. [6]

A present-day counterweight comes from **Al Wiseman**, a Little Shell elder and Métis historian, interviewed by **Mike Green** for the University of Montana's **Native News** in 2024. Wiseman describes creating educational footlockers to make Métis history accessible. The same published story includes a photograph caption identifying Indigenous games participants gathered at First Peoples Buffalo Jump. Wiseman's own childhood memories concern other places, not an eyewitness account of a historic hunt here. Keep those distinctions rather than moving his biography onto this ground. [7]

**Interpretive conclusion:** the site's story is not complete when the visitor understands how animals were harvested. It also concerns who carries knowledge, who has authority to share it, and why a public park does not make every cultural place public content. The strongest moment is a named person's continuing relationship to the place, not a cliff-edge thrill.

The Blackfeet Nation’s *Restoring Balance* records a decision made by leaders of four Blackfoot Confederacy nations in 2009 and the return of 88 buffalo to Blackfeet lands in 2016. Chairman Harry Barnes said, “We must now take care of Iinnii.” Read that sentence after the public interpretation of historical buffalo use. Barnes is speaking about present responsibility. The reintroduction described here took place on Blackfeet lands, separately from this public gallery at Ulm. The publication’s cover credits the Blackfeet Tribal Historic Preservation Archives for a portrait of Bird Rattle, circa 1930s. It supplies no comparison point at the park. [11]
