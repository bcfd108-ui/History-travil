# U.S. Grant Home State Historic Site — the furnished house Galena gave its general in 1865
HT-RES-IL-us-grant-home · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Ulysses S. Grant came to Galena in the spring of 1860 as a man whose civilian life had not worked. The site's own history tells it plainly: he had ended a fifteen-year military career six years earlier and had enjoyed little business success since, and he moved to northwestern Illinois hoping to reverse his economic misfortune by working in the Galena leather-goods store owned by his father and managed by his younger brothers, Simpson and Orvil. He was, in the site's phrase, a clerk in name only, spending considerable time traveling the store's trade territory across southwestern Wisconsin, southeastern Minnesota and northeastern Iowa. Grant and his wife Julia rented a modest Federal-style brick house on the west side of the river, at about $100 a year, and lived there until he left in the spring of 1861 to serve in the Civil War. The City of Galena's history adds the local fact that matters most to this ground: in Galena Grant met the politically influential friends — Congressman Elihu B. Washburne first among them — who stood behind his rise. The leather store stood in a lead city already past its peak; the man clerking in it was four years from commanding the armies of the United States. [1, 6]

What he did in those four years is national record and is stated here only as far as this house needs it. Commissioned colonel of the 21st Illinois Volunteer Infantry in June 1861, Grant rose through progressively significant Union commands, engineered the victory at Vicksburg in 1863, and in March 1864 was appointed lieutenant general in command of the Union armies, receiving General Robert E. Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865. On August 18, 1865, Galena received him home. The site's account, drawing on Julia Grant's own recollection, describes a grand triumphal arch spanning Main Street, a jubilant procession, speeches and evening fireworks, and Julia's memory of "a tremendous and enthusiastic outpouring of people" before the family was "conducted to a lovely villa exquisitely furnished with everything good taste could desire." The villa was the gift: a brick Italianate house on Bouthillier Street, on the ridge east of the Galena River, presented to the general that day by a group of his fellow citizens. A state memorial pamphlet preserved at the Internet Archive tells the homecoming the same way — procession, decorated arches, thirty-six young women in white waving flags and throwing bouquets from the Main Street arch, a levee at the Custom House hall and a reception ball at the DeSoto House — and records that the Grants left for Washington on September 12, 1865, where Grant resumed his duties as commanding general. [1, 7]

The house itself predates the gift and explains the gift's scale. It was designed by William Dennison — styled "Engineer and Architect" in the National Register nomination — and built in 1859–60 for Alexander J. Jackson, a former Galena city clerk. The state site describes a two-story brick Italianate structure: wide overhanging eaves on large wooden brackets, a low-pitched roof, and a columned covered piazza with a balcony above at the main entrance. Inside, the first floor holds an entry hall, parlor, dining room, library, kitchen with pantry, and a staff room; the second floor holds five bedrooms, one with a connecting dressing room. In June 1865, on the operator's account, Thomas B. Hughlett, acting on behalf of a small group of local Republicans, purchased the house for $2,500 and presented it to Grant two months later; the nomination's account of the same purchase gives a materially different figure, printed in Gaps and not merged. The state site names Elihu B. Washburne among the group of prominent local Republicans who purchased the house. Either way, the meaning of the transaction is the same and is the house's whole significance: a lead city that had sent its clerk off to war bought its returning general the finest kind of house it knew how to give, furnished, on the hill above the town. [1, 2, 4]

Grant never again lived in Galena full-time, and the house's own record is honest about that. He used it as his official political and voting address, lived there with his family during his 1868 presidential campaign, and returned for brief periods during his presidency (1869–1877) and retirement. In 1873 he said of it, in the words the site preserves: although it was probable he would never live much time among them and would in future be only a visitor, "I hope to retain my residence here . . . I expect to cast my vote here always." Caretakers maintained the house against his visits, the local newspaper reporting it "in excellent order and ready for occupation at any time" and that "visitors are always admitted" — an early, informal house museum inside a president's voting residence. His final stays in the house came in 1880, when he found a new sidewalk laid, outbuildings repaired, trees trimmed and a new wash house built. The state pamphlet refines the ending: as far as its research could tell, Grant left for New York in September 1880 and never stayed in the Galena house again, though he and Julia visited Galena itself for the last time in May 1883. He died on July 23, 1885, at Mount McGregor, New York, days after finishing his memoirs. The house outlasted the man by design: in 1904 Grant's children gave it to the City of Galena "with the understanding that this property is to be kept as a memorial to the late General Ulysses S. Grant, and for no other purpose." The city and the Grant Home Association found the keeping of it too costly, and the house passed to the State of Illinois — in 1931 by the operator's account, in 1932 by the nomination's — which has maintained it as a historic house museum and park ever since. A thorough restoration in 1955–57, directed under Richard Hagen in the nomination's account, returned the house to its 1868 appearance, working from the original drawings, plans and specifications and from the house as pictured in the November 14, 1868 issue of *Frank Leslie's Illustrated Newspaper*; crucially, much of the furniture the Grant family actually used had remained in the house. The home was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960 and placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1966. [1, 2, 4, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The house stands at 500 Bouthillier Street in Galena, Jo Daviess County, on a ridge on the east side of the Galena River with a commanding view of the city, in the nomination's description — inside the Galena Historic District and at the center of the three-block Grant Home Historic Neighborhood of small mid-19th-century homes that the state site describes around it. The National Register record carries the address as 511 Bouthillier Street; the operator, the state site and the reservation page all use 500 Bouthillier Street. The variance is printed in Gaps; the gate uses 500, the address a visitor can navigate to and check in at. Grant State Park, the tree-shaded ground south of the home with picnic tables, and the Long House — a log building of about 1851 moved to the site from Elizabeth, Illinois in 1976 to represent a typical settler's home of mid-19th-century Jo Daviess County — complete the state site's ground. [1, 2, 3, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** U.S. Grant Home State Historic Site, 500 Bouthillier St., Galena, IL 61036 — entered only on the site's interpreter-conducted tour, at the tour's scheduled times, checking in at the front porch 5–10 minutes early in the reservation season. There is no self-guided interior access at any season. The grounds, the adjacent exhibit building, and Grant State Park are the visit's public remainder when the house itself is closed or a tour is full. The rented 1860–61 Grant house on the west side of the river, and Washburne's house where Grant awaited the 1868 election results, are separate properties with separate gates and are not part of this anchor. [1, 2, 5]

## 5. Public progression

Reserve first, in season, and arrive at the front porch early — the tour is the visit, and it is short enough that the ground around it deserves the rest of the hour. Begin outside, on Bouthillier Street, and read the house as Galena meant it to be read in August 1865: an Italianate brick villa on the ridge above a lead city, brackets and balcony and low roof announcing that the town's gift was not shelter but honor. Take the tour in the house's own order. On the first floor, hold the two Galenas together: the parlor, dining room and library furnished to 1868, much of it with the family's own furniture, are a president's campaign residence; the kitchen, pantry and staff room behind them are the working house that caretakers kept "in excellent order and ready for occupation at any time" through the years its owner mostly did not come. Go up to the five bedrooms if you can climb, or take the booklet below if you cannot, and register what kind of home this actually was for the Grants — a voting address, a campaign headquarters, a place returned to in brief, charged intervals, last slept in in 1880. Come out and finish in the adjacent exhibit building, then in Grant State Park, and read the Long House last and correctly: a moved settler's cabin standing as the county's ordinary 1851 against the general's extraordinary 1860, interpretation doing honest work as long as nobody mistakes it for Grant fabric. **Strongest story moment · The clerk's town buys the general a house.** In the spring of 1860 Grant was a clerk in his father's leather store, renting at $100 a year on the west side of the river. In August 1865 the same town processed him under a triumphal arch and handed him a furnished villa on the hill — and he kept it, voted from it, and was buried, in every sense that Galena could manage, as its own. The distance between those two Galenas is five years and one war, and this house is where the distance is measured. Leave by Bouthillier Street in site hours; when the house is closed, the lawful visit is the grounds, the park and this reading — nothing more. [1, 2, 5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Address conflict, printed in full: the operator site, the state site and the reservation page all give 500 Bouthillier Street, Galena, IL 61036. The National Register record (NRIS 66000322) and the nomination give 511 Bouthillier Street. Same house; the operator's 500 governs the gate and navigation, and 511 is preserved here as the Register's form. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
- Purchase-price conflict, printed in full and not merged: the operator site states that Thomas B. Hughlett, on behalf of only a small group of local Republicans, purchased the house for $2,500 in June 1865 and presented it to Grant two months later. The National Register nomination states that in 1865 a committee of Galena citizens paid a reported $16,000 for the Jackson house, the lot and the furnishings as a gift for General Grant. A $2,500 house price against a $16,000 house-lot-and-furnishings total could reconcile the two — furnishings for a whole villa were a major cost, and the gift is consistently described as furnished — but no source checked this run itemizes the purchase, so the reconciliation is offered as a possibility, not a finding, and both figures stand as printed. [1, 4]
- State-acquisition year conflict: the operator site states that in 1931 the city deeded the house to the State of Illinois, after the Grant children gave it to the city in 1904. The nomination states that in 1932 the Grant Association presented the house to the state. A 1931 deed with a 1932 formal presentation would reconcile the two, but no source checked this run states that sequence; the dossier says the house passed to the state in 1931–32 and prints both years here. The state site agrees on the substance — state maintenance as a historic house museum ever since — without settling the year. [1, 2, 4]
- Admission conflict, printed in full: the operator site lists $5.00 adults and $3.00 children. The site's own reservation page for the current season states there is no charge for the tour, with donations appreciated in cash or check in person or by card online. The state site page checked this run lists no admission figure. A visitor should budget the operator's $5/$3, expect the reservation page's no-charge rule to govern a booked winter tour, and treat the discrepancy as the site's own two pages disagreeing — flagged for the verifying flow to settle with the site. [1, 5]
- Hours and tour-pattern conflict, printed in full: the operator site gives Wednesday–Sunday hours of 9:00–4:45 (March–October) and 9:00–4:00 (November–February), tours of 25–30 minutes forming continuously with waits of about 10 minutes. The reservation page gives fixed tour times every 45 minutes from 9:00 to 4:00 with a noon–1 p.m. lunch closure, reservations strongly recommended, walk-ups only into open spots. The state site gives a tour length of approximately 20–30 minutes. The dossier's gate follows the stricter instrument — reserve, take a fixed tour time, arrive early — because on the checked date the reservation season (on or after November 1, 2026) is in force; the operator's continuous-tour description is printed here as the main-season pattern it most plausibly describes, without any source checked this run saying so expressly. [1, 2, 5]
- Final-visit refinement, not a contradiction: the state site states Grant visited the home for the last time in 1880. The state memorial pamphlet states he never stayed in the house again after September 1880 and that he and Julia visited Galena for the last time in May 1883. Read together — last stay in the house 1880, last visit to the town 1883 — the two agree; the dossier keeps the distinction explicit because compressed accounts routinely collapse it into a single wrong date. [2, 7]
- Build-date form variance: the state site dates the building 1859–60; the operator site states it was constructed in 1860 for Alexander J. Jackson. Same construction campaign; the dossier uses 1859–60. The nomination further records that the house was built for Jackson, former Galena city clerk, to original specifications describing a brick main block of 36 by 40 feet, two stories of 11 feet each — detail carried here from the nomination text as retrieved. [1, 2, 4]
- Manager-name variance: the operator site states the house is managed by the Illinois Historic Preservation Agency as the U.S. Grant Home State Historic Site; the state site presenting the same property is the Illinois Department of Natural Resources' historic-sites system, and current-season visitor guides describe the U.S. Grant Historic Sites as administered by IDNR. The agency name has changed across reorganizations; the Locator names the site and its state operation without asserting which agency name is current in statute. [1, 2]
- Rented-house gap: the Grants' rented 1860–61 house on the west side of the river — the true "Grant in Galena" domestic ground — is pictured and described by the operator site but its address, survival terms and any public access were not established in the sources checked this run. It is not part of this dossier's anchor, and no visit to it is routed here. IDNR's recordation list separately enters a "First Grant House, 121 South High Street" (HABS IL-26-12) among Galena's documented properties; whether that entry is the rented house is not stated in the list as retrieved, so the identification is printed as a lead, not a finding. [1]
