## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A man could build a business, raise children and have neighbors who valued him, yet still have to prove that he was entitled to return home.

**Period record actually read:** A reproduced application on printed page **331** of *South Dakota History* identifies **Wong Free Lee**, also known as Wing Tsue, seeking permission to travel to China and return. It states that his eight children were born in Deadwood and that he intended their Chinese-language education while retaining his mercantile business. The article associates the affidavit with **[July 1902]**. [3] This is a legal statement reproduced in later scholarship, not the original archive file independently examined.

The reproduction demonstrates how family plans and commercial status had to be presented to immigration authorities. Its insistence on merchant status should be read within that administrative setting, not as a neutral inventory of everything he did or a value judgment about manual labor.

**Named descendant's own published voice:** **Edith C. Wong**, introduced as his great-granddaughter, describes taking up family genealogy after her father **Kam Leung Wong's** death in 2000. In a published address hosted by Jeptha Lodge #121, she recalls the **2004** descendants' reunion and explains that preserving this history depends on people and institutions even when buildings are gone. [4] The page identifies the occasion as the seventy-fifth anniversary of Mount Rushmore's completion, but an exact delivery date is not printed in the text examined. No recording was heard.

**Short recorded-in-text voice:** Edith says, **“After my father passed away in 2000, his genealogy passion has become mine.”** [4] That is her own account of continuing family work, not her great-grandfather's nineteenth-century voice.

**Interpretive synthesis:** The story joins a bureaucratic document with a descendant's act of remembrance. Neither replaces the other: one records claims made under exclusion, the other explains why the family continues to care. A museum visit should deepen that encounter, not turn a Chinese family into scenery for a fictional frontier.

### What the business told its neighbors

A reproduced 1898 advertisement identifies itself as Wing Tsue’s store. It offers groceries, silks and medicines and says, “Americans as well as Chinese are invited to call and inspect my goods.” Read the advertisement alongside the return application. The store spoke to whoever might come through its door. For federal scrutiny the family presented evidence of birth, residence and a business worth returning to. The words come from the facsimile as reproduced on page 337 of the state historical journal. This draft did not inspect the separate 1898 original. [3,7]

The published shop photograph adds a third kind of record. Shelving covered with merchandise tells the eye what the advertising list tells the reader. Its two young figures may be Wong daughters, according to the journal caption. The caption’s qualification matters. It keeps a visible sign of family participation from becoming an invented identification. [3]

## 3. Then / now
The 2009 article by **Edith C. Wong, Eileen French and Rose Estep Fosha** reports that the Wing Tsue buildings were lost in **2005**. [3] This rules out presenting the historic business as an intact surviving interior. A district map is not a reconstruction of the family home.

Edith's later speech is evidence of a descendant's perspective at its own occasion. Its statements about what remained physically visible should not be silently updated into an exhaustive 2026 survey. New memorials or interpretation may exist without restoring the original buildings.

Names vary across sources. The reproduced application uses **Wong Free Lee**; the scholarly article uses **Wong Fee Lee**; the city's profile is titled **Fee Lee Wong**. Preserve each form when identifying its source rather than treating the variants as different people or confusing the business name with a fully established personal name. [3,5]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Read before going:** Identify the applicant, document date and purpose. Recognize that an application written under an exclusionary system is not a casual family travel note.
2. **If the public museum is open:** Use its admitted galleries as the setting for learning. Do not promise a Chinese exhibit that has not been verified. If the relevant material is absent, retain the digital reading rather than seeking private historical property.
3. **Add the descendant's perspective:** Read Edith's brief passage about continuing her father's genealogy. Keep the speaker and occasion visible.
4. **End with the strongest story moment:** Hold together the effort to return home and the later effort to preserve that home's history. The loss of a building need not erase the named people, but neither can a new narrative recreate physical evidence that is gone.
