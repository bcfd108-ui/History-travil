## 2. The story

At a 2024 salmon celebration, **Jeff Mitchell**, identified by the Klamath Tribes' own newspaper as a Tribal elder, distinguished removal of dams from restoration of the larger watershed. His question provides this stop's center: **“Today, I think about what are salmon coming home to?”** The article reports his recollection of negotiations and community needs, rather than presenting river restoration as a finished triumph. This is a published account of spoken remarks, read in text; no recording was heard. [4]

A second named primary voice establishes the earlier stakes. In his **June 20, 2013 Senate testimony**, then-chairman **Donald C. Gentry** linked treaty resources to subsistence, cultural practice and family wellbeing. He described a responsibility to future generations, not merely a competing demand for water. His statement also acknowledged that water cutoffs could affect neighbors and Tribal families. This is dated testimony, not a present water-allocation instruction or current officeholder identification. [5]

The Klamath Tribes identify themselves as **Klamath, Modoc and Yahooskin-Paiute people**. Their published history describes winter food reserves followed by spring fish returns, and later participation in ranching, skilled trades, freighting and timber. These are living communities with changing livelihoods, not people frozen at the moment of first settler arrival. [6]

The original interpretive proposition is simple: begin with what a community needs to continue, then read the basin's engineering and economic history through that question. It is not necessary, or appropriate, to visit a cultural site or functioning water structure to do that.

## 3. Then / now

**Historical clarification:** the Tribes' history dates the treaty to 1864, congressional termination to 1954 and restoration of federal recognition to 1986. It emphasizes that restoration did not return the land base. Termination describes a federal policy and status change, not the disappearance of the people or their culture. [6]

**Fish distinction:** the Tribes' 2018 statement identifies c’waam as Lost River sucker and koptu as shortnose sucker. Its account of artificial propagation expresses both appreciation and concern that much more was needed. These fish are not salmon; success in one fish passage project cannot establish recovery of all treaty resources. The statement is historical evidence, not a 2026 population assessment. [7]

**Recent historical perspective:** the November–December 2024 Tribal newspaper juxtaposes the salmon celebration with a sucker-restoration emergency. Mitchell's remarks and then-chairman William Ray Jr.'s concerns show why celebration and unfinished work can coexist. No current recovery claim is inferred. [4]

## 4. What's still there

- **Public museum interior:** a county institution independent of Tribal government, accessible under its own visitor rules. Do not label it a Klamath Tribes museum or imply Tribal endorsement of every display. [2]
- **Historical building:** the museum FAQ identifies its 1935 building as a former armory and community center. Its present public use does not make it an original fishing or ceremonial place. [3]
- **Interpretive subjects:** the museum's own 2023 overview describes regional Tribal history, natural-resource management, ranching and other subjects. This supports a broad interpretive anchor, not verification of a current water exhibit, individual object or Tribal-authored label. [8]
- **Published community voices:** the linked Tribal sources can be read without entering community lands or facilities.
- **Excluded from this dossier:** waterfront access, infrastructure tours, collecting, fishing, ceremonial attendance and searches for culturally sensitive locations. Publicly published history is not a visitor invitation.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Suggested reading sequence, not a measured itinerary or a verified gallery layout:

1. **Enter and orient.** Establish which public displays are actually open. Do not promise an object or water-history installation that has not been checked.
2. **Read place through livelihood.** Compare the museum's regional interpretation with the Tribes' own account of work, food and community. Keep their authorship distinct.
3. **Separate dates and claims.** Read Gentry's 2013 testimony as a statement made then, and the 2024 newspaper as a later community account. Neither is a current operations bulletin.
4. **End with Mitchell's question in §2.** A fish returning to a watershed is not the same thing as every condition needed for community continuity being restored. Allow a quiet reflection rather than a celebratory photo challenge.
