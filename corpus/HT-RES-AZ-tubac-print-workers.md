## 2. The story

**Named historically documented printing/news worker: Edward E. Cross**, identified by the State of Arizona Research Library as the first editor of the Tubac *Weekly Arizonian*. Its collection history identifies **William Wrightson** as publisher and the Santa Rita Silver Mining Company as owner. These are different roles, not evidence that one person performed every task. The first issue is dated **March 3, 1859**. [2]

**Contemporary artifact:** the Library of Congress catalogs that exact four-page issue as volume 1, number 1, **LCCN sn82014067**. Indexed bibliographic and rights text was read; direct page retrieval failed, and the newspaper page images/full editorial text were not inspected. No statement is quoted as Cross's firsthand voice. [3]

An older park-history article documents Cross's earlier work as a printer before his editorial career. It is later institutional interpretation, not an apprenticeship record or a current exhibit label. [4]

**Original interpretive voice:** A newspaper turns local work into something that can travel. The person who prepares a page decides what receives space, what counts as news and whose words become part of the record. The press is the visible object; the less visible work is selecting, arranging and publishing. A town's printed voice is therefore not the voice of everyone who lived there. It belongs to particular people and institutions with particular interests.

This entry concerns labor and public communication. It contains no operating procedure, equipment instructions or reenactment of the editor's personal conflicts.

## 3. Then / now

The present operator lists an **1859 Washington Press** as a permanent exhibit and identifies it as the press used for Arizona's first newspaper. It says it remains operable. That is an operator provenance claim, not an independent mechanical inspection or proof that a demonstration occurs on every opening day. [5]

A modern presentation is a modern interpretation. Even with a historic object, today's demonstrator is not an eyewitness to 1859, and a fresh souvenir impression is not an original newspaper. No claim is made that the present gallery reproduces the exact historical workplace, staffing or layout.

The period newspaper's political and commercial connections matter. The State Library chronology later follows the title to Tucson under changed ownership/editorship. Those later publications must not be represented as products of the same Tubac shop or the same editorial voice. [2]

## 4. What's still there

The public museum offers a verified printing-history display, and institutional catalogs identify surviving newspaper issues. These are complementary sources: one supports a lawful visit; the other supports historical research. Neither gives access to originals in storage. [3,5]

The museum's operator is **Friends of the Tubac Presidio and Museum, Inc.**, operating on behalf of Arizona State Parks and Trails. A state-park designation does not turn every building or neighboring property into public visitor space. [5]

No worker payroll, compositor's diary, shop roster or named woman print worker was retrieved. Cross's documented role should not erase the hands behind the rest of production, but those hands must not be given invented names or testimony.

No current demonstration timetable, original-issue display, take-home print or artifact-handling opportunity is promised. The itinerary works through public interpretation even without a demonstration.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**One museum visit, 45–60 minutes as an editorial estimate**, not an official tour length. Research first, no trivia layer.

1. **Before arrival: the dated issue.** Present the March 3, 1859 catalog record and the three distinct roles of owner, publisher and editor. Keep the uninspected page-image gap visible.
2. **At the public exhibit: object and labor.** View the press only from the space opened to visitors. The story is about the people and decisions behind printed communication, not equipment operation.
3. **Separate original from interpretation.** Label the historic artifact, any modern explanatory material and any contemporary demonstration as different evidence layers. Do not describe a reproduction as an original.
4. **Strongest landing: a printed voice is selective.** End with the surviving paper as a record made by particular people, rather than neutral proof of everything a community thought. The record is valuable precisely because its authorship and limits remain visible.

If the museum is closed or the exhibit unavailable, use the institutional catalog online. No road route, private shop visit or reenacted duel is included. A future photograph-tag find requires a confirmed public sightline and photography permission; none is activated here.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- Issue metadata was read, but page images and complete editorial text were not. No firsthand quote or unattributed editorial sentiment is invented. [3]
- “Arizona's first newspaper” refers to the geographic history now presented by the institutions. In March 1859 this location was within **New Mexico Territory**, as Sharlot Hall Museum's history explicitly notes; do not silently imply the later U.S. Arizona Territory already existed. [6]
- A collection title spanning multiple years can include Tucson publication. The first Tubac issue is the pinned artifact, not every later issue with the same name. [2–3]
- Object identification is based on the museum's account; repair/restoration history, replaced parts and exact chain of custody were not independently verified. [5]
- Historic work is distinct from present-day public demonstration. No current schedule or guaranteed staff availability found.
- Visitor photography/publication permissions, exact doorway coordinates and weather-specific opening remain unverified.
- The account does not claim that the English-language paper represents Indigenous or Mexican residents. Broader cultural claims require their own sources; no community voice is supplied by inference.
