## 2. The story
A healthcare city is made partly by people whose authority was once taken for granted in the wrong direction: they were responsible for care but expected to remain quiet.

**Joanne Dodd**, a Billings nurse who became a nursing administrator, recalled that hierarchy in a published interview with **Russ Riesinger for KTVQ**. She remembered nurses standing when physicians entered and yielding space in elevators. Her judgment was direct: **“I just didn’t think that was the right way to be treated.”** She described later teaching nurses around Montana about asserting their rights. These are her retrospective recollections, not a newly recovered hospital policy or a claim that every nurse's experience was identical. [4]

A contemporary document lets the visitor hear a different register of her voice. In a **February 20, 1981 Montana House Human Services Committee packet**, her statement identifies her as a registered nurse and vice president of nursing service at Billings Deaconess Hospital. Supporting HB 734, she describes accreditation as relying on **“strong education and coordination”** across hospital units. The statement was visually checked at PDF page 102. The minutes, printed page 7/PDF page 7, say her written testimony was entered through Bill Leary. **Do not stage this as a speech she demonstrably delivered in the room.** [5]

The proposal concerned removing a state-inspection requirement for accredited facilities. Dodd's statement is advocacy, not proof that the bill passed, that it improved care, or that its position is present law. The same minutes record a state health department response and proposed amendments. [5]

A separate locally published profile, *Yellowstone Valley Woman's* “A Heart for Healing,” places her early Deaconess experience in 1952 and follows her transition from nursing to executive, community-education and fundraising work. Its celebratory framing is useful evidence of public recognition, but not a complete institutional history or independent measure of outcomes. [6]

**Interpretive conclusion:** the strongest story is not a tour of medical equipment. It is the distance between being expected to move aside and putting a professional argument into the public record. The older testimony complicates a simple hero story: leadership also involves policies whose consequences require scrutiny. Visitors can respect a worker's voice without endorsing every policy she supported.
