## 2. The story
The most telling record is a daughter's ordinary letter that the government inspected.

**Miriam Kiyo Koyama** wrote from Portland to her father, **Dr. Keizaburo Koyama**, on **March 6, 1942**, while he was detained at Fort Missoula. Her published letter hopes that, when the family is forcibly removed, they might be sent where he is. She wonders whether a photograph reached him. At the bottom, her request is simple: **“Please write soon.”** On that same sheet is a purple government mail-examination stamp. The handwritten letter, date, signature and stamp were visually inspected on PDF page 7. [4]

The source is the **Koyama Family Collection, Japanese American Museum of Oregon**, published through Densho. It is not an imagined composite diary. The letter documents separation and uncertainty; it does not prove when her father received it or whether the family was reunited at Missoula. [4–5]

Fort Missoula's wartime confinement was administered by the **Department of Justice's Immigration and Naturalization Service**. The museum distinguishes it from the War Relocation Authority system. Its account identifies nonmilitary Italians, Japanese resident aliens, Germans and Japanese men brought from Latin America among those held during **1941–1944**. These were different populations and detention pathways, not a single interchangeable group of battlefield prisoners. [3]

The museum identifies Japanese men subjected to loyalty hearings and Italians whose backgrounds included merchant shipping and World's Fair employment. It also describes work, arts and recreation under confinement. Such activities show how people lived and exercised agency while detained; they do not establish consent or freedom. [3]

**A detainee’s published account sharpens the uncertainty.** In the April 6, 1942 diary attributed to **Bunro Sakaino**, translated by **Naoko Tanabe**, men had been making tables and shelves and developing friendships when a transfer order arrived without a destination. Families were to be told not to write until further notice. This is the published English translation, not an independent reading of the Japanese manuscript. [10] His speculation about New Mexico must not be substituted for the actual next destination: the collection history identifies **Fort Sill, Oklahoma**. It also states that pre-1997 provenance is unclear and authorship rests on internal evidence and subsequent research. [11]

An archival counterpoint demonstrates why source position matters. The University of Montana's catalog describes **Lyle Slade's December 3, 1979 interview** as a Border Patrol officer/guard's recollections, including his favorable assessment of conditions. Slade is not a detainee witness. The catalog was inspected, not the complete recording, and his characterization is not adopted as the prisoners' collective experience. [6]

**Interpretive conclusion:** begin and end with the family's words. Administrative categories explain how detention operated; a letter explains something of what separation cost. Neither scenery nor reconstructed comforts should soften forced confinement into a pleasant wartime stay.

## 3. Then / now
- **1941–1944:** the institutional period covered by the museum's Alien Detention Center history. Do not extend that label automatically to later military-prison uses. [3]

- **May 31, 2025:** the operator reports opening **Far From Home: The Internee Experience at Fort Missoula**, interpreting ten individual men through a restored barracks exhibition. Its announcement describes hands-on reproductions, not unrestricted handling of original artifacts. [7]
- **Present visit:** a museum interpretation is neither an original prison experience nor access to the entire former detention footprint. Winter season remains a separate gate.
