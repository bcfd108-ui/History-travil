# Illinois & Michigan Canal at Lockport — the canal's headquarters town, its office, and its warehouse
HT-RES-IL-illinois-michigan-canal-lockport · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The canal was an old idea made urgent by a new city. A water link between Lake Michigan and the Illinois River — and so between the Great Lakes, the Mississippi, and the Gulf — had been proposed as early as 1810 in the National Register nomination's account, and explorers had noted the low Chicago portage generations earlier. Congress approved the project in 1827, a Canal Commission was formed in 1829 to raise money and oversee it, and after years of dormancy ground was broken on July 4, 1836, at Bridgeport on the south branch of the Chicago River. The commissioners laid out a town of their own about a third of the way along the line and made it the headquarters: Lockport, named for the locks that would step boats down from the summit level. The nomination records that the first canal lock was built at Lockport and that headquarters was established here in 1839; the town's own museum dates the laying-out to 1837. From Lockport the work of designing, financing, and building a 96-mile hand-dug canal was administered. [4, 6, 7]

Two Lockport buildings carry that administration in physical form, and this dossier is built on the pair. The Gaylord Building, a limestone warehouse raised between May and September 1838 under acting Canal Commissioner Jacob Fry at a recorded cost of $4,014.29, stored canal construction materials at the canalside. The Canal Office Building — now the Will County Historical Museum — was built in 1845 in Greek Revival form and served as the Canal Commission's headquarters building; the nomination describes the headquarters building beside the canal at Eighth and State, still housing thousands of canal documents including original plans. The Panic of 1837 had slowed everything, money ran short, and the canal that was supposed to be quick took twelve years. [3, 4, 6]

It opened in April 1848. The LaSalle County Historical Society's canal account names the first passage precisely: on April 10, 1848, the canal boat General Fry, towed by the propeller A. Rossiter, arrived in Chicago from Lockport. The completed work ran 96 miles from Bridgeport to LaSalle-Peru on the Illinois River, with 17 locks and four aqueducts covering about 140 feet of elevation difference, at a total cost the same account gives as $6,170,226. Boats of about 100 feet were pulled by mules from the towpath, the full trip took about 24 hours at five miles an hour, and the canal operated about eight and a half months of the year. With its opening, a continuous waterway ran from New York to New Orleans through the interior — the nomination's judgment is that, as the Erie Canal had secured New York City, the I&M guaranteed Chicago's place as the key transportation center of the Middle West. Grain, stone, lumber, and coal moved on it, and towns the commissioners had platted to raise construction funds — Lockport, Morris, Ottawa, LaSalle among them — became canal towns in fact. The labor that dug it was immigrant labor in large part: the National Park Service heritage-area history names workers from Ireland, Sweden, Germany, Poland, and other nations, some of them Erie Canal veterans, brought in to dig earth and cut bedrock for the canal prism. Their individual names are largely absent from the sources checked this run, and that absence is part of the record here. [5, 6, 9]

The headquarters outlasted the canal's best years. The Canal Commission operated from the Lockport office until 1871, when control passed to the state; in 1876 a superintendent's residence was added to the office building and a bank vault installed to store canal records, and the building later served as headquarters for the successor Sanitary and Ship Canal. The Gaylord Building, surplus once construction ended in 1848, was rented and then sold by the Canal Board of Trustees and passed through commercial uses. Railroads took the passenger traffic by the early 1850s and then the freight; the canal's own end date is given three ways in the record (see Gaps), closing to commerce in 1933 by the Park Service account. What Lockport kept was the fabric: a downtown of canal-era limestone that became the Lockport Historic District, listed in 1975, with the canal office listed separately in 1972 and the canal's locks and towpath designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964. In 1984 Congress made the corridor the Illinois & Michigan Canal National Heritage Corridor — the first National Heritage Area in the United States — with the Canal Corridor Association as coordinating entity. [4, 5, 6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Lockport sits on the Des Plaines River valley at the canal's summit-to-river transition, about 30 miles southwest of Chicago. The canal prism, locks, and towpath run through the town; the Gaylord Building stands canalside at 8th Street, the Canal Office at 8th and State, and Lincoln Landing, an outdoor museum space, lies just outside the Gaylord Building. The National Register nomination, written when much of the upper canal near Chicago was already eroded and overgrown, judged the Lockport-to-LaSalle reach the surviving one: considerable historic features extant, the canal maintained in places as parkland, retaining enough original fabric to be preserved for recreational use. That judgment still describes the visit — Lockport is where the canal is a town, not only a trail. [1, 2, 6]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The paired downtown anchors above — Gaylord Building, 200 W. 8th St. (National Trust-owned, operated by the Gaylord Building Historic Site nonprofit), and Will County Historical Museum and Research Center, 803 S. State St. (Will County Historical Society) — plus the public streets of the Lockport Historic District and the canal towpath trail past the Gaylord Building on the old canal bank. The restaurant and art gallery inside the Gaylord Building are commercial tenants on their own hours and are not the historic gate; the first-floor canal gallery is. [1, 3, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Start at the Will County museum in the Canal Office Building, on its Thu–Sun gate, and start with the paper: this is the room the canal was administered from, and its research collection holds the canal's own documents. Read the headquarters story there first — design, finance, construction — while standing in the office that did it. Then walk the three blocks to the Gaylord Building and take the first-floor *Illinois Passage* gallery as the second half of the same sentence: the warehouse that stored the materials, now explaining what the materials built. Note the building itself as an exhibit — 1838 limestone, built in a single construction season for a recorded $4,014.29, derelict by the early 1980s, and restored in a four-year, $2.8 million effort led by Gaylord Donnelley that reopened it in 1987 and received a President's Award for Historic Preservation; the National Trust presents it as a national model of adaptive reuse. **Strongest story moment · The office and the warehouse.** Most canal sites give a visitor a lock or a boat; Lockport gives the enterprise's two working ends — the desk where the canal was financed and run, and the warehouse floor where its construction was staged — on the same short walk, with the canal itself outside the door. Finish outside: Lincoln Landing, then the towpath along the canal prism, walking the bank the mules walked, in the reach the Register nomination singled out as the one that survived. Reflection posture at the labor history: the canal was hand-dug, largely by immigrant crews whose names this record mostly does not keep. [1, 2, 3, 4, 6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Gaylord Building hours gap, printed in full: the building's own site, checked this run, heads a section "Historic Site Hours" and then prints only phone, fax, email, and address — no hours. The National Trust listing gives Tuesday–Friday 9 a.m.–5 p.m.; place-data listings add Saturday and Sunday 12–5 p.m. and a Monday closure. No source checked this run reconciles these. The dossier therefore gates the Gaylord gallery by phone (815) 838-9400 and does not publish a Gaylord hours line as settled. [1, 3]
- Canal Office address conflict: the operator's own page gives 803 S. State St.; the Enjoy Illinois listing gives 803 N. State St. for the same museum and phone. The operator's address governs in the trip gate; the conflict is printed here. [4, 8]
- Town-founding date variance: the Will County operator page says Lockport was laid out by the canal commissioners in 1837; the headquarters-building summary says the commission laid out the town in 1836, the year of groundbreaking; the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation community page says platted in 1837. This dossier uses 1837 in the Story (two of three, including the operator) and records 1836 here. [4, 7]
- Headquarters chronology variance: the NR nomination states headquarters was established at Lockport in 1839 and describes the headquarters building as operating until the 1960s; the Will County page dates the office building's construction without a year in the text checked and the building summary dates it 1845, with commission operation until the 1871 state transfer. The dossier distinguishes the headquarters function (1839, per the nomination) from the surviving office building (1845) and does not merge them. [4, 6]
- Canal end-date conflict, three ways: the Will County operator page gives operation 1848–1914; the National Park Service heritage-area page says the canal officially closed to commerce in 1933; the NR nomination's date line gives operation 1848–1936. All three are printed; the Story uses the NPS 1933 form for closure to commerce and claims nothing tighter. [4, 5, 6]
- Gaylord Building post-canal sequence: the building summary records that on completion of the canal in 1848 the building was surplus, briefly rented, then sold by the Canal Board of Trustees; the Trust's page describes later varied commercial tenancy exemplifying the canal's commercial success. Tenant-by-tenant history was not retrieved this run. [3]
- Lock numbering at Lockport: the nomination states the first canal lock was built at Lockport, but no source checked this run establishes a present-day lock-by-lock access inventory for the Lockport reach (which locks survive in visitable form, and their condition). The towpath progression above promises the canal prism and bank, not a specific numbered lock interior. [6]
- Immigrant labor record gap: the NPS heritage history establishes the canal workforce's national origins and Erie Canal experience in general terms; payrolls or named Lockport crew lists were not retrieved this run. No individual digger is named in this dossier, and none should be invented for it. [5, 9]
- Will County museum staffing caveat is the operator's own: the museum is volunteer-based and the operator asks visitors to contact ahead to ensure a volunteer will be present. The Thu–Sun 11–3 line is therefore a published gate, not a guarantee. [4]
