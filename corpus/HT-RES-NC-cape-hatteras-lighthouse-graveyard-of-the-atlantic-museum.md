# Cape Hatteras Lighthouse and Graveyard of the Atlantic Museum

Use the museum’s signed lot just past the building and its guest entrance. The small lot opposite is for beach access. Leave the ferry lanes, beach and shore out of this visit. ## 2. Story

A lighthouse had to stay clear enough to do its work every night. The United States Light-House Board’s 1871 directions required daily dusting of the optical surfaces with a feather brush and specified clean, dry chamois for polishing. A flame in the middle of the glass was only the start. Soot, abrasion and a film of oil could undo the work that carefully shaped prisms were meant to perform. The keeper's instructions are evidence of the job expected, without proving how every shift actually went. [4]

At Cape Hatteras the first tower took a first-order Fresnel lens in 1854. The replacement tower followed in 1870. One NPS timeline describes the first tower’s lens being installed in the replacement tower, while the construction account quoted in the HABS history calls the apparatus received in December 1870 the “new lens.” The same later light station required residents who understood how to care for glass, lamps and the machinery around them. NPS identifies Unaka Benjamin Jennette as principal keeper from 1919 and publishes a photograph of him cleaning the Fresnel lens. He turns the general instructions into the work of a named person on this ground. [5–7]

The assembly survived in pieces. NPS records the optics being removed after vandalism around 1949. In the 2000s the Park Service and state museum worked to bring surviving glass and the pedestal and clockwork together. In May 2024 interim museum director Maria Vann told Public Radio East that the lens remained at the renovated museum and described new interpretation of how it works. The off-tower display lets a visitor look at the equipment a keeper once had to reach by stairs. Check the operator for present display status rather than treating a past loan or reopening report as today’s exhibit guarantee. [8–10]

## 3. Then and now

The striped 1870 tower at Buxton was moved in 1999. Its earlier setting appears in the 1989 survey photograph. The indoor visitor anchor for this account is the state museum in Hatteras village, a separate facility. The tower and the museum have separate operators and access rules. A historic lighthouse setting does not locate the museum entrance. [1, 3, 5, 11–13]

## 4. What survives and where a visitor may stand

- State museum: signed guest lot just past the building, direct guest entrance and open public galleries. Operator asks museum guests to avoid the opposite beach-access lot. [1–2]
- Lens assembly: the restoration and display of surviving parts have documentary support. Current presentation may change. The historical apparatus described here and the tower’s present beacon belong to different periods of operation. [5, 8–10]
- Tower and previous shoreline footprint: shown as historical context. No climb, shoreline walking, ferry travel or original-footprint approach in this entry. [3, 11–12]

## 5. Strongest visit moment

Enter through the museum guest door. Find the lens interpretation if it is on display. Pick out one of the curved glass bands, then read the 1871 cleaning directions. **The clean edge of a single prism** is where the engineering and Jennette’s daily work come together. The story can be read indoors even if the exhibit is temporarily unavailable. [4, 6, 8–10]

## 6. Evidence limits and disagreements

The general NPS FAQ gives December 1, 1870 for the replacement tower beginning operation. The Light-House Board text quoted in the HABS documentation says the light was exhibited on December 16, as does the NPS cultural landscape inventory. Those may represent different milestones, but the underlying Board issue and station log were not independently compared here. The NPS timeline and the HABS quotation also describe the lens's 1870 movement in different terms. Keep the difference visible pending the Board’s transfer or installation record. The 2025 recreation total of 2,684,009 covers the entire national seashore, not this tower or state museum. No comparable door count is verified. [5, 7, 12, 15]
