# HT-RES-NY-fdr-hyde-park — The house that kept growing until it held a presidency
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A farmhouse on a Hudson bluff that was rebuilt three times by three generations — first into an Italianate villa, then into the stuccoed, stone-winged mansion in the 1941 photograph — and that stayed, through a governorship and four presidential terms, the place its owner was born in, governed from, came home to, and chose to be buried in.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a house and its ground: Springwood, the main house of the Home of Franklin D. Roosevelt National Historic Site, at Hyde Park in Dutchess County, New York, on the east bank of the Hudson River about midway between New York City and Albany. The operator is the National Park Service. The NPS directs visitors to begin at the Henry A. Wallace Visitor and Education Center, and the NPS park brochure states that house visits are by guided tour only. The NPS's own fees page states the terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: all visitors must have an entrance pass — a Standard Pass is $15.00 for entry to the Home of Franklin D. Roosevelt for anyone 16 years or older, an Annual Pass (the Roosevelt-Vanderbilt pass) is $45.00, and America the Beautiful passes are also accepted; children under the age of 16 do not need an entrance pass; the park does not accept cash, with passes sold credit/debit only at the Wallace Visitor Center; entrance to the Home is by guided tour only, tickets are sold on a first come, first served basis, and there are no advanced reservations for individuals. The same fees page carries the hours of the adjoining Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library and Museum, operated by the National Archives and Records Administration on a separate 16-acre tract within the estate: open 7 days a week, 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. November–March and 9:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. April–October. The park's mailing address, as the NPS contact page carries it, is 4097 Albany Post Road, Hyde Park, NY 12538, and the NPS directions page places the park entrance on Route 9 at that address, approximately 5 miles north of Poughkeepsie. The site was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000056, per the NPS National Register record).

## 2. The story
The ground came first as paper. The NPS land history of the Roosevelt Estate records that the estate was part of the Great Nine Partners Patent, issued to British land speculators in 1697, and that two years later the speculators subdivided the patent into long, rectangular parcels fronting on the Hudson River, known as water lots. Actual settlement on the lots within the estate did not occur, the NPS land history states, until about 1750, when the Crooke family built a house near the present Red House. The second known house within the estate was the future Springwood house, built by Widow Everson on the Wheeler Place at some point between 1780 and 1793 — a dating the NPS's own sources do not carry consistently, and the disagreement is printed in full in §6 rather than smoothed over here.

By the eve of the Roosevelt purchase, the western water lots had become country estates of wealthy New Yorkers (NPS land history). The old Everson house was owned by Josiah and Mary Wheeler. The nomination adds: Wheeler, who acquired the house in 1845, added a three-story tower to the south end and a servants' wing to the north, and the Wheelers planted the hemlock hedge that survives today. The NPS place page carries the same change in short form: the house was renovated into an Italianate villa about 1850.

James Roosevelt (1828–1900) purchased the Wheeler Place west of the Post Road in **1867**, after a fire at the family's prior home, Mount Hope, and renamed the estate Springwood (NPS land history). The nomination records the family's alterations: a dining-room bay and smoking room in 1887, the present staircase in 1892, the verandah extended in 1893, and the house electrified in 1908.

Two years after the death of his first wife Rebecca in 1878, James Roosevelt married Sara Delano (1854–1941), and on **January 30, 1882**, Franklin Delano Roosevelt was born at Springwood (NPS land history). The nomination states the site's central claim in one sentence: the Home of Franklin D. Roosevelt is the only place in the United States where a President was born, grew to manhood, and lies buried. Upon James Roosevelt's death in 1900, he left the Wheeler Place and Home Farm to Franklin, subject to the life estate of Sara (NPS land history). Franklin married Eleanor Roosevelt in 1905, and the family made the Springwood house, shared with Sara, their home.

The house the visitor sees is largely the product of one campaign. The NPS land history dates the expansion of the Italianate Springwood house into a Colonial Revival–style mansion to **1915–16**, undertaken by Franklin working closely with his mother. The nomination describes what that campaign did: the weatherboards were removed and the central portion stuccoed; a tower was built on the north to match the one on the south; a large playroom was added on the third floor; stone wings were added at each end — the south wing with a library on the first floor and three bedrooms on the second, the north wing with a dining-sitting room for servants and a small schoolroom below and five bedrooms and baths for servants above. The NPS Springwood place page adds that the roof of the central block was raised to create a full third floor, the entrance hall expanded, and a living room and large suite of bedrooms added, and that the enlargement, completed in 1916, accommodated Franklin and Eleanor's growing family while creating a more formal residence that reflected Franklin's political aspirations. The design work was by Hoppin and Koen of New York City, named as builder/architect in the nomination alongside the specific dates ca. 1800 and 1915. The interior is furnished, the NPS place page states, according to its appearance about 1941.

Franklin's public life ran through the house. The nomination records his battle from 1921 to 1928 to overcome polio and return to politics; the election-eve torchlight parades from 1910; election returns kept in the dining room during his presidency; and fireside chats delivered from the small study. The NPS land history follows the estate outward in the same years: a forest management plan begun in 1911 and plantations from 1912, on an estate it puts at 1,521 acres at its height, with the nomination counting over 200,000 trees planted and tree-replacement provisions written into Roosevelt's deed.

His presidential library was built in **1939–40** near the house, and he gave its 16 acres to the federal government under a 1939 joint resolution of Congress (NPS land history). In December 1943 he gave the Springwood house and surrounding 33 acres to the federal government, subject to the family life estate, and in 1944 the National Park Service designated the property a national historic site.
 Franklin Delano Roosevelt died on **April 12, 1945**, at Warm Springs, Georgia (NPS burial-site page). By his own written instructions of December 26, 1937, his body was to be buried "where the sun dial stands in the garden," and he was buried in the Rose Garden at Hyde Park on April 15, 1945, under a plain white marble monument executed to his own design — 8 feet long, 4 feet wide, and 3 feet high, without carving or decoration — installed in the fall of 1945 (NPS burial-site page). Eleanor Roosevelt was interred beside him upon her death in 1962, as he had wished. The National Park Service opened the site to the public in 1946, one year after his death (NPS Springwood place page and NPS land history).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a two-story wood-frame farmhouse of 46 by 39 feet, heavy timber frame filled with crude bricks and clad with wide boards, facing east toward the Albany Post Road and away from the river (NRHP nomination). **Now:** that farmhouse survives as the stuccoed central block of a Colonial Revival mansion, flanked by the fieldstone wings of 1915, its roof raised to a full third story, entered through a four-columned portico over a balustraded terrace.

**Then:** an Italianate villa with a south tower a story above the rest of the house — a profile the nomination says resembled "a train of cars" when James Roosevelt bought it in 1867. **Now:** the matched north tower of 1915 balances the composition the nomination credits the Hoppin and Koen renovation with tying together, a jumbled house given order and symmetry.

**Then:** a working estate reaching 1,522 acres at Franklin Roosevelt's death (NPS land history). **Now:** the National Park Service preserves 898 acres of it in two park units — this site and Val-Kill — with the library's 16 acres held separately by the National Archives in the same landscape.

**Then:** a glass-negative HABS view of July 1941 (see §7): the east front in full leaf, flags over the portico, potted palms on the terrace, the house as it stood in the last summer before the war. **Now:** the same east front, kept as a museum house furnished to its appearance about 1941, entered only on a ranger-guided tour.

## 4. What's still there
- The Springwood house itself: the original farmhouse core within the stuccoed central block, the Wheeler south tower and the matching 1915 north tower, and the two fieldstone wings of the 1915 Hoppin and Koen campaign (NRHP nomination; NPS Springwood place page)
- The interior as furnished to its appearance about 1941, including the study from which fireside chats were delivered, the Snuggery with its 1887 mantelpiece, and the rope-pulled lift Roosevelt used after polio, in the 1915 north wing (NRHP nomination; NPS Springwood place page)
- The Rose Garden north of the house, inside the hemlock hedge the Wheelers planted, with the graves of Franklin and Eleanor Roosevelt under the plain white marble monument Roosevelt designed, placed in October/fall 1945 (NPS Rose Garden page; NPS burial-site page; NRHP nomination)
- The Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library and Museum on its 16-acre tract within the estate, operated by the National Archives and Records Administration — a separate operator on the same ground (NPS land history; NPS fees page)
- The Henry A. Wallace Visitor and Education Center, where the NPS directs visitors to begin and where house-tour tickets are sold (NPS park brochure; NPS fees page)
- The planted landscape: plantations, orchards, and specimen trees across the preserved estate, planted under Roosevelt's own forestry program from 1911 onward (NPS land history; NRHP nomination)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Wallace Visitor Center, 4097 Albany Post Road:** Start where the NPS directs visitors to start — entrance pass in hand, house-tour ticket taken first come, first served, as the NPS fees page states, in a park that takes no cash.
2. **At the east front:** Read the house as its nomination teaches — farmhouse core, Wheeler tower, 1915 wings — from the lawn the July 1941 HABS negative was made across (see §7), before going inside.
3. **Inside Springwood, on the guided tour:** The entrance hall, the study of the fireside chats, the Snuggery, and the lift in the north wing — the house as a record of a man modifying his own environment after 1921, in the NPS place page's framing.
4. **At the Rose Garden:** The hemlock hedge, the sundial Roosevelt named in his 1937 burial instructions, and the unadorned marble block he designed — his grave, and Eleanor's beside him from 1962.
5. **At the library, on the same estate:** The building Roosevelt built in 1939–40 for his own papers, under its own operator and its own hours as the NPS fees page carries them — the archive next door to the house.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The original house's construction date is carried three different ways by the NPS's own sources, and all three print here unmerged. The NPS Springwood place page states the original house was built about 1793. The NPS land history states Widow Everson built the future Springwood house on the Wheeler Place at some point between 1780 and 1793. The National Register nomination states the main house was originally a two-story wood-frame house built sometime between 1790 and 1805, and its specific-dates line gives ca. 1800. No one of these is chosen over the others in this draft.

The final remodeling is likewise framed three ways. The nomination dates the last major structural changes to 1915 and names 1915 as a specific date. The NPS land history dates the expansion to 1915–16. The NPS Springwood place page states the enlargement was completed in 1916. The campaign is one campaign; its dating prints as each source carries it.

The site's federal chronology prints as its sources carry it, in steps that are easy to conflate and are not conflated here: the gift of the house and 33 acres in December 1943 (NPS land history); designation as a national historic site in 1944 (NPS land history) — the NPS repository finding aids phrase the designation as in 1943 at Roosevelt's direction, which is printed here as that page's wording rather than reconciled; opening to the public in 1946 (NPS place page and land history). The often-repeated shorthand that the site was "established in 1945" appears in lead-list sources and is not used in this draft, because no NPS source consulted states it in that form.

The presidential library's dates also carry a shorthand conflict: the NPS land history describes the subdivided parcel as containing "his presidential library that opened in 1940" and dates the building's construction to 1939–40. The library building was in fact dedicated on June 30, 1941, the date the Franklin D. Roosevelt Presidential Library and Museum itself carries for its dedication. Both are printed; "opened in 1940" is not silently converted into the dedication date, nor the reverse.

Estate acreage varies by date and scope and no single figure is asserted: the nomination gives 264.51 acres for the site at nomination and 33.23 for the original gift; the land history gives 1,522 acres at Roosevelt's death and 898 preserved today across two units.

The NPS fees page consulted for §1 carries no fixed clock times for house tours — only the guided-tour-only, first-come terms — so no tour times are printed. The nomination's geographical-data section carries UTM references in a form too degraded in the scanned record to transcribe reliably; no coordinates are taken from it, and the locator below prints none (see §10).
