# HT-RES-ID-pulaski-tunnel-trail — The trail to the tunnel where forty men lived

An abandoned prospect hole — no ore ever found in it — where a blacksmith-ranger got his crew of 45 inside on the worst night in American wildland fire history, and forty people walked out.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a walk: the Pulaski Tunnel Trail (Trail No. 38), two miles each way from a trailhead just south of Wallace, Idaho, ending at an overlook across the creek from the Nicholson adit portal. The trailhead carries the interpretive sign and map — the planning half of the pilgrimage made physical. The records name no hours, fees, or parking specifics for the trailhead, and none are printed here. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984 as the Edward Pulaski Tunnel and Placer Creek Escape Route. The record's visit is to the overlook: the adit portal is viewed from across the creek, roughly ten feet above it — you stand where the rescue parties came up the canyon.

## 2. The story
The summer of 1910 was drought. Pulaski's own words: *“For weeks there had been no rain and the woods were drier than I had ever seen them.”* Across the Northern Rockies more than seventeen hundred fires were burning. On the afternoon of **August 20, 1910**, a cold front hit with winds most sources put at **70 mph** — Idaho's legislature says up to 80; Pulaski called it “a terrific hurricane” — and the fires joined into one. In roughly two days the Big Burn burned about **three million acres** across northern Idaho, western Montana, and eastern Washington. The total toll is given as 85 to 87; the figure that holds steady is **78 firefighters dead**.

Pulaski — Edward Crockett Pulaski, born February 9, **1866**, in Green Springs, Ohio (some retellings say 1868, apparently from obituary math) — had prospected this region for over twenty-five years: miner, packer, railroad worker, sawmill and steam engineer, smelterman, blacksmith. He joined the three-year-old Forest Service in the summer of 1908. That day he had on the order of 150 men distributed across his district. As the blowup came up the canyon he gathered the men he could reach and ran: *“my one hope was to reach an old mine tunnel which I knew to be not far from us. We raced for it.”*

Inside, as fire gas and smoke filled the tunnel and one man rushed the entrance, Pulaski wrote: *“I drew my revolver and said, ‘The first man who tries to leave this tunnel I will shoot.’ I did not have to use my gun.”* He ordered the men face-down on the floor, then fought for the portal himself — wet blankets hung over the opening, his hat filled again and again with water from the mine seep — until he too went down unconscious. He came to at five in the morning to a voice saying “Come outside boys, the boss is dead.” His reply, verbatim: *“Like hell he is.”*

The trailhead is the planning half of the pilgrimage made physical: the map, the canyon, the West Fork of Placer Creek, and the route Pulaski's men ran on August 20, 1910 — and crawled back down over burning logs the next morning, until a rescue party met them. Injured men from the crew had reached Wallace by nine that Sunday morning; the supervisor sent two doctors in on foot with medicines, blankets, and provisions, while a seventeen-man crew cut trail all day Sunday, Sunday night, Monday, and Monday night to get the rest out. The dead came out on packhorses.

The site was lost for decades after the fire, rediscovered by a Forest Service archaeologist in **1979**, and restored by a citizens' group — the Pulaski Project — with the Forest Service. The trail was completed on the centennial of the blowup, **August 20, 2010**.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a raw prospect adit — the Nicholson adit, roughly 250 feet deep, six feet high, five feet wide, portal about ten feet above the creek — with burning timbers at its mouth and a creek outside running warm and full of ash. **Now:** a restored, National Register-listed site (1984), lost for decades and rediscovered by a Forest Service archaeologist in 1979, reached by the Pulaski Tunnel Trail (Trail No. 38), completed on the centennial — August 20, 2010 — and drawing thousands of hikers a year.

**Then:** an unmarked prospect hole in a burned canyon, its location carried in memory. **Now:** a signed, maintained trail drawing thousands of hikers a year to stand at an overlook across the creek from a hole in the ground where forty men lived through the worst night in the history of their trade.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The headcount, honestly: Pulaski's 1923 account says he collected 45 men; one was killed by a falling tree on the way in; five died inside; 39 crewmen and Pulaski walked out. The Forest Service's current account says he “saved all but six of his 45-man crew” — counting the man lost on the run in. The Forest History Society's figure of 43 is an outlier with no primary source behind it. Later historians add an attempt toward Wallace and the War Eagle mine, both cut off; Pulaski's own telling mentions only the one hope.

The trailhead record flags no gaps of its own. The two source records carry slightly different coordinates — the trailhead's and the overlook's — and the locator below prints each against the ground it belongs to rather than merging them.
