## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** a mining town's history does not end at the shift whistle. What happened at home when a miner's earnings disappeared belongs in the same story as the ore.

**Catherine “Kayo” Banovich-Lydon**, interviewed by **Robert D. McCracken on October 17 and 24 and December 7, 1987**, remembered her mother **Miruna Banovich** supporting five children after her father Mike's death from silicosis in 1921. Her household account connects boarding, gardening and family work. The daughter says: **“But it was enough for us to live.”** This answers a question about boarders' payments, whose amount she explicitly could not remember. [5, PDF pages 10 and 32, counting cover as page 1]

The quote's power is its limit: enough to live is not evidence of a comfortable income. This entry interprets the household as part of the mining economy without pretending to reconstruct its accounts. It is distinct from Elko's hospitality story: here the organizing question is what a family had to absorb after occupational loss, not the persistence of a dining tradition.

The transcript was read, not the recording heard. No accent, tone, recording timestamp or staged voice performance is claimed.

## 3. Then / now

McCracken's later town history presents mining widows as an important part of Tonopah's social economy. His account describes Miruna's food production and boarders and the children's collection of restaurant vegetable remnants for animals. It also presents her generosity to people unable to pay. This is a historical synthesis drawing on the interview, **not an independent second witness or a household ledger**. [6]

Today the Central Nevada Historical Society presents regional history through its museum and public research library. [7] Its exhibit description includes ethnic communities and everyday mining-camp life. Outdoor displays include a **reconstructed** town; original objects and reconstructed settings should not be conflated. [8]

No present display specifically naming Miruna or Catherine was verified. The museum supplies a lawful interpretive setting; the published sources supply the named story. Neither proves that an original Banovich building survives, admits visitors or is represented by a particular cabin.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The interview is edited retrospective testimony, not a verified census of local experience. Its preface explicitly cautions that statements may contain errors. One passage gives approximately ten boarders; the later synthesis says approximately a dozen. Keep both estimates attributed rather than inventing a precise capacity. [5,6]
