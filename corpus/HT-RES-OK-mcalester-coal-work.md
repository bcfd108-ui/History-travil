# McAlester coal country — wages, water, and the newspaper in Krebs

## 2 · The story

### Coal work followed families home

Krebs developed as a coal camp east of McAlester. The Gateway’s archive history describes the Osage Coal and Mining Company owning housing and paying miners in scrip. Johnnie E. Sewell’s mining-history article puts living conditions next to wages. In his account, households in some Oklahoma company towns shared wells. He quotes an unidentified miner’s joke that the water was so hard it took “a sledge hammer before drinking it.” It is the miner’s phrase as transmitted by Sewell, whose footnote groups published and Indian-Pioneer references. Treat it as remembered working-town speech, with the speaker unidentified. [3–5]

The same coal employment drew people from different places. The Oklahoma Historical Society distinguishes the early English and Irish miners at Krebs from later recruitment including Italians and people from other European countries and Mexico. Work organized a town whose food businesses, families and shops carried their own histories. OHS records a United Mine Workers local in 1898 and a later interurban link to McAlester. [3–5]

### A town speaking about work

In June 1894, during a coal strike, Krebs residents and businesspeople petitioned federal Indian agent Dew M. Wisdom. In the historical article that quotes their petition, they protested the prospect of miners being driven from a place “in which they have labored so long to accumulate so little.” The authors were petitioners speaking about the miners. The petition is also evidence that a wage dispute could become a dispute about a worker’s ability to remain with a household and community. The article’s quotation and its archival note were read. The underlying petition was not retrieved. [13]

### The inspector’s report and the publisher's promise

Ed Boyle put his name and job on the front page of the May 9, 1912 *Oklahoma Miner*, published in Krebs. He signed the official mine report as “Chief Mine Inspector of Oklahoma.” A subscription notice on the same page named Boyle as editor and owner and promised to devote half of subscription receipts to the relief fund for widows and orphans. The page shows an official and a newspaper trying to make a disaster publicly accountable in the same issue that solicited help. It does not establish how much was collected or paid out. [4,6–7]

For this paragraph the location matters. Boyle’s 1912 report concerns McCurtain in Haskell County, not the January 7, 1892 disaster in Krebs. The Krebs newspaper linked the town to the wider mining district. Reading the page here brings the labor press into the town’s story and keeps the two groups of affected families distinct. [3,6,8–9]

### A household waiting for a name

The January 9, 1892 *Daily Eugene Guard* carried early dispatches from the McAlester area. Amid incompatible estimates, it described wives and mothers waiting as people were brought to the surface. The numbers were still changing and the paper said the exact toll was unavailable. Federal and state compilers continued to give different totals years later. At the museum, keep the family at the center of that passage and read the totals with their sources. [8–10]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The Oklahoma Department of Mines table records **96** killed at Krebs on January 7, 1892. The federal Bureau of Mines retrospective lists **100**, and OHS describes approximately **100**. The contemporary *Daily Eugene Guard* dispatch offered provisional figures including more than 300 and later a probability of 200 dead in the same column. Those estimates are early reports, and the dispatch itself acknowledges the uncertainty. OCR on its mine designation is unclear. Federal and archive records identify No. 11. The verified family-by-family roster has not been recovered. [3,8–12]
- Ed Boyle is a named mining official, editor and first-person report signer. No named rank-and-file Krebs miner’s original testimony was retrieved. Sewell publishes the water joke as an unidentified miner’s words. The *Oklahoma Miner* print was read via the Gateway’s OCR, supported by the state office chronology. [4–7]
- The museum page lists ordinary opening hours independently of scheduled tours. A holiday listing, object list, surviving mine footprint and lawful historical vantage remain unverified. [1–2,11–12]
