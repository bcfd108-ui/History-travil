## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** The same place can be a visitor's spectacular image and a people's continuing home. A responsible visit starts by recognizing who can speak for that home and who decides what is shared.

**Named, Tribe-published account:** The official Havasupai website links the Tribe's June 7, 2022 Senate hearing testimony under **Chairman Thomas Siyuja Sr.'s** name. Its short statement **“In our Canyon home, water is life.”** expresses the connection between homeland, identity and future generations. The testimony argues for protection of water and cultural continuity. It is a dated Tribal advocacy document, not a present legal-status determination or a claim of universally agreed scientific findings. [3]

**Continuing community voice:** The Tribal Council's August 13, 2026 broadband announcement includes **Chairwoman Melinda Yaiva's** statement linking connectivity with education, health, safety and cultural revitalization. **Ophelia Watahomigie-Corliss**, identified there as Tribal Broadband Connectivity Program Director, describes nearly eight years of planning and calls the first-phase groundbreaking an important community and sovereignty milestone. The announcement describes work underway and intended future completion, not a verified completed network. [4]

These two sources are published directly by the Tribe. They show a community acting in the present, rather than existing only in an account of loss. Office titles are attached to the documents' dates; no historical title is silently treated as a current one.

**Historical memory with separate provenance:** A 2021 Havasupai Tribal Council news release, republished by ICT, remembers **Rex Tilousi** as a longtime advocate and former chairman. It identifies his work as a Grand Canyon cultural-interpreter ranger beginning in 1998. This is a Tribal-authored remembrance hosted by a separate publisher, not his recorded speech and not an invitation to any personal or commemorative site. [5]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Public interpretation is most useful here when it preserves a distinction: NPS operates a visitor institution; the Havasupai Tribe describes its own priorities and governs its own community. Collaboration does not erase sovereignty, and a film representing several communities does not make their voices interchangeable. Havasupai and Hualapai are not substitute names for one government.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the Tribal remembrance records a named person's work bringing cultural interpretation into the park institution. [5]

**Now:** the Tribe publishes its own statements about protecting continuity and developing its future, while the NPS center offers a collaborative film. [1,3,4] The comparison concerns authorship and authority, not a claim that past conflicts have been resolved.

The NPS description credits the film to the Intertribal Working Group, NPS, Grand Canyon Conservancy and Bristlecone Media. [1] The film has not been watched in this pass. No individual Havasupai statement, image or permission within it is reconstructed from its title or promotional description.

## 4. What's still there
The verified public resource is a visitor-center interpretation program and the separately accessible published Tribal accounts. The visitor center is not treated as the original setting of any quoted statement.

The official cultural demonstration page says winter demonstrations **may** occur at the visitor center. Its displayed detailed schedule concerns April 2026. That does not establish a November or winter Havasupai event, nor permission to record a participant. [6]

No historic structure, cultural object, community location or ancestral feature has been selected for physical exploration. The absence of such a route is deliberate: one can listen to a public account without claiming access to its places or practices.
