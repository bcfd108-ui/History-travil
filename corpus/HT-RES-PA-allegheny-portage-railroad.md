# HT-RES-PA-allegheny-portage-railroad — Canal boats hauled over a mountain on rails, ten stair-steps at a time
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

For twenty years the only way across the Allegheny Mountains was a staircase: canal boats floated onto railroad cars at Hollidaysburg, winched up five inclined planes by stationary steam engines, let down five more, and floated again at Johnstown — 36.65 miles of railroad doing the work a canal could not.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a walk on National Park Service ground at the summit level of the old Portage: the visitor center, the Lemon House, the Engine House No. 6 Exhibit Shelter at the head of Incline 6, and the Skew Arch Bridge at its foot, with the Staple Bend Tunnel preserved as a separate unit of the same historic site nearer Johnstown. The operator is the National Park Service — Allegheny Portage Railroad National Historic Site, off U.S. Route 22 about twelve miles west of Altoona. The primary records consulted for this draft name no hours or fees, and none are printed here. The ground itself is the exhibit: the trace of Incline 6, the engine house foundations under their shelter, the tavern that fed the passengers, and the one road bridge built for the line. The railroad has been gone since the 1850s; nothing here is an operating railway, and the visit stays on the park's trails and exhibit ground.

## 2. The story
Pennsylvania built the Main Line of Public Works to answer New York's Erie Canal, completed in 1825. The legislature authorized the Main Line in February 1826 — canals from Philadelphia toward Pittsburgh — and ran straight into the problem the National Park Service history states flatly: "How could a canal system be built through or over the mountains?" A four-mile tunnel under the mountain was considered and rejected as costly and unrealistic. The answer adopted was a railroad of ten inclined planes, five ascending and five descending, modeled on a similar system in England.

The Allegheny Portage Railroad ran 36 miles by the NPS history's round figure — 36.65 miles in the NPS Skew Arch Bridge account and the engine house overview — connecting the Hollidaysburg Canal Basin in the east with the canal basin at Johnstown in the west. Between the planes lay eleven levels, relatively level passages ranging, in the engine house account, from 0.15 mile to 14 miles. At the head of each plane stood an engine house with a stationary steam engine of 30 to 35 horsepower, driving an endless hemp rope — about 7 inches in circumference, later replaced with wire cable — that pulled cars up the plane and lowered them down, ascending and descending cars counterbalanced against each other where possible. The inclines were generally about half a mile long; by law their gradient could be no greater than 12 percent, and the steepest, Incline 8, ran at 9.9 percent. On the levels, cars were moved first by horses and later by locomotives, which lacked the power for the planes themselves. The system carried railroad cars and, once the sectional-boat system was worked out in the mid-1830s, canal boats themselves — floated onto flat cars in sections at one basin and rejoined to float on at the other.

The works along the line were stone on a large scale: the engine house overview counts four massive stone viaducts, more than 72 stone culverts, ten stationary steam-powered engine houses — and the first railroad tunnel in the country, the Staple Bend Tunnel, some 900 feet long near Johnstown (the early NPS survey form gives it as "a 900-foot tunnel, perhaps the first railroad tunnel in America"; the park's current accounts state it without the hedge). The railroad cost $1,828,461.38 of the Public Works system's $16,504,655.84.

The Portage officially opened on March 18, 1834. Philadelphia to Pittsburgh, twenty-three days by wagon in good conditions, became a four-day journey. It was dangerous work as well as ingenious: on the morning of May 4, 1852, a boiler exploded at Plane No. 6, killing three workers — Owen Reilly, Mathew Purcell, and Felix Handlin — and throwing a 500-pound piece of the boiler 100 feet down the plane; the other engine at the plane was unharmed and trains passed as usual that evening.

At the summit level the railroad acquired a tavern. By 1834 Samuel Lemon had built a substantial stone house east of the summit, and he and his family lived in the Lemon House from 1834 to 1847, operating a tavern that served passengers of the Portage Railroad and wayfarers of the Northern Turnpike alike.

Where the turnpike itself crossed the railroad, at Incline 6, the two systems had to be threaded through each other. On July 15, 1832, J. Fenlon, A. and J. Darlin, and R. Kininmouth won the contract to build "A Stone Bridge which will be require for the passage of the Turnpike road over the Rail Way on Section No. 36 of the Portage." In 1833 the design was changed to accommodate a bend in the Huntington, Cambria and Indiana Turnpike — hence the skew. The construction journal recorded on May 21, 1833, the expense of "Taking up & relaying masonry in consequence of alteration in place." The finished Skew Arch Bridge is 60 feet 5 inches long on its south elevation, 54 feet 11 inches on its north, and 22 feet 2 inches high; its stones were fitted and laid diagonally by hand without mortar, its imposts offset, L-shaped stones reinforcing its corners. It was the only road bridge purposely built along the Portage.

The Portage's replacement was built by the system it had served. On February 15, 1854, the Pennsylvania Railroad completed Horseshoe Curve and with it an all-rail line with no inclines and no transfers between the five divisions of the Main Line. The Commonwealth, after repeated attempts to sell, passed the Act of May 16, 1857; the Pennsylvania Railroad purchased the canal system for $7,500,000 on June 25, 1857, and took possession on August 1, 1857. The NPS history dates the Portage's operation "a little over 20 years from 1834–1857."

The ground came back into public hands in pieces. The National Park Service acquired the Lemon House in 1966; it served as the park visitor center until a new visitor center complex was built in 1992, and after a major restoration it reopened on May 10, 1997, restored to its 1840s appearance. The Skew Arch Bridge was stabilized and repaired in 1979. The Engine House No. 6 foundations — the original engine house there was completed by May 30, 1834, under an April 1, 1833 contract — survive under an exhibit shelter, with a working-model stationary engine and gears built by park volunteer Fred Connacher.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a working staircase of ten planes, hemp ropes seven inches around, stationary engines at every head, sectional canal boats riding flat cars over a 1,400-foot climb from Hollidaysburg and a near-1,200-foot drop to Johnstown. **Now:** a National Historic Site at the summit level — the trace of Incline 6, a shelter over Engine House No. 6's foundations, the Lemon House restored to its 1840s face, and the Skew Arch Bridge standing in the middle of old Route 22 — with the Staple Bend Tunnel preserved in a separate unit east of Johnstown.

**Then:** the turnpike bending over the railroad on a bridge whose arch had to be twisted to fit the road's curve, the stones laid diagonally by hand without mortar in 1833. **Now:** the same bridge, stabilized and repointed in 1979, carrying no traffic at all — preserved between the lanes of the old highway that replaced both the turnpike and the railroad.

## 4. What's still there
- The Skew Arch Bridge (1833) at the foot of Incline 6 — the only road bridge purposely built along the Portage, 60 feet 5 inches on its south elevation and 22 feet 2 inches high, stabilized and repaired in 1979
- The Lemon House — Samuel Lemon's stone tavern at the summit level, on its original location, acquired by the NPS in 1966 and restored to its 1840s appearance, reopened May 10, 1997
- The Engine House No. 6 Exhibit Shelter at the head of Incline 6, preserving the original engine house foundation stones, with a model stationary steam engine and gears exhibit
- The trace of Incline 6 itself, from the engine house head down to the Skew Arch Bridge
- Stone sleepers along the line — the large cut-stone blocks that held the Portage's rails in place of wooden ties, visible at the engine house site and along the trace
- The Staple Bend Tunnel, the first railroad tunnel constructed in the United States, preserved in a separate unit of the historic site east of Johnstown
- The visitor center complex (1992) at the summit level, with the park's exhibits and film

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Summit Level Visitor Center:** Start with the system whole — ten planes, eleven levels, two canal basins — before walking any single piece of it.
2. **At the Engine House No. 6 Exhibit Shelter:** Stand where the stationary engine stood at the head of the plane and read the machinery problem: 30 to 35 horsepower, an endless rope, and cars counterbalanced against each other.
3. **Down the trace of Incline 6:** Walk the plane the rope worked, past the stone sleepers, toward the bridge at its foot.
4. **At the Skew Arch Bridge:** Read the skew itself — offset imposts, stones laid diagonally without mortar — where the turnpike and the railroad had to cross without stopping each other.
5. **At the Lemon House:** End at the human scale of the system — the tavern that fed and lodged the passengers the planes carried.
6. **The Staple Bend Tunnel, as the park's own records shape it:** a separate unit of the same historic site, east toward Johnstown — its own ground, visited on its own approach, not a continuation of the summit walk.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The railroad's length is carried two ways by the same operator: the NPS history page gives "36 miles in length," while the NPS Skew Arch Bridge account and the engine house overview both give 36.65 miles. The longer figure is the one printed in this draft's narrative; the round figure is preserved here rather than silently harmonized.

The end of operation is likewise carried two ways. The NPS history states the Portage "was in operation for a little over 20 years from 1834–1857," and the purchase record is precise — Act of May 16, 1857, purchase June 25, 1857, possession August 1, 1857. Other summaries date the end of Portage operation to 1854, when Horseshoe Curve opened and took the through traffic. The primary records consulted here support the 1857 purchase, not a specific last day of operation; no closing date is asserted beyond it.

The Staple Bend Tunnel's priority is stated with a hedge in the early NPS survey record — "perhaps the first railroad tunnel in America" — and without one in the park's current accounts ("the first railroad tunnel constructed in the United States"). Both wordings are printed above as the record carries them.

The Lemon House carries its own documented gaps, and the NPS states them itself: there is "a great scarcity of information available about the home," plans or contracts for building it "remain elusive," documentary evidence is lacking "even as to the exact year the house was built," and no photographs, drawings, or written accounts of its interior exist. The 1840s restoration rests on archaeology, period artwork, and documentation of other local taverns — the NPS says so, and this draft does not improve on it.

No drawings or specifications for structures along the Allegheny Portage Railroad have been found, per the NPS engine house account, despite repeated investigations — including, per the Skew Arch Bridge account, for the bridge itself. No coordinates are printed: no primary source consulted for this draft carries latitude or longitude for the site.
