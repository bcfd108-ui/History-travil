# USS Cairo and the crew that left a working gunboat behind

## 2. Story

Early on December 12, 1862, Lieutenant Commander Thomas O. Selfridge took the Union ironclad Cairo up the Yazoo with four other vessels. The next day he reported their task and the loss. In his report, preserved in a published modern transcription, he recalls “two sudden explosions in quick succession.” The officer described water over the forecastle and crew members manning both the hand and steam pumps. Moving sick crew members, arms and people to other vessels and boats took priority. He estimated that Cairo disappeared in about twelve minutes. The NPS account states that everyone survived. [4–5]

Selfridge named the vessels and officers around him, including Captain Walke and Captain Hoel. He credited his own crew’s conduct without naming the individual people on the pumps. The objects later recovered allow a different nervous system of shipboard life to come into view. The museum records ordinary possessions and tools as well as weapons. A gunboat was also a workplace of meals, small tasks and watches. The full individual roster link on the NPS account is in transfer. Names and roles for the pump crews remain unverified. [3–5]

About a century later, park historian Edwin C. Bearss and companions Don Jacks and Warren Grabau sought the wreck. NPS dates their initial identification to 1956 and confirmation by the recovery of an armored port cover three years later. A 1964 lifting attempt damaged the waterlogged hull. The recovery team brought it out in three sections. The preserved display therefore has two histories in its fabric: the ironclad’s Civil War service and the engineering and conservation needed to bring it out of the river. [5]

## 3. Then and now

The vessel sank in the Yazoo. The original loss ground, recovered vessel, and current interpretive exhibit are separate. The display incorporates recovered components and support material. NPS documents the removal and treatment of the vessel’s armor after the recovery. The living crew and the twentieth-century recovery workers are part of the story of what visitors see described in the current exhibit documentation. [3–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

Selfridge described musketry that he initially interpreted as fire from shore and then attributed to the Marmora firing at a floating object. The NPS summary describes the flotilla coming under fire. Selfridge gives the first explosion near his port quarter and the second apparently below the port bow. The NPS summary sequences blast damage at the port bow and then on the starboard side near the armor. The pages disagree about the sequence and placement as reported. A contemporary observer’s position and a later physical damage interpretation can differ. The exact reconstruction remains open. [4–5]

The HathiTrust link to the printed Official Records page returned HTTP 403. The passage used here is the explicitly labeled transcription.
