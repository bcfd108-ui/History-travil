# HT-RES-VA-shenandoah-ccc-big-meadows — The meadow where a park was built by hand, on land other people had to leave
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A high Blue Ridge meadow at mile 51 of Skyline Drive, where in May 1933 one of the first two CCC camps in the national parks rose in tents, where a president lunched with enrollees that August and dedicated the park three years later, and where the road and plantings still carry the shape of the work — and of the displacement that made the ground available.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a developed landscape, not a single building: the Big Meadows area of Shenandoah National Park, Virginia, at mile 51 on Skyline Drive — the meadow, the site of CCC Camp NP-2 (later Camp Fechner), the Harry F. Byrd Sr. Visitor Center, the wayside, the lodge and campground ground, and the Drive frontage. The operator is the National Park Service. The NPS's own fees page states the terms as printed here: all visitors must have an entrance pass; the Standard Pass runs $15.00–$30.00 — $30 for a private vehicle and all its passengers, $25 for motorcycles, $15 per person on foot or bicycle at 16 or older, children under 16 free — valid for seven consecutive days; the Shenandoah Annual Pass is $55.00. The fees page states the park does not accept cash — fully cashless since July 1, 2025 — and Big Meadows is entered from Skyline Drive itself, with no separate gate. Byrd Visitor Center hours are seasonal, and the NPS listings consulted carry no single year-round schedule for it, so no visitor-center hours are printed here — that gap is stated rather than filled from a secondary source. The Skyline Drive Historic District was listed in the National Register on April 28, 1997 (NRHP reference no. 97000375, per the NPS's own record).

## 2. The story
The park was an idea before it was a place. In 1924 the Secretary of the Interior formed the Southern Appalachian National Park Committee, which recognized the potential of a scenic drive along the Blue Ridge crest in northern Virginia. Congress authorized Shenandoah National Park on **May 22, 1926** — but authorization was not land. Virginia had to buy the mountain and give it to the nation: the NPS's own park history records that the park was pieced together from more than 3,000 individual tracts, purchased or condemned by Virginia under its blanket condemnation authority, with legal challenges running to the end of 1935. [1][2]

The road came before the park did. Construction of **Skyline Drive** began in **1931**, in the Hoover administration, as drought-relief and Depression work, through the NPS and the Bureau of Public Roads. The NPS record gives the sequence: groundbreaking July 18, 1931; the central section, Thornton Gap to Swift Run Gap, opened September 15, 1934; the north section, Thornton Gap to Front Royal, opened October 1, 1936; the south section, Swift Run Gap to Jarman Gap, opened August 29, 1939; and the last 8.5 miles, Jarman Gap to Rockfish Gap, built as the Blue Ridge Parkway's first section in 1938–1939 and deeded to Shenandoah in 1961. Contractors graded and surfaced the road; its finishing — overlooks, walls, planting, the blending of raw cuts back into the mountain — was substantially CCC work. [3]

Franklin D. Roosevelt established the **Civilian Conservation Corps** in the spring of 1933 to relieve unemployment and carry out conservation work on public lands. Shenandoah was where the Park Service showed it off. The NPS's CCC history, by Reed Engle, records that the first two CCC camps in the national parks were established in **May 1933** at Skyland (Camp NP-1) and **Big Meadows (Camp NP-2)**, before Shenandoah was officially a park — Washington saw the future park's proximity as the ideal setting to demonstrate Roosevelt's Depression cures. The camps began operation on May 11, 1933. Big Meadows' camp began, in the cultural landscape account's words, as surplus tents, field kitchens, and latrines; permanent buildings followed — barracks, an infirmary, administration buildings, a garage, a recreation building — and the camp, housing about 200 men, extended from Milam Gap to Fishers Gap and north to the present campground. It was later named **Camp Fechner**, for CCC director Robert Fechner, who died in 1939. [4][5]

On **August 12, 1933**, the President made the first presidential visit to a CCC camp, lunching at Big Meadows with CCC Company 350 — photographed at an outdoor table with General Malone, Louis Howe, Secretary Ickes, Fechner, Secretary Wallace, and Rexford Tugwell, the company behind them, in an FDR Library photograph held by the National Archives (NAID 195930). Engle is plain about the purpose: Roosevelt ensured the uplifting image of Shenandoah's camps was flashed around the world, and Shenandoah, long before it was born, was baptized by the CCC. [6][7]

The work itself is itemized in the cultural landscape record. At Big Meadows the CCC undertook erosion control, removal of dead chestnut trees — the blight-killed stands show in period photographs behind the new development — planting, and construction of trails, shelters, and picnic areas with fountains, tables, and fireplaces. The Corps established a nursery at the meadow's northeastern edge to propagate native vegetation for stabilizing the Drive's slopes, and, in the meadow itself, then a designated national soaring site, built a runway and a temporary hangar for sailplanes. Across the park, Engle records, between May 11, 1933 and March 31, 1942, ten CCC camps were established within or on leased land adjacent to Shenandoah, with more than 1,000 young men in camp at any one time. Until the park's establishment, most work was confined to the Drive's 100-foot right-of-way, Skyland, Rapidan Camp, and Big Meadows, where Virginia had already purchased most of the ground. [4][5]

The park underneath the work has a harder story, and the NPS tells it. The mountain was not empty. The NPS-hosted archaeological account of the displaced, by Audrey J. Horning, records that at least **500 families** were displaced as the park was pieced together from its 3,000-plus tracts, people described at the time as almost completely cut off from the current of American life, in what some considered a humanitarian act. The NPS's own park history is blunter: no one, it says, seemed to have given serious thought to the 400–500 families that had no desire to move; the forced resettlement of 465 families between 1935 and 1937 was, in its words, a classic case of bureaucratic ineptitude. Federal policy reversed itself in the open — first that residents would not be disturbed unless in the path of development, then, on February 1, 1934, Director Cammerer's statement that all inhabitants would have to leave. The Resettlement Administration bought 6,291 acres in seven bordering locations for resettlement communities; by spring 1938, 175 families had been relocated to them, 42 elderly residents had been given life estates, several families had been physically evicted and their houses burned, and the majority had simply left on their own. And to restore — or, in Horning's correction, to create — a "natural" landscape from the abandoned settlements, CCC crews dismantled buildings and obscured the traces of habitation, Nicholson Hollow the one exception. The hands that built the park's natural appearance also erased much of the evidence of the people it displaced. [2][8]

The park the CCC was building was also, by policy, a segregated one. The NPS's segregation history records that managers laid the groundwork for a "separate but equal" policy before the park was established — Cammerer's handwritten "Provision for colored guests" on a memorandum of November 30, 1932 — and that the stated 1936 policy contemplated separate facilities for white and colored visitors as necessary to conform with Virginia's customs. The concessionaire's plans accordingly included, alongside the lodge and cabins at Big Meadows, "a development for colored people" at **Lewis Mountain** — campground, smaller lodge, and cabins — Shenandoah's first and only area set aside for African American visitors, in the same construction and design as the rest of the park and likewise CCC-built. Lewis Mountain was quietly desegregated in 1945 on Secretary Ickes's direction, and Shenandoah's facilities were integrated in 1950. Big Meadows itself was, in its founding years, a white visitors' landscape; that is part of its record. [9][10]

On **July 3, 1936**, Roosevelt came back to Big Meadows and dedicated the park in the meadow the CCC had camped in. Over the next five years the CCC, the NPS, and the Virginia Sky-Line Company built out the developed area still in use: picnic grounds and campground (CCC, 1937), five cabins (1938), the Lodge and Wayside (1939), maintenance area (1939), and employee housing (1941). The CCC's run at Shenandoah closed March 31, 1942. [4][5]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a mountain meadow farmed and grazed for generations, ringed by the homes of families Virginia was buying out or condemning, a raw new road cut along the crest. **Now:** the park's largest open meadow, kept open by active management, with the developed area grouped at its edge where the 1930s plans put it. [1]

**Then:** Camp NP-2 in tents in May 1933, then barracks, infirmary, garage, and recreation hall for about 200 men, Milam Gap to Fishers Gap. **Now:** no camp buildings stand; the site is a marked footprint in the meadow. What the camp built is everywhere around it; where the camp stood is grass. [1][5]

**Then:** Skyline Drive as a construction corridor — raw banks, cut slopes, and crews rounding and planting the roadside back into the mountain (see §7). **Now:** a 105.5-mile historic district whose blended, planted banks read as if the road had always laid along the crest. That naturalness was manufactured, much of it by CCC hands. [1][3]

## 4. What's still there
- Skyline Drive itself at Big Meadows — mile 51 of the 105.5-mile drive, with its CCC-finished banks, overlooks, and plantings; a National Register historic district (97000375) [3]
- The Big Meadows developed area as laid out in the 1930s: picnic grounds and campground (CCC, 1937), lodge and wayside ground (1939), and the functional grouping the cultural landscape record finds still intact [1][5]
- The Harry F. Byrd Sr. Visitor Center at mile 51, the Mission 66-era visitor center carrying the park's interpretation, including the CCC camp story [1]
- The Camp Fechner site footprint in the meadow — Camp NP-2's marked ground; a site, not standing buildings [5]
- Lewis Mountain, south on the Drive — the CCC-built campground, picnic ground, and cabins of the park's segregated African American visitor area, desegregated in 1945 and integrated in 1950 [9][10]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only:

1. **At the Byrd Visitor Center, mile 51:** Start where the park puts its interpretation, then read the developed area from the meadow edge as a single 1930s plan, its facilities grouped by function around the open meadow. [1]
2. **On the Camp Fechner ground:** Walk the marked camp site. The camp that began in tents in May 1933 and held about 200 men is a footprint now; the stop is the contrast between the empty grass and the amount of surrounding landscape those men made. [5]
3. **Along the Drive at the meadow's frontage:** Read the roadside closely — the rounded, planted, blended banks are the CCC's signature work, the deliberate erasure of the road's own construction, photographed in progress in §7. [1][3]
4. **At the picnic ground and campground:** The CCC-built picnic ground of 1937 in continuous use — the Corps' work at its least monumental and most durable. [5]
5. **At Lewis Mountain:** End where the park's founding policy is built into the ground — the CCC-built campground and picnic ground set aside for African American visitors under segregation, in the same materials and design as Big Meadows. Either stop alone tells half the story. [9]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The camp count does not agree across the NPS's own publications, and the disagreement is printed rather than resolved. The Big Meadows cultural landscape account states that six CCC camps were set up in Shenandoah, and park signage names six: Big Meadows, Skyland, Pinnacles, Piney River, Baldface, and Dundo. Engle's NPS history states that ten camps were established between 1933 and 1942 within, or on leased land adjacent to, the park. The counts appear to measure different sets, but neither source reconciles itself to the other. [4][5]

The displacement count is likewise carried in overlapping figures. Horning's account gives at least 500 families displaced from more than 3,000 tracts. The NPS park history gives 400–500 families in the park's path and 465 forcibly resettled between 1935 and 1937; the park's teaching account gives 175 families relocated to the resettlement communities and 42 former residents in 16 families granted lifetime tenancy. All are printed as their sources state them. [2][8][10]

One element of this entry's tasking could not be verified in any NPS source and is corrected here rather than repeated. The tasking named "Camp Greentop" as a Shenandoah CCC camp and as an African American camp at Big Meadows. NPS sources place Camp Greentop in **Catoctin Mountain Park, Maryland** — a Recreational Demonstration Area cabin camp built for the Maryland League for Crippled Children, in use from 1938, which served only white campers for many years under Jim Crow practice and desegregated in the 1950s. No NPS Shenandoah source consulted names a Camp Greentop in this park; what they carry is Lewis Mountain as the segregated African American visitor area, and that is what §2 prints. The Greentop association appears to be a conflation of two New Deal camp stories, flagged so it does not travel further. [9][11]

The south section of Skyline Drive carries two opening dates in NPS publications: the Skyline Drive record gives August 29, 1939 for Swift Run Gap to Jarman Gap, while Highways in Harmony gives September 29, 1939. Both are printed; §2 uses the former. [3][12]

The first photograph's record (§7) states its own limits: photographer and date unknown. Its HAER VA-119 cataloguing associates it with the Skyline Drive documentation, and its "34" catalog element might suggest 1934, but the record expressly declines to date it, and no year is printed from a suggestion. The Skyline Drive Historic District's National Register record carries no single point for Big Meadows, so §10 prints a point only at the scope an NPS source verifies.
