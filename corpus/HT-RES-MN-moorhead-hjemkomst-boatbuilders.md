## 2 · Story — A ship in a potato warehouse

The first timber was milled on July 4, 1972 at Harvey Engen’s mill near Viking, Minnesota, according to the Historical and Cultural Society of Clay County. A boat that eventually took more than a hundred oak trees had begun with a rough estimate of fifteen. Family, friends and donors supplied wood and labor. The society describes the crew brushing paraffin onto the cut ends, stacking timber to dry and working a surface planer that Robert Asp modified with reduction gears, chains and belts. This is a museum account of a collective building process. Neither quantity is a contemporary inventory. [3]

The welter of rigging now fits under a museum roof in Moorhead. The builders had a less forgiving roof. In 1974 Asp and helpers converted Hawley’s former Leslie Welter Potato Warehouse into their shipyard. The city leased the building to Bob and Rose Asp for ten dollars a year according to the museum. The builders removed the first floor and assembled the hull on the basement floor. Finishing the boat left the problem of getting it out. In July 1980, they opened the front wall, moved the hull on timbers and steel rollers, then lifted and turned it in the street. [3]

Robert Asp sailed on the ship on Lake Superior in 1980 and died that December. His family and other crew members continued the project. At Duluth on May 6, 1982, Rose Asp said, “Godspeed, I love you,” in the dispatch by Richard McFarland preserved in UPI’s index. The report mentions food, fresh water and modern electronics. It is a window into the practical voyage the builders had prepared, and the words mark the family handoff. [3–5]

The ship reached New York in June, then crossed the North Atlantic. The county society dates the arrival at Bergen to July 19 and Oslo to August 9, 1982. A 1989 campus newspaper account reports a fourteen-foot crack after a storm and a decision to continue. MNopedia adds that the Norwegian skipper judged the crossing could go on. Naval or crew log confirmation has yet to establish the detailed sequence and geometry of the damage. [3–4,6–7]

## 3 · Then / now

The building site was in Hawley. The launch was in Duluth. The surviving ship is the same twentieth-century vessel on view in Moorhead. It was patterned after the Gokstad ship and is itself an artifact of this project. The museum site provides the ordinary public viewing anchor. [1–4]

## 5 · Trip progression

Go directly to the gallery. Study the long hull and its upper curve from the ordinary public rail. Read the account of the basement and the opening cut into the warehouse front. **Story landing — The wall had to come down.** From the open ship gallery, follow the length of the woodwork with your eye and consider how the builders moved it out, one controlled increment at a time. Then read Rose Asp’s sendoff. In poor weather stay with the indoor visit when open. [1,3,5]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- The July 1972 milling and 1974 warehouse conversion describe different stages. The county museum counts twelve crew on arrival in Norway. The newspaper wire describes a skipper and “a crew of 12” when departing Duluth, which may count roles differently. A signed sailing roster was not obtained. [3,5]
- The May 1982 UPI archive's direct AMP page returned HTTP 403 in the web reader. Its indexed dispatch supplied the short Rose Asp quote. First-person crew log pages and plans were not obtained. The society's journal inventory records donated slides and voyage-related holdings; it does not establish open photo rights. [5,8]
- Bergen and Oslo are separate arrivals, on July 19 and August 9. The archive coverage of the crossing and the indoor display do not establish any exterior build-site anchor in Hawley. [3–4,6–7]
