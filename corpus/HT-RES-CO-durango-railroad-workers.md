## 2. The story

**Named firsthand worker: Alva F. Lyons**, interviewed in Durango by **Rae Haynes, November 10, 1989**, for the Center of Southwest Studies at Fort Lewis College. The published transcript, especially printed pages 7–11, was read. It records his recollections of returning to Durango after military service, railroad employment, labor conditions and courtship. His daughter **Martha Lyons McDaniel** later supplied bracketed editorial notes, explicitly distinguished by the edition. Audio was not heard. [3]

His plain return-home statement is **“Well I come right back to Durango and go to work.”** [3, printed p. 7]

The institutional finding aid identifies Lyons as a San Juan Basin narrow-gauge conductor and preserves his papers as **M238**, including material related to the Order of Railroad Conductors. [4]

**Original interpretive voice:** The passenger remembers the journey. The worker has to make a life around it. When a railroad story is told only through scenery and equipment, the ordinary stakes disappear: time, wages, friendships, courtship and the ability to be at home. This account shifts the focus from the train as an attraction to work as a condition of belonging in a town.

A retrospective interview is not a payroll record or a neutral summary of every labor dispute. Preserve the speaker's perspective without treating it as the experience of all workers or all households. No operational explanation from the wider transcript is reproduced.

## 3. Then / now

Then: Lyons remembers working life alongside relationships and community. Now: a public local-history museum provides a lawful interpretive setting, while another institution preserves his transcript and papers. These are **two different institutions**, not a claim that Animas Museum holds M238 or displays his belongings. [1,3–4]

The museum occupies the former **1904 Animas City School**, not a railroad worksite. [1] Its civic setting helps keep this visit about the town and its people rather than treating access to operating railway property as necessary for understanding railway history.

The museum's public program also documents an oral-history presentation about childhood at the Rico depot. That is a separately named, geographically different account; its video was not watched and is not merged with Lyons's story. [2]
