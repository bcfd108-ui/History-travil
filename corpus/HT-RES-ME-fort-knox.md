# HT-RES-ME-fort-knox — The Granite Fort That Never Fired a Shot

Division ME seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a high bluff above the narrows of the Penobscot River at Prospect, Maine, stands the state's first granite fort and its largest historic fort: a five-sided fortress of granite and brick, built between 1844 and 1864 against a British naval attack that never came, garrisoned twice in wartime, and never fired a shot in anger. Fort Knox survives essentially as its builders left it — about eighty percent finished, its casemates, spiral granite stairs, dry moat, and shore batteries intact — and today it is Fort Knox State Historic Site, administered by the Maine Bureau of Parks and Lands, with the Penobscot Narrows Observatory reached through its gate.

## 1. Trip gate

Fort Knox State Historic Site is administered by the Maine Bureau of Parks and Lands (Department of Agriculture, Conservation and Forestry). The visitor address is 740 Fort Knox Road, Prospect, ME 04981, on Route 174 just off U.S. Route 1, across the river from Bucksport; phone 207-469-6553. The Bureau's park page gives the season plainly: open 9:00 a.m. to sunset daily from May 1st to October 31st, unless otherwise posted at the gate, fee charged. In the off season the fort and observatory are closed, but visitors may park outside the gate and walk in during the same hours; facilities are closed then.

The site's visitor pages (fortknoxmaine.com) break the season down further. May 1–June 30: site and observatory 9:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m., the fort itself until 6:00 p.m. July 1–August 31: site and observatory 9:00 a.m.–6:00 p.m., the fort until 7:00 p.m. September 1–October 31: site and observatory 9:00 a.m.–5:00 p.m., the fort until 6:00 p.m. or sunset, whichever is earlier. November 1–April 30 the fort and observatory are closed while the grounds remain open 9:00 a.m. to sunset, year-round, weather depending.

Admission is per person, sold only at the gate booth on arrival; there are no online tickets. Fort and park entry only: Maine resident adults (12–65 years) $6.00; Maine resident seniors (65+) free; non-resident adults $8.00; non-resident seniors $4.00; children 5–11 years $3.00. Adding the Observatory elevator: $9.00 / $3.00 / $11.00 / $7.00 / $6.00 in the same order. These figures match the Bureau's published day-use fee schedule, which also notes that the Maine State Park Pass does not cover the Observatory. The observatory is entered through the fort site — the Maine Department of Transportation directs all observatory visitors through Fort Knox State Historic Site — and the visitor site directs arrivals to the Guest Education Center, with its free museum and gift shop.

The visitor boundary is that of a state historic site on a river bluff: in season everything is ticketed through the one gate; out of season the ground stays open on foot, but the buildings do not.


## 2. The story

The fort exists because the Penobscot valley had already been invaded twice. The Maine Department of Transportation's account states it directly: Maine was repeatedly involved in northeast border disputes with British Canada, and the area between Castine and the lumber city of Bangor was invaded and occupied by the British during the Revolution and again during the War of 1812. The next attack, if it came, would come up the Penobscot past the narrows at Prospect, toward Bangor's lumber trade.

The National Register nomination records that the site was selected by Major General Wool in 1838, at the time of the United States' controversy with Great Britain over the northeastern boundary. The land, 124.5 acres, was acquired in September 1843–March 1844. The post was named for the Revolutionary War hero and first U.S. Secretary of War, General Henry Knox, of Thomaston, Maine; the Department of Transportation adds that Knox was born in Boston and retired to Thomaston in 1796.

Construction began in 1844. The Department of Transportation states that the fort was designed by Chief Engineer Joseph Totten, with other engineers as superintendents of construction, among them Isaac Ingalls Stevens and Thomas L. Casey. What rose on the bluff was, in the nomination's words, a massive granite-and-brick five-sided fortress with a dry moat and sloping glacis on the landward sides and two powerful flanking shore batteries — the first fort in Maine built of granite, and, the Bureau states, Maine's largest historic fort.

The work stopped in 1864 — the nomination is blunt: construction continued until 1864, when it ended forever. About eighty percent complete, the total cost was $928,500.31. The fortress was designed to mount 137 cannon; it was never finished, and the enemy it was built against never came up the river.

The fort was first garrisoned during the Civil War: the nomination records an occupation from July 1863 to March 1866 by a detachment of some fifty Maine Volunteers, the fort armed at this time with 111 heavy guns. The Department of Transportation adds the garrison's character — the first troops were mostly volunteers in training before being sent to active posts, and included members of the celebrated 20th Maine. In June and July of 1898, during the Spanish-American War, the post was occupied again, by a regiment of Connecticut Volunteers: the nomination numbers them at about 354 men, and the Bureau records that they lived outside the main fort structure in large tents. The Bureau's summary of the whole military record is one sentence: although it never saw combat, Fort Knox was garrisoned during the Civil and Spanish American Wars.

The Army's ownership ended by statute. An Act of Congress of March 4, 1923 authorized the Secretary of War to dispose of the Fort Knox reservation and its buildings, and Maine purchased them for $2,121 on December 4, 1923. Fort Knox State Park has been administered by the Maine State Park and Recreation Commission since July 1, 1943. The property was entered in the National Register of Historic Places on October 1, 1969, on a nomination by Charles W. Snell of the National Park Service's Division of History, dated July 10, 1970, which judged the fort a superb and completely unaltered example of a large mid-19th century granite coastal fortification and probably New England's finest unmodified specimen of the period's military architecture.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the bluff at the narrows was a construction site that became a fortress. For twenty years, master craftsmen raised granite walls twenty feet high and forty feet thick around a five-sided parade, cut casemates into the two river walls, and dug tunnels down the bluff to two shore batteries. The fort's working life was two short wartime occupations: Maine volunteers training here in the Civil War years, and Connecticut volunteers tenting outside the walls for two months in 1898. Between and after those garrisons, the fort stood empty above the river it was built to close.

Now, the same ground is a state historic site of lawns, picnic ground, and open granite. The fortress a visitor walks is the fortress the nomination described in 1970 — its judgment was that the brick and stone work remain unaltered except for small removals for building purposes, and the site is presented today on that basis. What has changed around it is the crossing: the Penobscot Narrows Bridge now carries U.S. Route 1 over the river beside the fort, and its observatory is visited through the fort's own gate, so fort and bridge tower are taken in on a single ticket. Across the river, the Bucksport waterfront still gives the reading the fort was built to command — the view this entry's first photograph records in snow.

## 4. What's still there

The main fortress survives as the nomination describes it: a five-sided work of granite blocks, about 146 feet by 252 feet, its walls averaging about twenty feet in height and forty feet in width; with moat and glacis, the whole measures about 350 feet by 250 feet. Two of its five walls face east upon the river, containing eight great arched casemates with ceilings heavily vaulted to support the artillery platforms on the stone deck above; both levels of the river walls were armed. At the junction of the river walls, in the center east, is a large projecting bastion built with cannon ports and rifle galleries for flanking fire along the walls. North of the bastion is the main entrance: a large flat-arched doorway topped by a granite pediment and flanked by pilasters.

Inside, the plan is an open courtyard — the parade ground — marked by numerous manholes giving access to large bomb-proof areas below ground intended for storage of supplies and provisions. At the northeast and southeast corners of the river side are two spiral staircases in which a complete circular column is formed by the treads, each tread a solid piece of granite. The landward walls held the fort's domestic life: officers' quarters in the south wall (two sections of two rooms each, each about fifty by twenty feet); enlisted men's quarters in the 252-foot west wall, in eight compartments of about thirty by fifteen feet; stables and storage in the north wall, with rifle galleries along the outer faces and guns on the deck above.

Outside the walls, the three landward faces are defended by a wide, deep, granite-faced dry moat, covered by a massive sloping glacis whose northeast end is galleried, finely faced in granite, and pierced by cannon ports and rifle slits, its north face terraced with great rough-hewn granite blocks. Near the fort's northeast and southeast corners, two tunnels with granite staircases descend the bluff to the shore batteries, entrenched behind stone parapets, each with its brick hot shot oven and magazines — the larger, seaward south battery laid out for about twenty-five guns.

The modern site's visitor plant stands alongside: the gate booth, the Guest Education Center with its free museum and gift shop, the picnic area and self-guided trail the Bureau lists among the site's facilities, and the elevator entrance for the Penobscot Narrows Observatory, reached only through the fort grounds.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Buy the ticket at the gate booth — fort only, or fort with the observatory elevator — and begin at the Guest Education Center, where the free museum gives the fort's outline before the granite does. Then enter the fortress the way its garrison did, through the main entrance in the north river wall: the flat-arched, pedimented doorway in the center of the river face.

From the doorway, take the parade ground first as a plan: read the five sides, find the manholes to the bomb-proof storage below the courtyard, then work the river walls — the eight arched casemates, the gun deck above them, and the projecting bastion at the junction of the two river walls, built to fire along the walls themselves. Climb one of the two granite spiral staircases in the northeast and southeast corners, where the treads wind into a complete circular column of solid granite.

Cross to the landward walls for the living quarters — officers' rooms in the south wall, the eight enlisted compartments in the west wall, stables and storage in the north — then go outside to walk the dry moat and glacis, where the galleried northeast end still carries its cannon ports and rifle slits. Follow the tunnels down the bluff to the shore batteries and their hot shot ovens; the south battery, laid out for about twenty-five guns, is the fort at water level, aimed at the channel it was built to close.

If the ticket includes it, end with the observatory elevator, which runs on the shorter hours the visitor pages post — the fort stays open an hour or more later. And if the day allows a crossing, close from the Bucksport waterfront, where the whole work reads at once in the view this entry's first photograph was taken from.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The end of construction is not stated the same way in the primary record. The nomination (Snell, July 10, 1970) states that construction began in 1844 and continued until 1864, when it ended forever, and the Bureau of Parks and Lands repeats the span: constructed between 1844 and 1864. The Maine Department of Transportation's page, however, describes the engineers serving as superintendents of construction from 1844 to 1869, Stevens and Casey among them, and 1844–1869 is the span that circulates widely in secondary accounts. Whether superintendence, finishing work, or nothing at all ran on to 1869 is not resolved by the sources fetched; §2 prints 1844–1864, following the nomination, and the 1869 figure is recorded here rather than reconciled.

The size of the 1898 garrison is also given two ways: the nomination numbers the Connecticut Volunteers at about 354 men; the Bureau states that about 500 Connecticut troops were stationed here. Both figures stand, unresolved.

The fort's guns are documented precisely for 1970 and not for today. The nomination records that the fortress was built to mount 137 cannon, was armed with 111 heavy guns during its Civil War occupation, and had only three heavy guns left in 1970 — two 10-inch Rodmans and one 15-inch Rodman. No operator source fetched states how many guns are on the ground now, so no present-day count is asserted. Within the nomination, one figure is illegible in the digitized copy consulted: the north shore battery's gun platforms are given a number the scan does not read cleanly, while the south battery is given as about twenty-five guns. The north battery's number is left unstated rather than guessed.

The street address is printed two ways in the state's own pages: the visitor site and the Department of Transportation's mailing address give 740 Fort Knox Road, the address carried in §10, while the Department's directions text locates the entrance at 711 Fort Knox Road. Both numbers stand in the state record.

Two records sit twenty years apart unexplained. The nomination states that Maine purchased the fort on December 4, 1923, for $2,121 — and that the park has been administered by the Maine State Park and Recreation Commission since July 1, 1943. What the fort was, administratively, in between is covered by no source fetched. The nomination likewise fixes the property at 124.5 acres, while modern secondary listings give the park as 124, 125, or 144 acres; no current primary source fetched states a present-day acreage, so only the nomination's figure is printed.

The landmark designation is dated less firmly in the primary record than in the secondary one. The Register record dates the listing to October 1, 1969, and the nomination file is carried in the National Park Service's National Historic Landmark collection; the designation date of December 30, 1970 commonly printed for the landmark was verified in this pass only in secondary sources, and is not asserted here. Finally, no HABS or HAER survey was located for this dossier — the Library of Congress site could not be read from this desk in this pass, its search returning an access error — so no survey number is claimed in §10, and the fort's HABS/HAER status is left open.

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** DrStew82's winter view of Fort Knox from the Bucksport waterfront, across the Penobscot River. The frame is taken from the far bank, over a band of dark river water at the bottom. Above the water the fort's ground rises in snow: terraced, snow-covered earthworks — the glacis — fill the middle distance, and on them stands the long granite river wall, its row of dark casemate openings running the wall's length, the small pedimented main entrance visible in its face, and a second granite face, the bastion front, at the left. Snow lies on the terreplein above the wall. Behind the fort the forested bluff rises under a flat overcast sky, and a spruce branch intrudes at the bottom center — the photographer's own bank. The Penobscot Narrows Bridge, which stands beside the fort, is not in this frame. The frame shows the fort as its builders meant it to be read: from the water.
