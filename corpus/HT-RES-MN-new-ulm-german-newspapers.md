## 2 · Story — The newspaper changed hands

A German weekly started printing again in a town whose earlier newspaper office and press had been destroyed during the U.S.–Dakota War of 1862. The Library of Congress title history identifies Albert Wolff and Josef Hofer as the founders of the *New Ulm Post*. The online bibliographic record transcribes the first issue as February 5, 1864. The paper described itself, in the catalog historian’s English rendering, as “An independent newspaper for freedom, justice, and progress.” That is the newspaper’s stated purpose as translated by the catalog. [3–5]

The 1916 Brown County history places the early office in a Broadway post-office building later known as the Forster building. It gives a precise sequence: Hofer became sole owner with the June 10 issue, Ludwig Bogen took over with the July 29 issue and Lambert Naegele joined in 1865. There were moves to Minnesota Street in 1869 and Center Street in December 1876. A case or desk carried out of one address and into another was part of the business of keeping a weekly alive. The *Post* also printed partly in English in 1873, according to that account. [3,6]

A language could carry opposing political positions. The county history places the elder Bogen’s paper with Republicans and the paper under his son with Democrats. It describes another German weekly, *Der Fortschritt*, as beginning in 1891 with the Farmers’ Alliance, then passing to a Socialist cooperative in 1912. In December 1915, the *Post* acquired the plant and subscriber list. The county historian’s hostile appraisal of the cooperative is an editorial judgment. The ownership change and the transfer of subscriptions are the verifiable point of the account. [3,6]

A preserved November 1914 page puts a local flour mill’s and a hardware company’s advertisements alongside German-language reading. The indexed scan identifies the mill and stove company. Newspapers put local sellers, readers and editors in the same weekly transaction. The LOC title history dates the final issue to May 12, 1933 and attributes the closure to shrinking readership and advertising. Its account provides no sales ledger. [3–4,7]

## 3 · Then / now

Then the *Post* worked at multiple addresses, the first in an earlier post office. The present museum occupies the later federal post office built in 1909–1910, opened in 1910 and used by the Postal Service until 1976 according to the museum. The society moved in in 1984. This building gives visitors a public setting in which to read the history of local communication. No surviving press equipment or original newsroom is implied. [1–2,6,8]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- The first office and current museum have both been called post offices. The 1864 office in the county history and the 1910 federal building in the agency and operator records refer to different premises. A period plat matched to the early addresses was not verified. [1,6,8]
- The county history gives exact 1864 issue dates for changes. The title essay gives months. They agree at month scale. The later online title record flags a printed January 1912 date as January 1914 in its description. That is a catalog correction, so retain both readings in date-sensitive citations. [3–4,6]
- The 1916 issue detail returned HTTP 403, and direct render of the November 27, 1914 scan returned HTTP 403. The indexed scan description and library chronology were available, along with an OCR transcription of the 1916 county history. No uninspected German text is offered as a verbatim quote. First and last issue images and the plant ledgers remain open. [3–7,9]
