# Walter Anderson’s two rooms in Ocean Springs

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP23 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
Walter Inglis Anderson worked in several ways at once. He drew and wrote about the coast, carved blocks and wood, and decorated pottery that helped support him. Agnes Grinstead Anderson, his wife, remembered the rhythm after a long visit to Horn Island: “He was paid for decorating ten pieces of pottery a week.” She explains in the archived interview how several weeks away could leave a rush of pottery work when he returned. This is her recollection, preserved in a transcript. [2, 3]

Anderson’s own logs also give that work a voice. In an excerpt reproduced by the museum, he begins an observation, “It was an embarrassment of riches.” He was painting pelicans when a terrapin, crab and king rail appeared around the mangroves. The record describes the act of looking and the interruption that became part of it. The manuscript collection includes actual logs and later annotations. The museum’s printed excerpt supplies the words quoted here. [3, 4]

Ocean Springs commissioned an extensive public mural. The register nomination follows the painted surfaces around the room, over frames, jambs, and other details that would disappear in a cropped picture. It records the $1 payment, the start of painting in 1951 and work continuing into 1952. The large room needed paint, access to surfaces and the time to work throughout a shared building. Its designs bring plants and animals into view among fields of repeated shapes. [5, 6]

Anderson’s family discovered another mural at the cottage after he died in 1965. The archive identifies it as *Creation at Sunrise*, a painting carried across four walls and ceiling. The former private room and the civic room were made under different conditions. The museum allows a visitor to consider them together because the cottage room’s mural survives as relocated interior fabric and the city’s public mural survives in its original room. [3, 5, 7]

## 3. Then and now
Original municipal mural work remains on the Community Center surfaces. The adjacent modern museum provides interpretation. The Little Room portion comes from the Shearwater cottage. The historical designation of the Community Center is a distinct record from the present museum admission and from the cottage grounds. [1, 3, 5–7]

## 5. Public progression
Use the operator’s Washington Avenue museum entrance. Start with a work on paper or a ceramic piece, if on view, and read how pottery work and log-keeping could overlap in one week. See the Little Room when on view and distinguish its moved, painted surfaces from the Community Center’s original setting. End at **Where the Paint Crosses the Frame**. In the Community Center, if open, notice the design continuing over a frame or trim. If that room is reserved, use the museum’s indoor interpretation or the Little Room as the alternative. The campus visit requires no trip to the cottage or islands. [1–8]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- The older archived museum page prints older or internally mixed hours. The current museum homepage posts Mon–Sat 11–5 and Sun 1–5, plus a dated Thanksgiving closure. [1, 7]
- The nomination describes construction in 1945–48 and 13 months of painting begun in 1951. The museum institutional chronology gives 1949–50 for the building, a 1950 commission and 16 months of painting in 1951–52. Museum publicity describes about 3,000 square feet of mural; the amended nomination estimates about 2,500 square feet of wall surface. The difference in measurement scope and construction dates remains open. [5, 6]
- No original commission, apprentice or setup payroll, or verified public-domain photograph of the historic room was established. The cited journal passage is a museum excerpt and the archive link is a finding aid. [3–6]
