## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** the steel plant's workforce included the people handling its purchasing and correspondence. Their working lives did not necessarily fit the calendar an employer allowed.

**Edna and Leo Loveridge** gave a joint oral-history interview on September 6, 2005, conducted by **Connie Nielson**, with **Layne Whittaker** assisting. The transcript is published on Lehi City's website and identifies the John Hutchings Museum of Natural History. [5]

On printed page 216, Edna explains that she joined Geneva's Purchasing department after a year at BYU. When discussing her marriage in November 1945, she says **“they wouldn’t give me a few days off for that so I quit.”** She later returned as a secretary, then left to begin their family. This is her published firsthand account, not a personnel-file finding or a claim about every woman's treatment at Geneva. [5]

Leo identifies himself as a U.S. Steel employee and describes a long Geneva career, retirement and a later return. Crucially, he invites Edna to explain her own working history rather than letting it vanish behind his. [5, pp. 215–216] The distinctive interpretive focus is the pair of careers, not an anonymous family supported by one male wage.

**Original synthesis:** a visitor can read this as a history of negotiated time: work, marriage, military service, return and parenting. Office labor belongs in the industrial story alongside production work. The interview does not establish the employer's reasoning, a formal leave policy, or how typical this experience was; those require other records.

Willard Richard Peterson first worked weekends while still in high school. He remembered the distinction between being allowed to work as a laborer and being permitted to take an operating job. He helped survey ground for a dolomite plant and later issued tools in central maintenance. Returning from service, he chose the rolling mills. Asked what he liked, he remembered the buildings themselves: “steam heat, air conditioning.” That detail explains a choice of workplace better than the outline of the mill on a map. [10]

Peterson recalled additional stands for rolling strip for appliances and tin cans. He also remembered returning as a supervisor to train workers. Set that account next to the Loveridge interview. One describes the changing product and the skills a new crew needed. The other brings office work and the household calendar into the same industrial history. Both can be read in Provo without visiting the former plant. [5,10]

## 3. Then / now

The mill was a Utah Valley workplace, not a BYU campus facility. The Provo archive is a present repository and research setting, not a surviving steel workplace. Historical references to Geneva, Vineyard, Orem and Provo should not be converted into a single interchangeable site name.

A contemporary Utah geological survey account reports the 1987 purchase of a plant closed since August 1986 and resumed production that September. [6] That evidence separates corporate operating milestones from a worker's own return date. It does not establish present physical access or which remnants survive.

BYU describes Geneva-related holdings including corporate records and photographs in **MSS 3122**. [7] It also identifies **MSS 586**, a short historical collection about early Utah steel development, as open for public research. [8] Neither description proves that the Loveridge transcript is held there, or that any named item is currently exhibited.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The Loveridge transcript includes general plant chronology that does not align cleanly with the contemporary survey: a reference to closure in 1984 and wording linking a December 1988 return with restarting the plant. [5][6] Preserve the speaker's recollection, but do not use it as an independently verified corporate chronology. His own return date may differ from the plant's reopening. The quoted household episode is separate from those numerical claims.
