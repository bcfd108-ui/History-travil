# HT-RES-MI-fort-michilimackinac — The fort a ball game took, rebuilt from its own soil
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

A French fur-trade depot stood on the south shore of the Straits of Mackinac from 1715 to 1781 — a wooden palisade town that France built to hold the western Great Lakes trade, that Britain inherited in 1761, that Ojibwe players took in 1763 with a lacrosse game played at its open gate, and that the British finally emptied in 1781 for a stone fort on Mackinac Island. Nothing built in the 1700s stands on the ground today. What stands there instead is a reconstruction raised since 1960 out of one of the longest-running archaeological excavations in North America — and the National Historic Landmark, designated in 1960, is the site itself, not the rebuilt walls: the Park Service's nomination states plainly that the structures are reconstructions and are not considered a nationally significant resource.

## 2. The story
The French presence at the Straits began on the north shore. In 1671 Father Jacques Marquette established the Jesuit mission at present-day St. Ignace, and around 1683 the French added Fort de Buade beside it (Michigan State Historic Preservation Office, via MiPlace). In 1701 Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac transferred the garrison to Detroit, the mission closed, and — in the words of the National Historic Landmark nomination — the vital straits were left "apparently unprotected for fourteen years."

In 1715 the French returned to the south shore. The nomination records that French soldiers came back that year and a new Fort Michilimackinac was built on the southern shore; the SHPO summary dates the establishment to 1715 and the decision to build to about 1713–1715, with Constant le Marchand de Lignery leading the contingent sent to do the work. The fort's purpose was trade before war. The SHPO states it directly: "The fort was not built primarily as a military facility but as a link in the French trade system, which extended from Montreal through the Great Lakes region and northwest to Lake Winnipeg and beyond" — a supply post for French traders in the western Great Lakes and a primary stopping-off point between Montreal and the western country, from which the French "carried out the fur trade, sought alliances with native peoples, and protected their interests against the colonial ambitions of other European nations." The nomination describes the mature post's interior as it has been rebuilt: traders' houses, a chapel joined to a blacksmith's shop and parsonage, a French cemetery behind the church, British soldiers' barracks, the commandant's quarters by the Water Gate, and the King's Storehouse.

The French flag came down with Canada. The nomination says the fleur-de-lis flew over the fort until 1760, when the French abandoned the post after Canada was lost, and that English troops occupied Fort Michilimackinac in 1761; the SHPO dates the formal relinquishment to 1761, after the French defeat in the French and Indian War. Under the British the fort remained a major fur-trade facility, but the SHPO records that the Ottawa and Chippewa of the Straits "found British policies harsh compared to those of the French and they resented the British takeover."

In June 1763, as part of Pontiac's Rebellion, the fort was taken by a game. The SHPO's account: "a group of Chippewa staged a ball game outside the stockade to create a diversion and gain entrance to the post and then attacked and killed most of the British occupants." The game was baggataway, a forerunner of lacrosse, played before the open gate; weapons concealed by the women watching were passed to the players at the signal, and the garrison was overwhelmed. The French inhabitants were not the target and were not harmed. The nomination compresses the same event into one sentence: "A sudden Indian attack part of Pontiac's rebellion, on the garrison resulted in its massacre in 1763, but within a year the redcoats had re-occupied Fort Michilimackinac." The day of the month is not given by either the nomination or the SHPO summary and is contested in later accounts; it is printed as a conflict in §6, not settled here.

The end came from vulnerability, not defeat. The British judged the wooden mainland fort too exposed — the nomination gives the immediate cause as the fear, in 1781, "that the Americans were about to attack" — and in 1781 the commander abandoned the post and established a new fort on Mackinac Island, a limestone fort initially also named Fort Michilimackinac and known today as Fort Mackinac. The mainland ground emptied. The SHPO records that the vacant site became Michilimackinac State Park in 1909, under the Mackinac Island State Park Commission.

The ground then became an excavation that has never really stopped. The SHPO records that a professional archaeological program began in the late 1950s and that reconstruction based on the archaeological evidence began in 1960; the operator dates the annual summer dig to 1959 and describes it as one of the longest ongoing excavations of its kind in the nation, with the fort "reconstructed based on historic maps and more than 60 years of archaeological excavations." The designations followed the digging: Michigan State Register of Historic Sites, 1956; Michigan Historical Marker, 1958; National Historic Landmark, 1960; National Register of Historic Places, 1966 (all per the SHPO's designation list).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a palisaded trade town on the south shore — by the nomination's description, traders' houses, a chapel, a cemetery, barracks, a commandant's quarters, and a storehouse inside wooden walls, garrisoned first by France (1715–1761) and then by Britain (1761–1781). **Now:** the same ground inside Michilimackinac State Park, west of the Mackinac Bridge piers (nomination), carrying a reconstruction of the fort — sixteen buildings open per the operator — built and still being built from the excavation of the original site.

**Then:** the depot where the Montreal trade met the western country, "a primary stopping-off point between Montreal and the western country" (SHPO). **Now:** a ticketed living-history museum interpreting, for 2027, the single year 1777 — the post as a British garrison town in the American Revolution.

**Then:** in 1781 the garrison crossed the water to a stone fort on Mackinac Island and left the wooden fort to rot. **Now:** the island fort (Fort Mackinac) and the reconstructed mainland fort are both Mackinac State Historic Parks sites, facing each other across the same straits the trade once crossed.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The archaeological site itself — the landmark. The nomination is explicit that the landmark is a site, that its boundary is coterminous with the state park boundary and takes in ground beyond the fortification "because of the likelihood of subterranean resources associated with historic settlements in the vicinity," and that the reconstructed structures "are not considered a nationally significant resource" (nomination, boundary discussion)
- The reconstructed fort — palisade, gates, and sixteen buildings open and furnished as period settings or themed exhibits, including the underground Powder Magazine ruins exhibit and the "Treasures from the Sand" archaeology exhibit (operator, checked November 26, 2026)
- The living excavation: archaeologists on site each main season, early June to mid-August, in a dig the operator dates to 1959 and counts among the longest ongoing excavations of its kind in the nation (operator)
- The Visitor's Center under the Mackinac Bridge, the free main parking lot off Straits Avenue, and the surrounding Michilimackinac State Park ground, open around the clock year-round (operator)
- The shore itself: the operator notes the site stands on the shore of the Straits of Mackinac with views of the Mackinac Bridge, the straits, and the Upper Peninsula — the same water the post was built to command (operator)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the conditions are the operator's: the fort is a ticketed seasonal site, and the progression below stays on the operator's public ground and published program:

1. **At the Visitor's Center under the bridge:** Start with the orientation the operator provides — the 15-minute film *Michilimackinac: Crossroads of the Great Lakes* in the King's Storehouse sets the trade system the fort served. The landmark you are visiting is the ground, not the buildings; hold that distinction from the first step.
2. **Inside the palisade, on the parade ground:** Read the rebuilt town against the nomination's inventory — traders' houses, chapel and cemetery, barracks, commandant's quarters by the Water Gate, the King's Storehouse. Every wall here is a 20th- and 21st-century argument from evidence, and the exhibits say so.
3. **At the excavation, in season:** The dig is the site's real continuous thread — running since 1959, it is still pulling the original post out of the soil the reconstruction stands on. Watch it as the operator presents it.
4. **At the shore:** End facing the straits and the island. The garrison's 1781 crossing to Fort Mackinac is the fort's last 18th-century act, and the island fort is still there to be read across the water.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The day of the June 1763 attack is not carried by either primary summary checked — neither the NHL nomination nor the SHPO's account gives a day — and later accounts divide: June 2 is the date in most modern tellings, while June 4 — George III's birthday, the occasion the game is said to have been staged to honor — appears in the state historical marker text and in older chronicles. This entry prints "June 1763" and carries the day as an open conflict. Casualty figures likewise circulate in secondary accounts in several forms; neither the nomination nor the SHPO summary states a count, so no count prints here — the SHPO's "killed most of the British occupants" is the strongest statement the primary record checked supports.

The fort's abandonment prints two ways in secondary accounts: 1781, the year the nomination and the SHPO both give for the move to Mackinac Island, and 1783, which appears in some modern summaries as the final abandonment. No primary source checked in this pass supports 1783; 1781 is used in §2 and the variant is flagged here rather than reconciled. Inside the nomination itself, the French departure carries a smaller seam of the same kind: the nomination says the fleur-de-lis flew until 1760 and the French abandoned the post after Canada was lost, while dating the English occupation to 1761 — the SHPO's form, "relinquished in 1761," is used in §2.

The nomination also flags a conflict this entry does not resolve: "Conflict arises among academicians as to the dates of the second French occupation of Fort Michilimackinac" — the earlier French forts of the same name stood at St. Ignace, and their dates are argued. What is not argued in the sources checked is the 1715 establishment of the south-shore fort itself.

The operator's own pages disagree on the fort's summer opening hour: the attraction page gives June 5 – September 5, 2027 as 9:00 a.m. – 7:00 p.m., while the operator's seasonal-hours page gives 9:30 a.m. – 7:00 p.m. for the same window; the same two pages also give different last admissions for Fort Fright (6:30 p.m. on the attraction page, 8:30 p.m. on the seasonal-hours page). Both forms print in §1 or here with their sources; a visitor should confirm against the operator before going.

No verified point prints in §10. The NHL nomination carries a table of latitude and longitude coordinates "defining a rectangle locating the property," but in the digitized copy checked for this entry the figures are illegible — the scanned table's numbers are scrambled beyond faithful transcription — so no corner, and no converted or derived point, is carried. The nomination's approximate acreage for the nominated property reads 27 acres in the same copy, and that figure is legible and prints in §10's register note.
