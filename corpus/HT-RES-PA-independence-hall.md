# HT-RES-PA-independence-hall — The room on Chestnut Street where two documents were debated and signed
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A working colonial state house — built for Pennsylvania's Assembly, not for posterity — whose first-floor Assembly Room held, eleven years apart, the debates that produced the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution of the United States, and which the National Park Service now presents as a restoration built on evidence, with the conjectural parts named as conjectural.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a walk into a single building: Independence Hall, the former Pennsylvania State House on Chestnut Street between Fifth and Sixth Streets in Philadelphia, the centerpiece of Independence National Historical Park and operated by the National Park Service. The ground-floor Assembly Room is the room the visit turns on. The operator sources consulted for this record state no hours and no fees for the building, and none are printed here. The building was inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 1979, on the strength of the two documents debated and signed inside it.

## 2. The story
The building was raised as the State House for the colony of Pennsylvania. Per the National Park Service's architectural record, construction started in **1732**, parts of the building were still being built when the Pennsylvania Assembly began meeting in it in **1735**, and the building — without the tower — was completed in **1748**. The Historic American Buildings Survey's significance note dates the construction 1733–56 and describes a building erected to serve as the State House for the colony of Pennsylvania (see §6 for the date variance). Carver Samuel Harding created much of the ornamentation in the central hall, and some of that original woodwork still survives.

The Pennsylvania Assembly occupied the Assembly Room from 1735 to 1775 and established the room's layout — a Speaker's platform at the front, the members arrayed before it — that continued through what came next. In the spring of 1775 Pennsylvania's legislature moved to a room on the second floor, and the Assembly Room became the meeting place of the Second Continental Congress. It was in this room, the National Park Service records, that the Declaration of Independence and the United States Constitution were debated and signed — the Declaration in **1776**, and the Constitutional Convention's work in **1787**. The HABS significance note adds the middle term: in **1781** the room received ratification of the Articles of Confederation, the nation's first comprehensive frame of government.

The building's silhouette has not stood still. A tower and steeple were added in the mid-18th century; the wooden steeple had rotted by 1773 and was removed in **1781**. In **1828** the city hired architect William Strickland to restore the steeple; Strickland followed the general design of the original but deviated from it, incorporating a clock and additional ornamentation. The name changed with the building's afterlife: around the Marquis de Lafayette's visit in **1824** the old Assembly Room was called, for the first time, the "Hall of Independence," and the State House began its evolution into a national shrine.

Inside, the room was repeatedly remade. The City of Philadelphia removed the Assembly Room's original paneling in the early 19th century — a move that incensed the public — and in **1831** architect John Haviland was hired to restore the room; Haviland's work included some inaccuracies and was later removed by the National Park Service. By the 1850s the room bore little resemblance to what the founding generation saw: its furnishings, paneling, and paint colors had all been changed, and city officials had decorated the walls with portraits and filled the room with Revolutionary relics, including the cracked Liberty Bell.

When Independence National Historical Park was established in **1948**, one of the National Park Service's first goals was to restore the Assembly Room to its appearance during the American Revolution. Historians, architects, archaeologists, carpenters, and painters combined their efforts in what the Park Service calls the most complex project it undertook in Philadelphia. Preservationists scraped away decades of paint to determine original colors, examined nails — wrought iron nails indicated 18th-century work — and eventually stripped the room of all its wooden paneling, exposing the original 1730s brick walls. That work recovered the original ceiling structure, the original locations, shapes, and dimensions of the window, door, and fireplace openings, and the last remaining piece of original woodwork on the room's walls: the cockleshell frieze carving, whose paint fragments revealed the original paint color of the east wall. The preliminary furnishing of the room was completed by the summer of 1955, built from primary sources — letters, diaries, receipts, and books.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a colonial capitol under construction for two decades — Assembly meeting in a partly finished building from 1735, the main block complete without its tower in 1748. **Now:** a National Park Service building whose Assembly Room is a documented restoration: original 1730s brick, the surviving cockleshell frieze, and furnishings assembled from letters, diaries, receipts, and books.

**Then:** a wooden steeple, rotted by 1773 and taken down in 1781, leaving the tower bare. **Now:** William Strickland's 1828 steeple — a restoration in general design, but with a clock and ornamentation the original never had, as the Park Service's own architectural record states.

**Then:** a working legislative chamber whose layout the Pennsylvania Assembly set between 1735 and 1775. **Now:** that layout presented for two later bodies — the Second Continental Congress and the Constitutional Convention — with the seating arrangement, northern states to the north and southern states to the south, explicitly labeled conjectural by the Park Service.

## 4. What's still there
- The building itself: the former Pennsylvania State House on Chestnut Street between Fifth and Sixth Streets, centerpiece of Independence National Historical Park
- The Assembly Room on the first floor, restored by the National Park Service to its Revolutionary-era appearance on documentary and physical evidence
- The Rising Sun chair — the Speaker's chair — one of only two objects in the room the Park Service identifies as actually present in the room in the 18th century
- The cockleshell frieze carving, the only surviving element of the room's original 18th-century woodwork, and the second of the two original artifacts
- Original fabric uncovered in the restoration: 1730s brick walls, the original ceiling structure, and the original window, door, and fireplace openings
- Samuel Harding's surviving original woodwork ornamentation in the central hall
- Period furnishings that date to the 1700s but were not in this room — the chandelier and the Windsor chairs among them — presented alongside modern reproductions accurate to the period

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **On Chestnut Street, at the north front:** Read the building as the State House first — brick main block, wings, and the 1828 steeple that is a restoration with admitted deviations, not the 1776 silhouette.
2. **In the central hall:** Look for Samuel Harding's surviving original carving — the fabric that was never removed.
3. **In the Assembly Room:** Separate the room into its three evidentiary layers as the Park Service itself presents them — the two original artifacts (the Rising Sun chair and the cockleshell frieze), the period pieces that were not in this room, and the modern reproductions — and hold the seating plan as conjecture, not fact.
4. **Back outside, on the south side:** The room's story is a restoration story as much as a founding story; the south front is where the HABS survey's second 1939 frame was taken, paired against the Chestnut Street frame in this entry's photo record.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The Assembly Room's arrangement is a restoration based on evidence, and the Park Service says so plainly: it does not know exactly how the room appeared, or exactly where each man sat for the Second Continental Congress or the Constitutional Convention. The arrangement visitors see — raised platform, the Speaker's chair and table, two semi-circular rows of tables with Windsor chairs, green wool tablecloths, folding screens in the eastern corners — is, in the Park Service's words, the best possible arrangement based on the available physical and documentary evidence. The north-states-north / south-states-south seating is conjectural. The room reflects elements of both the Second Continental Congress and the Constitutional Convention, not one documented moment. Some displayed objects carry a connection to individual signers, but the Park Service does not know whether any of them were actually in the room during their owners' time there. Only two objects are identified as original to the room: the Rising Sun chair and the cockleshell frieze.

Construction dates vary by source. The National Park Service's architectural record: construction started 1732, Assembly meeting from 1735, building completed without the tower in 1748. The HABS significance note carried in the Library of Congress record: constructed 1733–56. Both are printed here as carried; this record does not merge them into a single span.

The steeple over the building today is not the original: it is William Strickland's 1828 work, which deviated from the original design by adding a clock and further ornamentation. John Haviland's 1831 Assembly Room restoration likewise contained inaccuracies and was removed by the National Park Service. What a visitor sees is a 20th-century restoration of an 18th-century room, assembled after the room had been stripped to its 1730s brick.
