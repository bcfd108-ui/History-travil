# HT-RES-WI-taliesin — The shining brow above the valley, rebuilt twice out of its own ashes
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Frank Lloyd Wright built his home and studio in 1911 on the brow of a hill in his mother's family's valley south of Spring Green, named it Taliesin, and then spent the rest of his life rebuilding it — twice out of fire, and continuously out of his own restlessness. It is a National Historic Landmark, part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing, and it is visitable only the way its operator sells it: on a ticketed tour, beginning at a visitor center down the road, with a shuttle ride to ground the public does not wander on its own.

## 1. Trip gate

Taliesin Preservation — the nonprofit that runs tours of the estate, which is owned by the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (see §2) — states on its Tours page, checked November 26, 2026: "Taliesin Preservation offers tours of Taliesin, the iconic home, studio, and 800-acre estate of architect Frank Lloyd Wright, located in Spring Green, Wisconsin." Every tour begins at the Frank Lloyd Wright Visitor Center, in the operator's words: "Tour Start: Begin at the Frank Lloyd Wright Visitor Center, where you'll take a shuttle to the estate." There is no self-guided entry to the estate in the operator's published terms; the one partial exception the page names is a "Self-Paced House Tour," offered May–October starting at 3:00 PM, which is still a ticketed tour product.

The operator's seasonal operating hours, as published: April — house tours only, Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays, 10:00 AM – 4:00 PM CST; May 1st – October 31st — open daily, 9:00 AM – 5:00 PM CST; November – December 13th — house tours only, Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays, 10:00 AM – 4:00 PM CST. ## 2. The story
The valley came first. The Wisconsin Historical Society's National Register record for Taliesin states that the valley near Spring Green, in the Driftless Area of southwestern Wisconsin, was settled in the 1860s by Wright's mother's family, the Lloyd Joneses; the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation's history adds that Wright acquired, in his mother's name, nearly 32 acres in the valley in the spring of 1910, placing his home on the "brow" of the hill — "one of my favorite places when I was a boy." The name he chose, Taliesin, is Welsh for "shining brow," and the building was set into the brow of the hill rather than on its crown.

In 1911, per the Wisconsin Historical Society, Wright began Taliesin as a home, studio, and farm for himself and Mamah Borthwick. The estate grew around the house over the following decades — the National Park Service's landmark record (National Register reference 73000081) treats the property as a district whose contributing parts, as the federal record summarizes them, include the Taliesin residence itself, the Hillside Home School buildings with their drafting studio and theater, the Romeo and Juliet Windmill, the Midway Barn farm complex, the Tan-y-Deri house, the dam, and the designed landscape. The operator's current description gives the scale: a 37,000-square-foot complex of home, studio, and school on an estate the operator counts at 800 acres, with buildings from nearly every decade of Wright's career, from the 1890s to the 1950s (the acreage figure conflicts across sources; see §6).

On August 15, 1914, the house's living quarters were destroyed by a fire set by a servant, who murdered seven people, including Mamah Borthwick, while Wright was working in Chicago. That sentence is carried here by three sources in agreement: the Wisconsin Historical Society's record ("one of Wright's servants set fire to the building and murdered seven people, including Borthwick"), the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation's history ("a servant set fire to the living quarters and murdered seven people, including Borthwick"), and Taliesin Preservation's own history page ("In 1914, arson destroyed the living quarters of Taliesin – one-third of the house – and seven were murdered"). The event is stated here in that plain form and no further: the fuller detail carried by later accounts is addressed in §6. Wright rebuilt. The Foundation's history quotes his stated reason — Taliesin "should live to show something more for its mortal sacrifice than a charred and terrible ruin on a lonely hillside in the beloved Valley" — and dates the second house, Taliesin II, complete by the fall of 1915.

In April 1925 the living quarters burned a second time. The Wisconsin Historical Society dates the second fire to April 22, 1925, and gives its cause as likely electrical; the Foundation's history describes a powerful storm causing a devastating electrical fire the same month. Wright rebuilt again — Taliesin III — and Taliesin III is the house that stands today, though "stands" understates a building its architect kept cutting into for another three decades.

In 1932 Frank and Olgivanna Lloyd Wright founded the Taliesin Fellowship at the estate — in Taliesin Preservation's words, "a community that provided architectural training with a holistic, 'learn by doing' approach," whose apprentices designed and worked on structures across the property. The Wisconsin Historical Society's record lists what the Fellowship years did to the ground: apprentices added a 5,000-square-foot drafting studio to the Hillside Home School, remodeled the original Hillside gymnasium into a theater, expanded Midway Barns, and made scores of changes to the residence. A third fire, on April 26, 1952, consumed one wing of the Hillside structure, and Wright used the loss to redesign its dining room and theater (Wisconsin Historical Society). From 1937, Wright wintered at Taliesin West in Arizona and returned to Wisconsin each summer; the National Park Service's landmark guide records that he lived at Taliesin each summer until his death in 1959.

The designations came after. The National Register record (reference 73000081, National Park Service) carries the property as a district listed in 1973, significant in landscape architecture, education, and architecture, with significant years 1911 and 1959. Taliesin was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1976 — the Taliesin Preservation Act findings in Congress give the date as January 7, 1976 — and in 2019 it became a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the group listing "The 20th-Century Architecture of Frank Lloyd Wright" (operator and National Park Service). Ownership and care divide as the Wisconsin Historical Society states them: "Today, the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation owns the Taliesin estate," and "Tours at the Taliesin estate are run by Taliesin Preservation, Inc., a separate organization."

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a 1911 home, studio, and farm on nearly 32 acres acquired in Anna Lloyd Jones's name (Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation), on a hill brow above a valley Wright's mother's family had settled in the 1860s. **Now:** an estate the operator counts at 800 acres (Taliesin Preservation) — or 600 acres in the Wisconsin Historical Society's and Congress's figures (see §6) — holding buildings from the 1890s to the 1950s, a National Historic Landmark since 1976 and a World Heritage Site since 2019, entered only on the operator's tours.

**Then:** living quarters built 1911, burned by arson in 1914; rebuilt as Taliesin II by the fall of 1915; burned again in April 1925. **Now:** Taliesin III, the third house on the brow, continuously altered by its architect and his Fellowship until 1959 and preserved since — the building in the §7 photograph, photographed from the slope below it, is that third house as the Highsmith transparency records it.

**Then:** a working farm and an architect's retreat from professional Chicago. **Now:** the summer seat, historically, of an architectural school — the Fellowship founded in 1932 turned farm buildings into drafting rooms, dormitories, and a theater, and the estate's public face is a visitor center in Wright's Riverview Terrace building, adapted to that use by Taliesin Preservation in 1993 (operator history page).

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its source attached:
- The Taliesin residence (Taliesin III) on the brow of the hill — the third house on the site, rebuilt after the April 1925 fire and altered through 1959 (Wisconsin Historical Society; Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation)
- The Hillside Home School complex, including the drafting studio added by Fellowship apprentices and the theater remodeled from the original gymnasium and redesigned again after the April 26, 1952 fire (Wisconsin Historical Society)
- The Romeo and Juliet Windmill, restored in 1992 by Taliesin Preservation in partnership with the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation (operator history page)
- Midway Barns (Midway Barn), the estate's agricultural center from the 1930s until Wright's death (Wisconsin Historical Society)
- Tan-y-Deri, the house Wright designed for his sister and her husband, counted among the district's contributing buildings in the federal record (National Park Service)
- The Frank Lloyd Wright Visitor Center — Wright's Riverview Terrace restaurant building, opened as a restaurant in 1967 and purchased and adapted as the visitor center by Taliesin Preservation in 1993 (operator history page); the operator's Tours page describes it as housing the Riverview Terrace Café and the Wright Gift Shop, in "Wright's only remaining restaurant, now on the National Register of Historic Places"
- The designed landscape itself — the hill, the dam and pond, the courtyard and gardens — which the federal record lists as contributing to the landmark district (National Park Service)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — and for this entry the conditions are the operator's: the estate is reached on a ticketed tour from the visitor center, by shuttle, and no part of this progression is a direction to enter the estate any other way:

1. **At the Frank Lloyd Wright Visitor Center (5607 County Highway C):** Start where the operator starts you. The building is itself a Wright work — his Riverview Terrace restaurant — before it is a ticket desk. Every tour boards its shuttle here.
2. **On the shuttle ride to the estate:** The approach is part of the design. The estate was composed as a landscape, and the federal record lists the landscape among the landmark's contributing parts — read the valley, the dam, and the hill as the first exhibit.
3. **On the House Tour, in the residence:** The third house on the brow. Hold the three numbers together — 1911, 1914, 1925 — and read the living room as a room built three times, the last version standing.
4. **At Hillside, if your tour includes it:** The school the Fellowship rebuilt into its own workplace — drafting studio, theater — the estate's second center, and the building fire reached a third time, in 1952.
5. **From the slope below the house, where ground open to your tour permits:** The §7 photograph's angle — the house riding the brow of the hill, neither on top of it nor hidden by it. That placement is the whole argument of the place, visible in one frame.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The 1914 death toll conflicts between the older federal paperwork and every institutional history checked in this pass. The Wisconsin Historical Society, the Frank Lloyd Wright Foundation, and Taliesin Preservation each print **seven** murdered, and §2 uses seven on that agreement. A count of **eight** circulates in readings of the 1970s landmark nomination. The nomination itself was not re-read line by line in this pass, so the discrepancy prints here unreconciled: seven in the text, on three named sources; eight flagged, not adopted.

The estate's acreage prints two ways. Taliesin Preservation, the operator, says 800 acres, twice, on pages checked for this entry. The Wisconsin Historical Society's record says the estate "totals 600 acres," and the findings of the Taliesin Preservation Act of 1994 describe "the nationally significant 600-acre site." No source checked explains the difference — later acquisition and different counting are both possible and neither is asserted here. Both figures print, with their sources, and neither is used without one.

The county prints two ways. The National Park Service's National Register record places Taliesin in **Iowa County** — the property lies in the Town of Wyoming, south of Spring Green — while the Wikimedia Commons category record for the building gives Sauk County, which is the county of the village of Spring Green and of the postal address. The federal record is used in §10; the conflict is printed here because a visitor's map will show both.

The National Register listing date also varies in secondary lists (March 14 and April 14, 1973 both circulate); the National Park Service's own asset record for reference 73000081 gives the published date as April 14, 1973, and that is the date this entry relies on, without printing it as a headline fact. The second fire's date is given by the Wisconsin Historical Society as April 22, 1925; the Foundation's history says only April 1925 and describes a storm-driven electrical fire, where the Society says the cause was "likely" electrical. §2 prints April 1925 and carries both cause accounts.

Finally, prices: the operator's Tours page prints exactly one price — the $66 House Tour — and this entry prints exactly that one. Any fuller price list in circulation comes from pages other than the operator's own Tours page as checked November 26, 2026, and is not carried here. The operator also notes its 2027 tour schedule was not yet live at this check; the seasonal hours in §1 are the operator's current published terms, not a promise about any future season.
