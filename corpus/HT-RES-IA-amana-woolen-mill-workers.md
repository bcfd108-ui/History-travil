# Amana — The weaver who went out to buy wool

*Iowa OP02 research draft for Dash.*

## 2. Story

### The mill had to earn outside money

The Amana Colonies’ textile business had roots in the Community of True Inspiration’s earlier mills. The Amana tourism account dates the Main Amana mill to 1860 and the start of work on the millrace to 1867. This was a community industrial undertaking, with wool bought and cloth sold beyond the villages. A historian trying to read the buildings should keep separate the date a mill business began and the ages of its different surviving structures. The federal photograph of the boiler and dye houses, for example, captions the smokestack’s construction as 1901–02. [3–5]

### A job could move from the loom to the farm gate

Historian Peter Hoehnle reports the oral history of Helene Schmieder Rind. At twenty-one she started in the mill as a weaver despite her father’s objections. Manager Peter Zimmermann made her an assistant and sent her to buy wool from area farmers. One farmer initially refused to “sell wool to a skirt,” in the recollection transmitted by Hoehnle. She won over suppliers. After Zimmermann died in February 1933, business manager Arthur Barlow relied on her knowledge across departments, and she effectively ran the mill while he sought a manager. She later served as assistant manager and became the first woman on the Society’s board. [6]

The mill’s own published history remembers Rind asking for office work and being hired as an assistant. It also names Helen Leichsenring, who spent most of her reported 67 years attaching bindings to blanket edges with an industrial sewing machine, and her daughter Ramona, who later worked with her. This is an operator account. It introduces the labor behind a finished blanket while leaving payroll dates and original interviews open. [13]

### The Great Change changed the workday

In 1932 the reorganization of Amana’s communal system rearranged both the place and payment of work. Hoehnle traces the closure of communal kitchens and the ways women moved into paid work, including at the mills. The same study transcribes Barlow’s April 27, 1932 letter to Carl Hess, a Homestead broom maker. Barlow wrote that the “new system will go into effect next Monday morning.” Hess’s shop was to close for the time being. This letter concerns a different worker and shop. Read it alongside Schmieder’s trajectory to see how the same reorganization created openings and shut others. [6–7]
