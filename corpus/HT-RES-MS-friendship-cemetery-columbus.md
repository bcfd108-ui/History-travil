# Friendship Cemetery and the names absent from a reconciliation story

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP16*

## 2. Story

In April 1866 women in Columbus gathered at the cemetery to decorate soldiers’ graves. The names in the historic landscape survey include Matt Morton, Mrs. J. T. Fontaine and Mrs. Green T. Hill, who met at the Morton house before the observance. The local *Mississippi Index* led with the Confederate dead, then praised the women because “no distinction was made” when they also honored buried United States soldiers. Those are the newspaper’s words as transcribed in the survey. They show how a local editor framed the event on April 26, 1866, and whose names the excerpt leaves unrecorded. [2, pp. 5–6]

The burial landscape had already become a wartime record. The survey reports that Columbus received wounded men and prisoners, including from Shiloh. Its burial figures give 2,194 Confederate and 49 Union dead buried here. It places the subsequent transfer of the Union burials to Corinth National Cemetery in October 1867. One year after the flowers, a family or bereaved comrade returning to Columbus might soon have needed another address. The attrition that filled the cemetery and the transfer that changed it are part of the same story. [2]

Francis Miles Finch’s *The Blue and the Gray* appeared in the September 1867 *Atlantic Monthly*. Its introductory notice describes women decorating Confederate and National soldiers’ graves at Columbus. Read the notice alongside the local paper's 1866 report and the date of the transfer. The magazine carried an account about flowers laid on both sets of graves while the Union dead were still buried at Columbus. The observed act, the magazine’s later account, and the many subsequent claims of national origin concern different things. [2][3]

## 3. Then and now

Union Lodge No. 35 of the Independent Order of Odd Fellows established the cemetery in 1849. The local chapter of the burial story now sits within a city cemetery, transferred in 1957 and later expanded. The landscape survey describes three early ellipses and a later gridded area. The grounds still contain burial and mourning space. The historic 35-acre nomination boundary and the later cemetery property are different extents. [2][4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

- **Public outdoor boundary.** The signed Fourth Street South entrance and customary open visitor area, subject to posted directions, active services and weather. No cemetery paths are designated as a route. [1][2]
- **Historical evidence outside this visit.** The Morton house locates the planning meeting in the reported account, and Corinth is the separate reinterment location. Neither is a stop in this entry. [2]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

If the public gate is open, take a quiet moment at the entrance where the cemetery’s own history marker stands. Leave any space needed for visitors attending a burial. **Strongest story moment — The graves that moved.** Read the newspaper’s April 1866 account beside the survey’s date of reinterment. Picture families turning to two towns for a record of the same wartime deaths. [2][3]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The heritage-area page credits this observance as the original one leading to national Memorial Day. The HALS report reviews several nearly simultaneous observances and calls the underlying motivations in Columbus uncertain. This dossier establishes the Columbus event and what its local paper reported. It keeps the national origin unsettled. [1][2]
- The state property entry prints July 1949 for the first recorded burial despite an 1849 establishment date and its own discussion of early burials. The HALS narrative dates the cemetery’s earliest burial to 1849. The initial ledger has not been read and the listing typo cannot substitute for it. [2][4]
- The excerpts give contemporaneous “about forty” Federal graves and the later burial total 49. The count scopes and methods could differ. The underlying burial ledger and original April 26 paper issue were not retrieved. [2]
- Posted gate hours and a complete list of the organizers or women decorating remain unverified. No claim to participation or motive is assigned to those unnamed in these records.
