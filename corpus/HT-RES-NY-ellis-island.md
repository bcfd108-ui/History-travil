# HT-RES-NY-ellis-island — The fireproof hall in the harbor, rebuilt after the wooden station burned
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A small oyster island in Upper New York Bay, enlarged by landfill into a 27.5-acre federal station, where between 1892 and 1954 the United States inspected the largest share of its newcomers — and where the brick Main Building that replaced a burned wooden depot still stands as a museum of that passage.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is an island, reached only by boat: Ellis Island, in Upper New York Bay, administered by the National Park Service as part of Statue of Liberty National Monument since May 11, 1965 (NPS fact sheet). The island's Main Building houses the Ellis Island National Museum of Immigration. The NPS fees page states the terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: the National Park Service does not charge an entrance fee to visit the museums on Liberty Island and Ellis Island, but a ferry ticket from Statue City Cruises — the only NPS-authorized ferry provider, and the only ferry permitted to dock at the islands — is required. The page's ferry table lists $26.00 for adults (13+), $23.00 for seniors (62+), $17.00 for children 4–12, and $0 for children under 4; ranger tours are free, audio tours are included, and the Ellis Island Hard Hat Tour of the south-side hospital complex adds $55 and is open only to visitors over 13, subject to availability. The authorized on-site ticket office is inside Castle Clinton in The Battery, Manhattan; ferries also depart from Liberty State Park in Jersey City. America the Beautiful passes do not cover the ferry, because the ferry charge is a transportation fee, not an entrance fee. The property was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966, as part of the listing "Statue of Liberty National Monument, Ellis Island and Liberty Island" (NRHP reference no. 66000058, per the NPS's own National Register record).

## 2. The story
The island had other names and other uses before it had a registry room. The NPS fact sheet records that local Indigenous tribes called it "Kioshk," or Gull Island, and that for its oyster beds and shad runs it was known as Oyster Island through the Dutch and English colonial periods. The 1975 National Register nomination adds that the island took its present name from its last 18th-century owner, Samuel Ellis, and came into federal possession as a harbor-defense site, its early batteries superseded by the Fort Gibson of the War of 1812 period. For most of the nineteenth century it remained a military post in the harbor. In 1890 President Benjamin Harrison designated it the site of a federal immigration station, and in 1891 the Immigration Act put immigration under federal administration, created the Office of Immigration, and appropriated money to build the new inspection station; the Marine Hospital Service took responsibility for examining arriving immigrants' health.

Before construction began, the island was doubled in size with landfill, a ferry slip was dredged, and older military buildings were adapted for reuse. The first immigration building, built of Georgia pine, opened on **January 1, 1892**. The first person processed that day, as the station's own tradition and the NPS's published accounts report, was Annie Moore of County Cork, Ireland, traveling with her two younger brothers; some 700 immigrants passed over the docks on the first day. Immigration was light in the depression years that followed — fewer than 20,000 arrivals a year, in the NPS's account — and then, on **June 15, 1897**, fire destroyed the complex of wooden buildings. About 140 immigrants and numerous employees were on the island; no one was killed.

The government announced almost immediately that Ellis Island would be rebuilt in fireproof materials. The New York firm of Boring and Tilton won the design competition, against a field that included McKim, Mead & White. The first of the new buildings, and the one that still defines the island, was the Main Immigration Building: red brick with limestone trim in the NPS's description, holding a baggage room on its first floor and the great Registry Room — the second-floor examination hall — above it. It opened on **December 17, 1900**. The Kitchen and Laundry and the Powerhouse followed in 1901; the island was enlarged again by landfill for a hospital complex, and the Main Hospital Building opened in March 1902, built for 125 patients and promptly overwhelmed, in the NPS's account, by immigrants diagnosed with trachoma, favus, and other contagious illnesses that warranted exclusion. Over the next seven years the hospital complex grew — an administration building, a hospital extension, a psychopathic ward — and a further enlargement of the island made room for a separate Contagious Disease Hospital and isolation wards.

The peak came quickly. The NPS's published figures put the busiest year at 1907, with 1,004,756 immigrants processed, and the single busiest day on April 17, 1907, when 11,747 people arrived. Then the law narrowed the gate. The Emergency Quota Act of 1921 ended the open-door policy, limiting each nationality to 3 percent of its representation in the 1910 census; the Immigration Act of 1924 cut that to 2 percent of the 1890 census and, just as consequentially for this island, allowed prospective immigrants to be inspected at consulates before they sailed — making the trip to Ellis Island unnecessary for most. The island's later years were largely detention and deportation work. The Public Health Service vacated the hospital facilities on March 1, 1951, and the Ellis Island U.S. Immigration Station ceased operation on **November 12, 1954**, after what the NPS's current pages count as almost 12 million immigrants processed between 1892 and 1954 (see §6 for the larger figure the older NPS documents carry).

The island then sat empty for three decades. By presidential proclamation in 1965 it became part of Statue of Liberty National Monument, under the National Park Service. The Main Building was restored — the HABS documentation of the 1980s records extensive restoration undertaken in the mid-1980s around the Statue of Liberty's centennial — and on **September 10, 1990**, after 30 years of abandonment, it opened as a museum. The NPS fact sheet adds the claim the island now carries into American family memory: it is believed that approximately 40 percent of America's population can trace their ancestry through Ellis Island.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a 3.3-acre island (the NPS fact sheet's figure; see §6) of oyster beds and harbor batteries. **Now:** 27.5 acres of filled land in three once-separate numbered islands joined by fill, holding the Main Building, the hospital complexes, and the museum — the growth itself, from ballast, subway excavation, and landfill, is part of the record the NPS fact sheet carries.

**Then:** a Georgia-pine depot that burned to the ground in a single day, June 15, 1897, with no loss of life. **Now:** the fireproof brick Main Building of December 17, 1900, its Registry Room restored as the museum's central hall.

**Then:** a second-floor examination hall through which, in the HABS survey's words, all immigrants at the station passed after 1900, with Public Health Service doctors examining for trachoma, favus, and other excludable illnesses. **Now:** the same hall walked at museum pace, with the south-side hospital buildings — closed to ordinary visitation — opened only on the guided Hard Hat tours the NPS fees page lists.

## 4. What's still there
- The Main Immigration Building (opened December 17, 1900; Boring and Tilton), now the Ellis Island National Museum of Immigration, with the Registry Room on its second floor
- The Kitchen and Laundry Building and the Powerhouse (1901), per the NPS immigration-station history
- The Main Hospital Building (opened March 1902) and the hospital complex on the island's south side, including the Contagious Disease Hospital and isolation wards — visitable only on guided Hard Hat tours
- The ferry slip and dock ground where arriving barges landed passengers from the steamship piers
- The American Family Immigration History Center's searchable passenger-manifest database for the Port of New York, 1892–1924 (NPS fact sheet; the underlying manifests are held by the National Archives and Records Administration)
- The island ground itself: 27.5 acres, enlarged from the original island by successive landfill campaigns between the 1890s and the 1930s

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **On the ferry:** The only way in — Statue City Cruises from The Battery or Liberty State Park, ticketed as the NPS fees page states. The approach across the ferry slip is the arrival every immigrant in this story also made, by barge from the steamship docks.
2. **At the Main Building's first floor:** The baggage room level, where the inspection sequence began after December 17, 1900.
3. **In the Registry Room:** The second-floor examination hall — the room the HABS record says every immigrant at the station passed through — where legal and medical inspection decided admission, detention, or exclusion.
4. **At the hospital side:** The Main Hospital Building of March 1902 and, on a Hard Hat tour, the south-side contagious-disease complex that the quota-era island grew into.
5. **At the manifest records:** The American Family Immigration History Center, where the 1892–1924 passenger manifests can be searched against a family name.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The headline count does not agree across the National Park Service's own publications, and the disagreement is printed rather than resolved. The NPS's current immigration-station history says "almost 12 million immigrants were processed" between 1892 and 1954, and its fact sheet says "over 12 million." The older NPS documents say more: the 1975 National Register nomination states the island was the gateway "for more than 16 million immigrants between 1892 and 1954," and the HABS Main Building data pages repeat that "over 16 million people passed through the main building." Both figures are carried here as their sources state them; this draft does not reconcile them, and the difference may reflect counting all passengers and crew against steerage immigrants processed — but no source consulted says that, so it is not asserted.

The island's original size is also carried two ways: the NPS fact sheet says the island was enlarged "from its original 3.3 acres to 27.5 acres," while the NPS Foundation Document for the monument says "the original 3.5-acre island was increased to 10 acres from 1890 to 1892." Both print as their sources carry them.

Annie Moore's age is not printed in §2 because the published accounts do not agree: accounts of the January 1, 1892 opening describe her variously as 15 and as 17. Her name, her County Cork origin, her two brothers, and her place as the first person processed are consistent across the accounts; her age is not, and no primary record settling it was consulted in this pass.

The NPS National Register record for this listing (66000058) carries no latitude or longitude, so the locator below prints none. The 1975 nomination works at district scale and gives boundary latitude/longitude corners for the nominated area rather than a single point for the island or the Main Building; a corner is not printed here as if it were the place. Coordinates appearing in lead-list sources were not used.

The first photo's records (§7) name no photographer and carry no capture date for the frame, so neither a photographer nor a year is printed for it. The scaffolding visible on one tower in the frame is consistent with the mid-1980s restoration the HABS data pages describe, but consistency is not a date, and none is asserted.
