## 2. The story

The Army post was the second "Fort Boise." The first was the HBC trading post near the Snake River, abandoned in the mid-1850s. After violence on the Oregon Trail in the Snake country — the 1854 Ward party killings and the 1860 Utter party attack among the events cited — Army commanders recommended a permanent post. The Civil War delayed it until the 1862 Boise Basin gold rush. On **January 14, 1863** the Secretary of War authorized a new post about forty miles up the Boise River from the old trading post. **Maj. Pinckney Lugenbeel** left Fort Vancouver on June 1, reached the valley on June 28, and formally located the post on **July 4, 1863**. While Oregon cavalry built the first log structures, a townsite was laid out beside the post. That town became Boise. [1, 2]

The fort's most durable work was done in stone. The Army brought stonemason **Charles May**, and sandstone quarried in the nearby foothills went into the post's first permanent buildings. **Building 6**, the post quartermaster's building (100 × 30 ft), carries a carved gable date of **"Sept. 1, 1864"**. **Building 1**, an 1864 officer's house on the original Officers' Row, is built of large, irregular, closely fitted sandstone blocks. **Building 4** combines a sandstone wing with a brick portion (blueprints suggest 1870; unverified); ACHP calls it the **Surgeon's Quarters**, home to post surgeons and their families for more than 120 years. Later Army building added Federal-revival brick quarters (Nos. 23–24, 1905; No. 44, 1909), a 1909 gymnasium and post exchange (No. 29), a 12-bed Army hospital (No. 33, 1908), cavalry barracks (No. 34), and a Prairie-style commanding officer's house (No. 45, 1910). [1, 6, 7]

The fort's military purpose was campaigning against the region's Native peoples, the Shoshone, Bannock and Northern Paiute. The 1972 nomination, written in the language of its time, frames this as controlling "Indians" and records the post's role in the **Snake War (1864–1868)**: the 1st Oregon Cavalry headquarters moved there on August 28, 1864; Gov. Caleb Lyon held a council there on October 10, 1865, whose treaties the Senate never ratified; a Military District of Boise was run from the post in 1866–67; and **Gen. George Crook** arrived in December 1866. The post was again a base in the **Bannock War (1878)** and the **Sheepeater campaign (1879)**. Soldiers also intervened in territorial politics (1867) and the Owyhee mining-claim war at Silver City (1868), and the post was a social center for early Boise: theater ("Fort Boise Varieties"), services, band concerts, holiday festivities. [1]

The post was renamed **Boise Barracks on April 5, 1879**. Troops left in **1912–1913** (see §6). Soldiers returned in **1916** to prepare for the Mexican border campaign and again in **1942–44**. From about 1919–20 the grounds served veterans: first the U.S. Public Health Service, then the Veterans Bureau and the Veterans Administration, which still runs the Boise VA Medical Center there. Land not needed for the hospital was ordered turned over to the State of Idaho on March 14, 1944 (nomination). In 2013, the fort's sesquicentennial, Preservation Idaho led a volunteer- and donor-funded exterior rehabilitation of the vacant Surgeon's Quarters, the first use of the VA Secretary's authority to accept such a donation for preservation. [1, 2, 6, 8]

**Reflection posture:** the founding of Boise and the dispossession of Shoshone, Bannock and Northern Paiute people are one story. Their own accounts of the Snake War and the Bannock War are not in the Army and VA sources used here (§6). This dossier records the fort's campaigns and does not celebrate them.

## 4. What's still there

- **On the active VA campus (no public tour):** Building 1 (1864 officers' quarters), Building 4 (Surgeon's Quarters; rehabilitated 2013), Building 6 (1864 quartermaster building), and Army-era brick buildings recorded in 1972 (Nos. 13, 23, 24, 29, 33, 34, 42, 44, 45; and No. 69, a later sandstone gate house). Current survival of each 1972-listed building was not verified. [1, 2, 6]
- **Public:** the Fort Boise marker on N. 5th St. (finder location) and Fort Boise Park. [3, 5]
- **Gone:** the 1863 log buildings; many original and early structures were demolished (per the nomination's building narrative). [1]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Army departure:** nomination — troops stationed "until 1912"; VA — post "formally abandoned in 1913." [1, 2]
- **When veterans' care began and who ran it:** nomination — "a veterans hospital occupied the main part of the grounds from 1920 on"; ACHP — USPHS acquired 1919, VA acquired 1938; Intermountain Histories (secondary) — loaned by Gov. D. W. Davis to USPHS, then the Veterans Bureau, then VA in 1930; VA history page — VA "from 1950 to the present." Unreconciled; deed and federal transfer records not consulted. [1, 2, 6, 8]
- **Utter vs. "Otter" party:** the 1972 nomination's text reads "Otter massacre" (as transcribed); VA's page names the Utter party, Sept. 9–10, 1860. "Utter" is used here. [1, 2]
- **Building 4 date:** later blueprints assign 1870; the nomination says verification is lacking. ACHP treats it as one of three early buildings still standing. [1, 6]
- **Building numbers on the 1907 map vs. the 1972 narrative:** the 1907 Quartermaster map labels No. 45 "Field Off." (the nomination dates No. 45 to 1910 as commanding officer's quarters). The map may carry later annotations. Unresolved.
- **Fort Boise Park's history:** the city's park page (checked) gives amenities and hours but no acquisition history. A secondary search summary says the city acquired it from the federal government in 1950; not verified from a city or deed record. Its location inside the 1972 rectangle is computed (§7), not documented.
- **Native perspectives:** no Shoshone-Bannock, Shoshone-Paiute, or Northern Paiute published account was consulted for this dossier. A Tribal voice should be sourced before publication (the Parma dossier cites *Sho-Ban News*, June 15, 2017 as a starting point).
- **Marker:** inscription and location known only from HMdb (finder); the marker's sponsor and date were not established.
- **Gen. Jonathan Wainwright** is named in the nomination as stationed there early in his career; dates not verified.
