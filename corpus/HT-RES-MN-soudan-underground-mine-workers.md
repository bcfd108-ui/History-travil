## 2 · Story — The cable that joined two workplaces

Start above the shaft. For a miner, a shift underground depended on people working overhead. The National Historic Landmark file describes the engine house with a 600-horsepower motor, a 39-foot cable drum and 3,000 feet of cable. It gives 1,000 feet per minute as the hoisting speed for rock and ore. Keep that number with the hoist’s industrial work. The DNR describes the current passenger descent separately, about three minutes to level 27. [2,4]

Early miners worked open pits. As work went underground, the necessary surface plant grew around the shaft. The landmark file lists air compressors, generators, the drill shop and a dry house with 200 lockers. A drill bit that wore down on ore could be sharpened in the shop above. The dry house is a reminder of how a shift ended for a person, as the crusher and conveyor show what happened next to the ore. The federal survey also records the trolley and a miners’ lunchroom. [4–5]

The landmark investigator reported a third-generation retired miner’s recollection of his grandfather: “in the open pit days the wagon went by every day with the bodies.” The investigator does not provide a death count or identify that grandfather. The recollection records what a mining family carried forward about the pits. Read it quietly. [4]

Charlemagne Tower’s railroad linked the ore to Two Harbors on Lake Superior. The Two Harbors dossier follows the same journey to the docks. The landmark file gives more than 568,000 long tons shipped and 1,800 men employed at the 1892 peak. These are the file’s figures. A payroll and reporting-period definition were unavailable. [4,6]

## 3 · Then / now

Then, drills and hoists supplied a railroad and shipping system. Commercial operation ended in 1962. U.S. Steel gave the mine and land to Minnesota in 1963 and DNR dates the first tours to 1965. Today staff use maintained equipment to take seasonal passengers 2,341 feet down, then an underground train approaches the Montana stope, the last working area according to DNR. The 2,500-foot maximum mine depth reported in the historic survey describes a different scope from the tour. [2,4–5]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

- **Opening year.** DNR’s tour text counts operation from 1882. The landmark file dates active mining to 1884 and separately records the company incorporation in 1882. The evidence reviewed leaves the precise first extraction date open. [2,4]
- **First shipment.** MnDOT’s roadside inventory reproduces July 3, 1884 on the northern plaque and July 30, 1884 on the southern commemoration, while its narrative and the landmark file say July 31. The original dispatch log has not been located. Separate this railroad movement from the Lake Superior vessel-loading dates in Two Harbors. [4,6–7]
- **Off-season interiors and personal records.** Individual exhibit doors still need on-site confirmation. No contemporary shift book, miner letter or accident register was available in this pass. The family recollection is a secondhand quotation in the designation file. [1–5]
