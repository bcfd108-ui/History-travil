## 2. The story

The institution's story does not finish when settlers arrive. Its own permanent exhibition sequence carries the visitor from **We Were** through **We Are** to **We Will Be**. The last section gives achievements, aspirations and concerns a place alongside historical disruption. This is interpretation chosen by the community telling its own story, not a generic “vanished people” narrative. [4]

**Named published Tribal institutional voice:** director Bobbie Conner says in the operator's media kit, **“We do not live in the past--the past is alive in us.”** This is a directly attributed published statement, not a recording heard during this research. [3]

A concrete recent example makes that principle tangible. CTUIR's June 10, 2026 announcement describes a cradleboard exhibition including work by CTUIR member **Maynard White Owl Lavadour**. Conner relates the care invested in cradleboards to children's safety, preciousness and a community's expectation of a future. The release says the show ended August 29, so it is evidence for the story, **not a promised November exhibit**. The article also distinguishes items from a newly acquired collection, existing museum holdings and a Maryhill loan; their custody should not be flattened into a single origin. [5]

The strongest visitor question is therefore not “how old is this?” but “what knowledge and responsibility does it carry forward?” That is an editorial prompt, not an invented quotation or an invitation to reproduce a design. The cultural institution, its makers and community retain authority over what is shared.

## 3. Then / now

The operator describes an institution built to preserve the Cayuse, Umatilla and Walla Walla Tribes' history while presenting their account publicly. Its media kit records opening in 1998 and locates its cultural work in the present, not simply in a reconstructed past. [3]

The current exhibits page lists the three-part permanent sequence and features **Wissenbach's Painted Indians**, paintings of Tribal leaders made by a non-Tribal church rector. The artist's perspective must not be substituted for those individuals' voices. No end date or day-of installation check was established. [4]

This dossier's then/now comparison concerns continuity of interpretation and care. It is not a matched photograph, a replica-village access claim or an alternative route to Celilo. Do not collapse the three Tribes into one undifferentiated identity.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Suggested interpretive sequence, not a booked tour:

1. **At admission, establish the day's actual access.** Resolve any necessary accessibility and photography questions before assuming accommodations or recording permission.
2. **Follow the community's chosen exhibition order.** Listen/read before bringing a settler-trail chronology to it. Do not improvise narration over the exhibits.
3. **Give difficult history room.** If displayed material addresses forced displacement or boarding schools, pause without turning loss into a challenge, costume activity or score.
4. **Stay for the present and future.** Do not leave after the historical galleries as if the community's story ended there. Use the currently open future-oriented interpretation, rather than promising a particular video will be running. [4]
5. **End with Conner's statement.** Reflect on one thing the visit changed about the distinction between preserving an object and sustaining knowledge. The cradleboard example in §2 can inform that reflection even though its temporary show has ended.

Allow time to absorb rather than assigning an unsupported minimum duration. If holiday opening or required accommodations cannot be confirmed, defer the physical visit; do not substitute unrestricted reservation sightseeing.
