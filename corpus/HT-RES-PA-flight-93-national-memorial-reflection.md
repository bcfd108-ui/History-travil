# Flight 93: a common field and forty names

Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Reflection only. This entry covers the memorial's own public ground as the National Park Service presents it — the Visitor Center, the Memorial Plaza, the Wall of Names, and the Tower of Voices. It is not a reconstruction of the flight, the crash, or the morning of September 11, 2001 beyond what official records state plainly. The crash site and debris field are the final resting place of the passengers and crew; they are not a visitor destination, and no part of this entry treats them as one. No upload or contact.

## 1. Trip gate

**Flight 93 National Memorial** is a unit of the National Park Service in Stonycreek Township, Somerset County, Pennsylvania. The National Park Service lists the Visitor Center address as **6424 Lincoln Highway, Stoystown, PA 15563**. The gateway entrance is on US Route 30 / Lincoln Highway. [1][2]

The memorial grounds are open daily from sunrise to sunset. The Visitor Center is open **9 a.m. to 5 p.m. daily**, closed on New Year's Day, Thanksgiving Day, and Christmas Day. No fees are charged at Flight 93 National Memorial. The site is self-guided; NPS rangers and volunteer Ambassadors staff the site daily. [3]

The Visitor Center features a permanent exhibition on the Flight 93 story within the context of the larger September 11, 2001 attack; the National Park Service advises planning 45 minutes to one hour for the exhibit space. There are no restrooms inside the Visitor Center; restrooms are across from the parking lot, with a family restroom available May 1 – October 31. Wheelchairs are available for temporary use at the information desk. [2]

Pets are not permitted inside the Visitor Center or on the Memorial Plaza, service animals excepted. There are no picnic, camping, or recreation areas. Visitors and media are not allowed on the crash site. [3][4]

## 2. The story

On **September 11, 2001**, United Airlines Flight 93 — a Boeing 757 bound from Newark, New Jersey, to San Francisco, California — was one of four commercial airliners hijacked that morning in a planned attack against the United States. The flight had been delayed 25 minutes before takeoff. On board were 33 passengers and seven crew members, and four hijackers. The plane crashed in a field in Somerset County, Pennsylvania. There were no survivors. [5]

The National Park Service record states the purpose of the memorial in the words of its enabling law. On **September 24, 2002**, Congress passed the **Flight 93 National Memorial Act (P.L. 107-226)**, "to authorize a national memorial to commemorate the passengers and crew of Flight 93 who, on September 11, 2001, courageously gave their lives thereby thwarting a planned attack on our Nation's Capital." The Act designated Flight 93 National Memorial as a unit of the National Park Service. [6]

A temporary memorial was established on a hilltop overlooking the crash site in early October 2001, with a section of fencing where tributes could be attached. From that same overlook, the families of the passengers and crew viewed the crash site immediately after September 11, 2001. On the evening of September 9, 2011, the flag was lowered over the temporary memorial for the last time. [7]

The permanent memorial was selected through an international design competition that received over 1,100 entries from 27 countries; the design by Paul Murdoch Architects and Nelson Byrd Woltz Landscape Architects was chosen and built in three phases. The Memorial Plaza and the Wall of Names opened to the public on **September 10, 2011**. The Visitor Center Complex was completed four years later. The Tower of Voices completed the memorial design on **September 10, 2020**. [4]

This entry does not reconstruct the sequence of events aboard the aircraft. The Visitor Center exhibition carries that record, in the National Park Service's own presentation, for visitors who choose to go through it in person.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** a field in Somerset County — ground the National Park Service describes as a former surface coal mine and scrap yard landscape, with a grove of hemlock trees at the edge of the crash site. **Now:** a memorial landscape built to restore life to that ground: open meadow, wetlands, and planted groves, with the crash site and debris field held as the focus of the design and the final resting place of the passengers and crew. An approximately 17-ton sandstone boulder marks the general area of impact, seen from the Flight Path Overlook and from the Memorial Plaza, not approached. [4]

**Then:** a temporary memorial on a hilltop — a parking area and a fence where tributes were attached. **Now:** a permanent memorial in three parts across the landscape: the Visitor Center Complex on the hill, the Allée and the 40 Memorial Groves descending toward the crash site, and the Memorial Plaza and Wall of Names along the boundary of the debris field, with the Tower of Voices standing near the entrance. [4][7]

The design statement recorded by the National Park Service, in architect Paul Murdoch's words, asks that the memorial be "quiet in reverence, yet powerful in form, a place both solemn and uplifting," and that it "restore life here, to heal the land." That statement is the interpretive frame the memorial itself offers; this entry adds none of its own. [4]

## 4. What's still there

All of the following is the memorial's own public ground, as the National Park Service presents it:

- **Visitor Center** — permanent exhibition on the Flight 93 story within the context of the September 11, 2001 attack, with artifacts, multi-media, and interactive exhibits; information desk; bookstore. [2]
- **Flight Path Walkway, Portal Walls, and Flight Path Overlook** — the black granite walkway at the Visitor Center leads through the tall Portal Walls along the flight path and includes a timeline of events; at the Overlook, the view opens to the crash site, marked by the sandstone boulder, the hemlock grove, and the Wall of Names. [4]
- **Allée and Memorial Groves** — the tree-lined Allée connects the Visitor Center Complex to Memorial Plaza; 40 Memorial Groves of 40 trees each, planted with Pennsylvania hardwoods, honor the passengers and crew members. The Allée crosses the wetlands on an 800-foot pedestrian Wetlands Bridge near Memorial Plaza. [4]
- **Memorial Plaza** — a quarter-mile walkway alongside the area where Flight 93 crashed; the Plaza marks the northern boundary of the crash site and allows visitors to walk beside the debris field. Outdoor signs in the forecourt present the September 11, 2001 story of Flight 93. [3][4]
- **Wall of Names** — forty polished white marble panels, each inscribed with the name of one passenger or crew member, set along a continuation of the flight path. The space between each panel marks their individuality; from a distance the wall appears solid. An unborn child is honored on its mother's panel. [4]
- **Ceremonial Gate** — hewn hemlock beams with forty angles cut into them, separating the Wall of Names from the crash site. It is opened only on September 11, for family members to visit the site together after the annual remembrance ceremony. [4]
- **Tower of Voices** — a ninety-three-foot-tall musical instrument holding forty wind chimes, one for each passenger and crew member, near the memorial entrance and visible from US Route 30. The chimes are wind-activated and sound at wind speeds as low as 7–10 mph. [4]
- **Excluded, by the memorial's own rule:** the crash site and debris field themselves. Visitors and media are not allowed on the crash site. [4]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

The public anchor is the memorial itself, entered from US Route 30 / Lincoln Highway at 6424 Lincoln Highway, Stoystown. The Tower of Voices stands near the entrance: a ninety-three-foot tower holding forty wind chimes for the forty passengers and crew members, intended by the National Park Service as both a visual and an audible reminder. [1][4]

From the entrance, the Visitor Center is the orientation point. Its permanent exhibition presents the Flight 93 story within the context of the larger attack; outside, the black granite Flight Path Walkway leads through the Portal Walls to the Flight Path Overlook, where the view opens across the Field of Honor to the crash site, the hemlock grove, and the Wall of Names below. [2][4]

The Allée descends from the Visitor Center Complex toward Memorial Plaza, passing the 40 Memorial Groves and crossing the wetlands on the Wetlands Bridge. The Memorial Plaza runs a quarter-mile along the boundary of the crash site and ends at the Wall of Names — forty marble panels, one name on each, with the Ceremonial Gate beyond it, closed to visitors. [3][4]

Nothing in this progression leaves the memorial's public ground, and nothing in it approaches the crash site. The order above follows the memorial's own layout — entrance, Visitor Center, overlook, Plaza — and can be shortened at any point; the grounds are open sunrise to sunset and the visit is self-guided. [3]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The Tower of Voices carries two dates in the National Park Service record, for two different events. The tower structure was dedicated on September 9, 2018. The forty chimes were installed and dedicated on September 10, 2020, and the National Park Service design record states that the Tower of Voices completed the memorial design on September 10, 2020. Both dates are printed here rather than merged. [4][8]

The National Register record also carries two dates. The National Park Service's Record of Decision for the memorial's General Management Plan states that designation as a unit of the national park system "automatically listed the site in the National Register of Historic Places (November 8, 2002)." The National Archives record for the listing carries reference number 04000272. This entry prints the reference number and the Record of Decision's date statement, and does not harmonize them further. [9][10]

The first-photo record below carries no capture date: the NPGallery asset record gives a submission date (November 17, 2021) but no create date or content dates, and no year is printed for the photograph. See §7.

No minute-by-minute account of the flight is attempted here, and none is needed for the visit this entry describes. Details of the investigation and of what was recovered are the Visitor Center exhibition's material, not this entry's; they are not summarized secondhand.
