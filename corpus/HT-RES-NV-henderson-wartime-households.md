## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A job could draw a household west without providing a place for that household to sleep. Read Henderson’s wartime growth through shelter, children, furnishings, and food rather than through production totals.

**Named civilian voice:** UNLV’s record of Della Mae Rostine’s interview by Irene Rostine, dated 31 October 1991, identifies a family that left Missouri in 1942, initially lived in Las Vegas, and subsequently settled in the Henderson townsite. Its archival description emphasizes the difficulty of finding housing with children, obtaining furnishings and groceries, and managing rationing. This is a retrospective oral-history source, not a contemporary diary. The catalog description was recovered through the official archive’s indexed result; direct page retrieval failed. No words are presented here as a quotation from Rostine’s unread full transcript. [2]

**A short recorded voice in transcript form:** Phyllis Webb Clark, interviewed by Lorna Suzette Clark on 7 April 1976, recalled arrival in 1942: “we were very lucky to have a place to live.” The official archive’s indexed transcript identifies this passage on transcript page 4 and describes families improvising shelter in the wider Las Vegas–Henderson area. This is Clark’s retrospective memory, not a claim that every family had the same experience. The full direct page and audio were not successfully retrieved; there is no verified playback timestamp. Her private residential addresses are deliberately omitted. [3]

**Who did not get the same housing choices?** Roosevelt Fitzgerald’s manuscript draft, *A Demographic Impact of Basic Magnesium of Southern Nevada*, uses interviews with Lee Henry Lisby and Sarah Ann Knight to discuss Black migration, housing shortages, self-built shelter, and segregation. The archive’s indexed text identifies those underlying interviews as 1978 sources. Fitzgerald’s analysis is a scholarly intermediary, not an interview conducted for this dossier. It prevents a universal story of equal access to the townsite. Full original interview passages and the large manuscript PDF remain unreviewed, so no verbatim witness quotation or precise neighborhood boundary is asserted. [4]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Household labor made settlement possible, but the ability to find a home was uneven. This is the dossier’s framing, not a statement that the museum currently presents all three accounts together. No chemical processes, weapon information, or industrial operational details are needed to tell it.

## 3. Then / now
Then: the city’s history page describes a wartime influx, temporary housing construction beginning in February 1942, and the development of schools, shops, and other community institutions. It dates incorporation to 16 April 1953. These are different milestones, not contradictory dates for one event. [5]

Now: the county preserves historic buildings as museum interpretation. A county-produced 2026 video description explains that several Heritage Street structures were moved from original locations. That supports treating the street as a curated setting, not as an intact wartime neighborhood; this pass did not verify the Townsite House’s individual move date. [6]

Read a house as material evidence with a documented history, not as a universal household portrait. Neither Rostine nor Clark has been shown to have lived in this displayed house. Do not imply they did. A building’s original location, its museum location, and a narrator’s remembered home are separate records.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
This is an interpretive sequence inside one authorized institution, not a navigational route or a timed commitment.

1. **Establish the gate.** Confirm the museum and house exhibit are open and follow the current visitor map. If not, use the archival reading as the visit’s substitute.
2. **Start with a household, not a plant.** Read the short Clark excerpt and the carefully labeled Rostine catalog summary before looking at a building. Ask what finding shelter would mean with children and limited furnishings.
3. **Examine evidence rather than invent it.** At the open Townsite House interpretation, find the museum’s own construction, relocation, and furnishing labels, if present. Record which distinctions are actually documented. Do not fill missing labels from this draft.
4. **Complicate the model home.** Compare the tidy surviving exhibit with the housing barriers discussed in Fitzgerald’s manuscript. Do not turn absence of segregation interpretation in one display into evidence that segregation did not exist.
5. **End at the strongest story moment:** A preserved room can make a household seem settled; the testimony asks how hard it was to get any room at all, and who was denied equal choices. That contrast is the payoff, not a visit to someone’s present-day home.

Optional unsent onsite question: **Which evidence identifies this house’s actual residents and furnishings, and where can visitors learn about families who could not obtain equivalent housing?** This is a question for interpretation, not a request to disclose private resident information.
