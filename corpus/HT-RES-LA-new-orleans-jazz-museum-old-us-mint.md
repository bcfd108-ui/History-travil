# New Orleans Jazz Museum at the Old U.S. Mint — the only mint that coined for both the United States and the Confederacy, and the vault that now holds Louis Armstrong's first cornet
HT-RES-LA-new-orleans-jazz-museum-old-us-mint · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The ground was a fort before it was a mint. The three-story, E-plan brick building stands on the site of Fort St. Charles — Fort San Carlos of 1792, built by the Spanish on the end of lower Esplanade where an earlier French bastion, Charles' Bastion, had guarded the river approach during the French and Indian War. Spain's plan, approved in 1792 under Governor François Louis Hector, Baron de Carondelet, linked five forts by embankments around the city; Fort San Carlos was the largest. After Spain sold Louisiana to France and France sold it to the United States in 1803, the fort was renamed Fort St. Charles and remained as a barracks and ordnance depot until it was torn down in 1821. For fifteen years the area was a commons. [3, 4]

The mint was a Jacksonian decentralization in brick. On March 3, 1835, Congress established three branch mints — Charlotte, North Carolina and Dahlonega, Georgia, to coin gold from the southern gold rushes, and New Orleans, a center for foreign trade and for gold arriving by river and sea, to mint both gold and silver coins to keep up with a growing America. The United States Mint's own history states the policy plainly: Charlotte and Dahlonega concentrated on processing the miners' gold into coins, while New Orleans minted both gold and silver. Philadelphia architect William Strickland, designer of the Second Bank of the United States, the Philadelphia Mint and the Tennessee State Capitol, designed the building without ever visiting the site. Builders John Mitchell and Benjamin F. Fox erected it between 1835 and 1838, bounded by Esplanade Avenue, Barracks, Decatur and North Peters Streets. The Greek Revival result is secure and functional rather than inspired in the Society of Architectural Historians' judgment: a projecting portico with four Ionic columns flanked by Doric piers, each corner defined by a gray granite pilaster, an exterior of red-tinted stucco scored to resemble granite blocks following the original finish, walls three feet thick at ground level. Strickland had not allowed for the soft soil — he never visited — and structural reinforcements were necessary within a year of completion; James Gallier Sr. inserted iron rods to stabilize the groin vaults. Deposits began in the spring of 1838 — the first silver deposited March 8 and the first gold April 10 in one detailed secondary account — and the first coins, thirty dimes, were minted May 8, 1838, in that account; another secondary account dates the first striking to March 8, 1838. The discrepancy is printed in §6; what is firm is that coinage began in 1838 and that every coin carried the "O" mint mark for New Orleans. In the 1850s, Captain P. G. T. Beauregard — then an army engineer — improved the building's fireproofing, replaced the slate roof with one of corrugated galvanized iron, then a new building material, and added iron balconies; a fuller fireproofing renovation followed under Captain Johnson K. Dunkin in 1856–1859, as the building's street marker records. [1, 3, 4, 5, 8]

The mint is the only building in America to have served both the United States and the Confederacy as a mint — the distinction the Louisiana State Museum prints on its own history of the building. Louisiana seized the mint after seceding in January 1861, and two months later it passed to the Confederate States of America. The American Battlefield Trust's account records that the mint began producing Confederate coinage, particularly half-dollars, but that a shortage of gold bullion compelled it to close operations on April 1, 1861, after which the facility quartered Confederate troops. When Union forces retook New Orleans in 1862, Marines from USS *Pocahontas* raised the United States flag over the mint on April 26, 1862, without an order from Commodore Farragut, who was still seeking the mayor's surrender, and warned onlookers the ship would fire on anyone removing it. A group including New Orleans resident William Bruce Mumford tore the flag down anyway; *Pocahontas* fired, Mumford was injured by a flying piece of brick, and he carried the flag to the mayor at City Hall while onlookers tore at it. Union Major General Benjamin Butler had Mumford tried by military commission for treason and, by Special Order No. 70 of June 5, 1862, ordered him executed. Mumford was hanged on June 7, 1862, in front of the mint, from a flagstaff protruding horizontally from the building in the Battlefield Trust's account — the mint's own marker states simply that he was hanged in front of the Mint on June 7, 1862, for having torn down the United States flag. He was forty-two. A lurid 1905 newspaper retelling that makes him a youth of about eighteen is lore against the record and is flagged in §6. The National Register record gives 1862 as one of the building's three significant years — 1835, 1838, 1862. Reflection posture applies to this ground: an execution site is read with dignity and without scoring language. [1, 3, 8, 9]

After the war the mint was the only one in the South to reopen. Assay operations resumed in the 1870s — the assay office reopened in 1876 in one detailed account — and full minting resumed by 1879, continuing until the last coins were struck in 1909 and the mint was formally decommissioned in 1911. Over its years of operation it produced, in the totals the sources inspected give, approximately $300 million in coins in the Society of Architectural Historians' figure, and over 427 million gold and silver coins with a total face value of over $307 million in the commonly cited count — different units, printed side by side in §6 rather than merged. After 1909 the building passed through a sequence of federal afterlives: assay office until 1932, federal prison from 1932 to 1943, veterans' offices, and United States Coast Guard storage from 1948 to 1965 in the marker's dating. The federal government transferred the building to the State of Louisiana in 1966 — the Louisiana State Museum's system history gives the transfer to the museum in 1966, and the museum's building page gives the same year. Following renovations in 1978–1980 in the marker's dating, it opened to the public in 1981 as a Louisiana State Museum site, housing the Louisiana Historical Center, which gives researchers access to the museum's document and map holdings dating from 1525 to the present. The building was listed in the National Register on March 30, 1973, and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1975 — the oldest extant structure to have served as a United States Mint, and the only one of the three 1835 branch mints that can still be visited in its original form and location, the Dahlonega building having burned in 1878 and the Charlotte building having been dismantled and reconstructed elsewhere in 1936. [1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8]

The jazz came by a different road, and it arrived with a cornet. The New Orleans Jazz Club was founded on Mardi Gras in 1948 by local jazz enthusiasts and musicians, many of them collectors, who almost immediately began dreaming of a museum. The New Orleans Jazz Museum opened its doors in 1961 at 1017 Dumaine Street and was a success from the start, outgrowing the cottage almost immediately as donations flooded in. In 1969 it relocated to display space around Economy Hall in the Royal Sonesta Hotel on Bourbon Street; when the hotel's management changed, it was forced in 1973 to a third location at 833 Conti Street, and despite heroic efforts by the club's board and membership it became financially impossible to keep open, and the collection went into storage. At about the same time the Louisiana State Museum was completing its renovation of the Old U.S. Mint, acquired from the federal government in a decrepit state, and found itself with a grand old building but no star attraction. On September 15, 1977, the entire collection of the New Orleans Jazz Museum was donated to the people of Louisiana and became the New Orleans Jazz Club Collections of the Louisiana State Museum. The Jazz Collection exhibit opened on the second floor of the Old U.S. Mint in the early 1980s under curator Don Marquis. Hurricane Katrina damaged both the building and the jazz collection in 2005; the Mint reopened in October 2007 after over two years of repairs in one account, and in 2011 including a state-of-the-art performance facility in another — the two dates are printed as a conflict in §6 rather than harmonized. The Old U.S. Mint became home to the New Orleans Jazz Museum as a named branch of the state museum in 2018, in the museum system's own history. [6, 7]

What the vault holds now is, in the Louisiana State Museum's own description, the largest and most comprehensive jazz collection of its kind in the world. It chronicles the music and careers of the men and women who created New Orleans jazz, and consists of instruments, pictorial sheet music, photographs, records, tapes, manuscripts and other items ranging from Louis Armstrong's first cornet to a 1917 disc of the first jazz recording ever made. It includes the world's largest collection of instruments owned and played by important figures in jazz — trumpets, cornets, trombones, clarinets and saxophones played by Bix Beiderbecke, Edward "Kid" Ory, George Lewis, Sidney Bechet and Dizzy Gillespie — some 12,000 photographs from the early days of jazz, over 4,000 78 rpm records dating from 1905 to the mid-1950s, several thousand LPs and 45s, approximately 1,400 reel-to-reel tapes, posters, paintings and prints, hundreds of examples of sheet music from late nineteenth-century ragtime to the popular songs of the 1940s and 1950s, several hundred rolls of film, ephemera, and architectural fragments from important jazz venues. The Jazz Museum's research page gives its holdings as over 25,000 items. The cornet at the collection's core is the instrument Armstrong was taught to play as a resident of the Municipal Waif's Home for boys in 1913–1914, sent there for shooting off a pistol on New Year's Eve; the museum's instruments page records that the cornet's non-detachable mouthpiece bears notches the young Armstrong cut in an attempt to aid his embouchure, and that he later described them in print long before the instrument came to light. The building today divides its work by floor: a mint exhibit on the first floor with a Morgan & Orr coin press, a Troemner bullion scale and displays of coins minted on site; jazz exhibitions drawn from the collection; the Louisiana Historical Center and the Donald M. Marquis Reading Room, open by appointment only, for researchers; and a $4-million performance space on the third floor with advanced acoustics and recording equipment, where live jazz is played almost every day and recorded for historical archives. A building built to turn bullion into money now turns performances into archives on its top floor, and keeps the money it made on its first. [1, 7, 10, 11]

## 5. Public progression

Confirm the day's hours first (§6), then enter from Esplanade Avenue and work the building from money to music. Begin on the first floor with the mint itself: the press, the bullion scale and the coins struck in these rooms, and the building's section — three-foot walls, groin vaults held by Gallier's rods, Beauregard's iron roof overhead. **Strongest story moment · The cornet with notches in the mouthpiece.** On the jazz floors, find Armstrong's first cornet and read the museum's instruments note beside it: a boy sent to the Waif's Home for firing a pistol on New Year's Eve cut notches into a non-detachable mouthpiece to help his embouchure, described them in print decades later, and the instrument surfaced matching his description — the collection's founding object is a piece of evidence that verifies its own story. Then stand outside at the front of the building, quietly, where Mumford was hanged in 1862 for tearing down a flag this building flew, and let the two histories share one facade without forcing them together: a mint that coined for two governments, a gallows for a man who pulled one government's flag down, and a museum that now records live music on the third floor for an archive meant to outlast all of it. Finish with a performance if the schedule offers one — the building's current work is audible, and the museum asks to be heard, not only read. [1, 3, 7, 11]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Hours conflict across the operator's own pages.** The Louisiana State Museum page for this museum, checked this run, lists Open Daily 9:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m., last ticket 3:30 p.m., closed on select state holidays, and closed on Mondays during the summer season. The Jazz Museum's own Visit page, checked the same run, lists Tuesday 9:00 a.m.–6:00 p.m. and Wednesday–Monday 9:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m. A second rendering of that same Visit page in search results lists Monday Closed, Tuesday–Sunday 9 a.m.–4 p.m. The three listings cannot all govern the same week. The trip gate therefore requires a phone confirmation (504-568-6993) and adopts none of the three as settled. [1, 2]
- **Address: 400 vs. 420 Esplanade Avenue.** The museum operates and publishes 400 Esplanade Avenue. The National Register record locates the resource at 420 Esplanade Ave. Both are printed; 400 is the visitor address, 420 is the register address. [1, 3]
- **First coinage date.** A detailed secondary account gives the first silver deposit March 8, 1838, the first gold deposit April 10, 1838, dies shipped April 9, and the first coins — thirty dimes — minted May 8, 1838. Another secondary account dates the first striking to March 8, 1838, with a first production run of 367,434 dimes. The deposit date and the striking date appear conflated in the second account. This dossier follows the deposit-then-striking sequence and prints both striking dates as unresolved against a primary mint record, none of which was inspected in this pass. [5]
- **Coinage totals.** The Society of Architectural Historians gives approximately $300 million in coins manufactured here before the 1909 closing. Commonly cited totals give over 427 million coins with a face value of over $307 million. The figures are in different units (value vs. count) and are not reconciled here; neither is rounded into the other. [4]
- **Prison dating.** SAH Archipedia and the street marker give federal prison use from 1932, SAH to 1943. The Louisiana State Museum's building page describes the use as a federal prison "during Prohibition" — which ended in 1933 — followed by Coast Guard use. The 1932 start is consistent; the Prohibition shorthand is not, and the 1932–1943 span is followed in the story. Coast Guard occupancy is dated 1948–1965 by the marker; other accounts describe Coast Guard storage without dates. [1, 4, 8]
- **Katrina reopening: 2007 or 2011.** One account gives the Mint reopening in October 2007 after over two years of repairs following Hurricane Katrina damage in 2005. Another gives a reopening in 2011, now including a state-of-the-art performance facility. The two may describe a building reopening and a later performance-space completion, but the sources inspected do not join them into one sequence, and both dates are printed. [6]
- **Mumford's age in retelling.** The documented record gives William Bruce Mumford as forty-two at his execution on June 7, 1862. A 1905 newspaper retelling of the mint's stories makes him "a youth about eighteen" and Mumford's mother a wandering figure of pathos — a sentimental retelling that contradicts the record on age and is carried here as lore, not evidence. The hanging itself, its date, and its ground in front of the mint are documented in the marker and in Butler's Special Order No. 70 as quoted in the contemporary-derived accounts. [8, 9]
- **Assay-office and decommissioning steps.** Detailed secondary accounts give an assay office reopening in 1876, full minting resuming in 1879, last coinage in 1909, formal decommissioning as a mint in 1911, and assay operations shutting down in 1932. The operator's building page compresses this to resumption "by 1879" and minting ceasing in 1909. The fuller sequence is printed in the story with its secondary status noted; no Treasury annual report was inspected in this pass. [1, 5]
