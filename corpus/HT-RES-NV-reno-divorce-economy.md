## 2. The story
**Barbara Davis** gives the story its worker's viewpoint. In a telephone interview by **Mella Harmon on November 19, 2014**, she recalls choosing a boardinghouse because ranch accommodation cost more than she wished to spend. When the maid left, Davis asked for the job. Her own concise recollection is **“I spent my six weeks there, cleaning the house and sightseeing.”** The downloaded published transcript, printed pages 1–3, was actually read; this is not a claim that the original audio was heard. [5]

Davis is a named person who performed the work, not an invented composite maid. Her account does not establish wages, daily hours, a contract, or the identity of the previous worker. She could not recall the house's location and was uncertain about meals. The uncertainty belongs in the story rather than being solved through an attractive-looking historic building. [5]

This changes the familiar visitor image. A long stay creates recurring tasks: rooms do not prepare themselves for the next occupant. This is an interpretive inference from her labor account, not a quantified reconstruction of Reno employment. The meaningful contrast is between the publicity of a destination and the ordinary work that made lodging possible.

The university project situates migratory divorce within Reno's housing, commerce and entertainment economy, with a historical change to a six-week residency period in 1931. This is historical context only, not present-day legal advice or instructions for obtaining a divorce. [1]

The project also distinguishes guest ranches from other accommodations and notes that some retained working agricultural operations. [6] Their visibility in popular stories does not make them representative of everyone's budget or work. No celebrity spouse, marital allegation, private litigant file or intimate detail is reproduced here. The participant is included for the work she publicly described.
