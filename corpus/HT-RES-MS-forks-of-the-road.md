## 2. Story

William Wells Brown described arriving at Natchez aboard a steamboat while enslaved and made to work for a trader. In the narrative reproduced in the park’s cultural landscape report, he recalled that people were “there kept one week” in a holding pen, during which several were sold. Brown’s account makes the week visible as time spent waiting under guard for a purchaser. The passage identifies Natchez and a pen. It does not identify a surviving plot or establish the exact spot of any individual sale. [3–4]

The park records that an 1833 ordinance excluding slave trading from inside city limits shaped the rise of market lots at the Forks. Traders used those lots to confine people and sell them onward, often after a forced journey from the Upper South. The market’s location changed the terms of city regulation without ending the business. Civil War occupation later changed uses of the ground; the landscape study records refugee camps and U.S. Colored Troops barracks in the area and distinguishes those from the earlier market. [2–3, 5–6]

The visit today uses outdoor panels at one public exhibit area within a changed townscape. Those panels can help a reader place Brown’s account in a larger trade network. Give the longest pause to the people who waited at Natchez and to the voices still missing from the record. [1–4]

## 3. Then and now

Then — Different lots served a market district. Brown’s account describes the experience of forced transportation and confinement in Natchez. The subsequent camp and barracks use belongs to a later phase. [3–6]

Now — NPS identifies a small public exhibit area with freestanding panels and a manacle memorial. These are interpretation, not surviving pens. The historical market district extends beyond the scope of the visit. [1–3, 5]

## 5. Public progression

Begin at the signed outdoor interpretation, during safe daylight conditions. Read the market geography and Brown’s account from within the exhibit area. Stay at that public anchor. [1–4]

**Strongest story moment — One week in a Natchez pen.** Let Brown’s measure of time set the pace at the exhibit that tells how the trade worked. Quiet reflection. [2–4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- NPS’s activities template includes fee and reservation flags, while the operating-hours page specifies daily outdoor access and the activity text describes open wayside exhibits. Follow posted instructions; the flags alone do not establish a ticket or reservation requirement for the sidewalk exhibit. [1–2]
- Brown’s account is quoted from the NPS reproduction. LOC catalogs the printed narrative; the original relevant edition page was not obtained for this return. The line does not locate the pen to an exact historic parcel. [3–4]
- The authorized present standing space is narrower than the former market district. No historical-site photo with open item-level provenance was verified for this dossier. [2–3, 5–6]
