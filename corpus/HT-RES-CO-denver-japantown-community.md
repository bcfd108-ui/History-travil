## 2. The story
**June Yasuno Aochi (Yamashiro) Berk**, interviewed by **Brian Niiya on December 18, 2018**, recalls postwar Denver through cramped housing, friendships and family work. In segment 9, girls make social space in hotel corridors and form a club. In segment 10, she describes her mother and brother making manju while she sold at the front of the family store. The shop also connected people moving between regions. Her compact explanation is **“this is how our store operated.”** [5]

This is neighborhood-making through labor and relationships, not a story that begins or ends with a monument. The transcript also recalls racist harassment and support across community lines. It should not be reduced to a cheerful account of effortless resettlement. The interview heading and segments 9–10 were read, not the recording or entire interview. [5]

The Japanese American Resource Center of Colorado describes community identity as repeatedly built and remade, and frames its own Legacy Project across prewar, wartime, postwar and later generations. This community-published statement supplies a present-day preservation perspective, not an endorsement of this travel draft. [6]

The documentary thread extends beyond one family. Colorado State Library's collection report identifies **Toshiro Tsubokawa** as publisher of the 1962 *Rocky Mountain Journal*, predecessor to *Rocky Mountain JIHO*. It records DPL's contribution of original issues to digitization. That is direct institutional evidence about preservation and collections; no individual newspaper article is presented as read. [7]

## 3. Then / now
- The principal testimony concerns **postwar Denver**, remembered decades later. It is not an Amache school account and does not imply all Denver arrivals came from one incarceration site. [5]
- **1962 onward:** the newspaper collection offers a separate record of community public life. The State Library's 2023 report identifies the final *JIHO* issue as February 14, 2007. [7]
- **Today:** JARCC publishes heritage projects, including newspapers, women's quilt history and other community records. Those pages demonstrate ongoing work, not an automatic walk-in museum or universal rights license. [6]

This dossier is distinct from Salt Lake City's redevelopment story: its focus is Denver's postwar household work, social connections and community publishing. No historical neighborhood boundary is treated as a present visitor-access boundary.
