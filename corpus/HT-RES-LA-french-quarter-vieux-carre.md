# French Quarter (Vieux Carré) Historic District — the original city of New Orleans
HT-RES-LA-french-quarter-vieux-carre · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

New Orleans was founded in 1718 under Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville, and the town that grew up on the crescent bend of the Mississippi was laid out as a formal grid in 1721: the royal engineer Le Blond de la Tour designed the plan and his assistant Adrien de Pauger directed the laying out of the streets. The plan comprised 66 squares forming a parallelogram, with a public square — the Place d'Armes, now Jackson Square — on the riverfront, flanked by ground reserved for church and government. Street names honored French royal houses and Catholic saints — Bourbon, Royal, St. Louis, Burgundy, Toulouse, Dumaine — and the quarter's basic street plan has not changed since. This gridded town, the Vieux Carré, is the original city; everything else in New Orleans grew outward from it. [7, 9]

The first buildings were fragile. Log and bark-and-reed construction met mud and hurricanes — severe storms struck in 1721 and 1722, in the town's first years — and the building stock was rebuilt repeatedly in more durable forms across the French period (1718–1763). France ceded Louisiana to Spain in 1762, confirmed by the Treaty of Paris in 1763, and Spanish administration governed the town for four decades. It is the central irony of the district's name that most of the "French" Quarter a visitor sees is Spanish-era or later fabric: the Great New Orleans Fire of 1788 and a second great fire in 1794 together destroyed some eighty percent of the city's buildings, and the rebuilding that followed was done under Spanish rule and Spanish fire codes. [7, 9]

Those codes remade the streetscape. Wooden siding was banned in favor of fire-resistant brick covered in stucco, structures were built adjacent to one another and close to the curb to form firewalls, and flat tiled roofs replaced the old French peaked roofs — while the still largely French-speaking population kept building in familiar colonial forms adapted from the Caribbean, with timber balconies and galleries. The result, layered over the first half of the nineteenth century, is the mixture the National Register record describes under criteria for both events and architecture: French, Spanish, Creole and American building layered on one unaltered 1721 grid. [1, 9]

The transfer ground of the district is its riverfront square. The ceremonies returning Louisiana from Spain to France (November 30, 1803) and transferring it from France to the United States (December 20, 1803) took place in the Cabildo on the Place d'Armes, and the square remained the civic heart as the American city grew upriver across the commons that became Canal Street — the Creole downtown quarter on one side, the American section on the other. In 1803 the city's population was approximately 8,000, of whom about 2,700 were enslaved people and about 1,300 free persons of color; the district's houses, courtyards and dependencies were built, serviced and inhabited within that order, and the surviving fabric includes the rear dependencies and quarters where enslaved and servant labor lived. The dossier states that plainly because the streetscape's beauty and its labor history are the same buildings. [7]

Through the nineteenth century the Quarter was the city's commercial and residential core — the National Register record lists industry, community planning and landscape architecture among its areas of significance, with a period of significance running from 1700 to 1974 and significant years including 1734 and 1893 — and then, from the early 1900s, it declined. Rents fell, fabric decayed, and the cheap, aging rooms drew the artists and writers who made the Quarter's bohemian reputation. The preservation response began early and became national precedent: New Orleans was the first city in the United States to pass an ordinance creating a historic district, and in 1925, responding to pressure from the local chapter of the American Institute of Architects, the Commission Council established the first Vieux Carré Commission. That first commission was merely advisory and faltered for lack of legal authority. [1, 3, 8]

The authority came from the state's constitution. Act 139 of 1936 amended the Louisiana Constitution of 1921 (Article XIV, Section 22A) — adopted by the state's voters on November 3, 1936 — to authorize the city to create a commission with real power, and the city did so by ordinance on March 3, 1937. The amendment defined the Vieux Carré section in the constitution itself: the area bounded by the River, the uptown side of Esplanade Avenue, the river side of Rampart Street, and the lower side of Iberville Street. Its purpose clause protected not individual monuments alone but the character of the whole. The Louisiana Supreme Court gave that idea its enduring name in 1941, in *City of New Orleans v. Pergament*: the purpose is "not only to preserve the old buildings themselves, but to preserve the antiquity of the whole French and Spanish quarter, the *tout ensemble*," against iconoclasm and vandalism. The Commission — nine volunteer members appointed by the mayor with council consent — still reviews exterior changes across the district under that doctrine. [3, 4]

The district's second survival fight was against a highway. An elevated riverfront expressway along the Quarter's edge was first suggested by Robert Moses in a 1946 arterial plan for New Orleans; the proposal — an elevated six-lane road that would have separated the French Quarter from the Mississippi River — survived on drawing boards with support from the Louisiana Highway Department and the City Council, and entered the Interstate System in 1964 as I-310. Opponents, among them the young lawyers William E. Borah and Richard O. Baumbach Jr., who later titled their history of the fight *The Second Battle of New Orleans*, carried the controversy to Washington. The National Historic Preservation Act was signed on October 15, 1966, and the Federal Highway Administration determined the expressway had to go through the new Section 106 review before the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation. The Council examined the site in person, held a public meeting, and advised against the project. On July 9, 1969, Secretary of Transportation John A. Volpe canceled the Vieux Carré Expressway, stating that the public benefits "would not be enough to warrant damaging the treasured French Quarter," and rejecting a depressed alternative as well for its cost, construction hazards and risk to the levee. The riverfront the visitor walks today — levee, Moon Walk and French Market ground open to the square — exists because that road was not built. [5, 6, 10]

Federal landmark status arrived in the middle of that fight. The Vieux Carre Historic District was designated a National Historic Landmark on December 21, 1965, and entered in the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966, the day the Preservation Act itself was signed. The district today remains a lived-in neighborhood of roughly 0.66 square miles — residences, hotels, restaurants, music rooms and workshops inside the protected fabric — and it came through Hurricane Katrina in 2005 with relatively light flood damage compared with lower-lying parts of the city, a fact of elevation and levee geography rather than of immunity. The Quarter is not a museum set; it is a working neighborhood under a preservation constitution, and the visit is a walk through both. [1, 2, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The original town of New Orleans as laid out in 1721, on the Mississippi River crescent in Orleans Parish — 66 squares in the original plan between the river and Rampart Street, from the commons at present Canal/Iberville to Esplanade Avenue. The National Register record bounds the district by the Mississippi River, Rampart and Canal Streets, and Esplanade Avenue; the 1936 constitutional definition substitutes the lower side of Iberville Street for Canal. The difference between the instruments is printed in the gaps below, not harmonized. [1, 4, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The public streets, sidewalks and squares of the district itself, walked from Jackson Square and the riverfront. Every building interior is private, commercial or separately ticketed ground; nothing in this dossier opens any door. Keep to public ways, respect that most buildings are homes and workplaces, and treat gated courtyards as private unless a business or museum gate expressly invites entry. [1, 3]

## 5. Public progression

Walk the district as a grid, not a checklist. Start at the riverfront: stand on the levee above Jackson Square, where the 1803 transfers happened in the Cabildo at the square's edge, and read the town outward — square, then the commercial riverfront streets (Decatur, Chartres, Royal, Bourbon, Dauphine, Burgundy, Rampart), then the residential upper Quarter toward Esplanade. Look for the layers in order: the unbroken 1721 street plan under everything; Spanish-era stucco and tile over brick; the iron galleries of the nineteenth century; rear dependencies behind carriageway gates, where the labor that ran these houses lived. **Strongest story moment · The road that is not there.** From the Moon Walk, the open riverfront itself is the artifact: an elevated six-lane expressway was approved for this exact edge and was stopped, in 1969, by the preservation law the fight helped create. End at Rampart Street, the old town's rear wall line, and look back across the whole *tout ensemble* the 1941 court named. [3–7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Landmark year.** Most accounts date the National Historic Landmark designation to December 21, 1965. The Cultural Landscape Foundation's district profile rounds the designation to 1966, and the National Register entry date is October 15, 1966. This dossier prints the designation as December 21, 1965 and the Register listing as October 15, 1966, and flags the 1966 shorthand as a rounding of the listing year. [1, 2, 8]
- **Commission dates.** Three dates are all correct and are often conflated: the first, advisory Vieux Carré Commission of 1925, which faltered; the constitutional amendment (Act 139 of 1936, adopted by voters November 3, 1936); and the city ordinance of March 3, 1937 creating the present Commission under that authority. The city's own history page leads with 1925 and 1936 and does not print the 1937 ordinance date; the 1937 date here is from the Louisiana Supreme Court's recital of the history. [3, 4]
- **Boundaries differ by instrument.** The 1936 constitutional definition runs to the lower side of Iberville Street; the National Register record bounds the district by Canal Street; the city planning definition adds Basin Street and St. Louis Street segments on the west. No single boundary line is asserted in this dossier; the walked visit stays inside all three definitions. [1, 4]
- **Block counts differ by source.** The 1721 plan is given as 66 squares (Britannica; EBSCO's research starter agrees on a sixty-six-square-block area); The Cultural Landscape Foundation describes 80 rectilinear blocks laid out in 1721 and an area of 0.66 square miles; the common modern definition counts 78 square blocks, and the landmark district is elsewhere stated as 85 blocks. The counts measure different things (plan squares vs. later subdivided blocks vs. landmark acreage) and are printed side by side rather than reconciled. [7, 8, 9]
- **A figure not relied on.** The Preservation Resource Center's expressway history prints the project's 1969 price tag as "$104 billion," which is plainly a typographical error in that article (the figure is out of scale for any 1969 public work) and is not used anywhere in this dossier. No corrected figure was verified from a primary source in this pass. [10]
- No district-wide visitor center, official walking-tour gate or district admission exists to verify; commercial tours operate in the district but none is endorsed or scheduled here.

The district's systematic photographic record is the Historic American Buildings Survey's Vieux Carre Squares documentation, cataloged by the National Park Service library as survey HABS LA-1100-H (National Register reference 66000377), with records held through the Library of Congress (record la0148). **Inspected in this pass:** the NPS library catalog record for the survey, which enumerates measured building records across the district — among them 509–511 Royal Street, Antoine's Restaurant at 713–717 Saint Louis Street, the Merieult House at 527–533 Royal Street and the Poeyfarre House at 532 Bourbon Street. Individual frames were not opened one by one in this pass, and no single frame is adopted or credited here.
