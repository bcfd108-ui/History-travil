# Elkader — The second arch had to wait

Iowa OP06 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

**The old iron span needed a decision.** The HAER history reproduces part of M. Tschirgi Jr.’s 1888 report on the earlier Turkey River bridge. He found the superstructure “both faulty in design and in very poor condition”. Those are the engineer’s words as transmitted by HAER, which cites the county newspaper. The full signed report was not recovered. The county hired him to design the replacement. The April 11, 1891 Engineering News report identifies the construction contract with Dubuque masons and contractors Byrne & Blake at $13,000. It describes the foundations and the local limestone used for the two arches. [4, 5]

**One season was enough for one arch.** The federal engineering history records local labor on the first arch before winter and a reduced crew the next year. The work carried on to finish the second arch. It cites *The Register* and gives the final historic cost as $16,282.49 after additional work. The contract and final amount measure different things. The opening date and materials can be documented. The names and wages of individual stone cutters, haulers and masons on the workforce remain open. [4–6]

**The roadway and sidewalk above the stone have their own date.** Elkader’s account reports traffic reopening December 1, 2023 after the rehabilitation that began in 2022. It names modern contractors C. J. Moyna and Sons and Taylor Construction and distinguishes stone repair from replacement of the deck, drainage and cantilevered sidewalk. Some of what a visitor sees is renewed work on a standing arch structure. The study of the original stone and the contemporary work is stronger when each is dated. [2, 7, 8]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic place.** Bridge Street over the Turkey River, on the downtown crossing site previously occupied by an iron bridge. The masonry structure is recorded in federal survey and register files. Modern concrete, sidewalk and drainage are documented interventions rather than fabric of 1889. [2, 4–8]

**Lawful public anchor.** Upper Keystone Park at Main and Bridge Streets, Elkader, identified in city event material as 103 S Main Street. Drive up to lawful downtown parking and approach on open public sidewalk. City notice says the gazebo area remains usable and signs and fencing govern on arrival. Remain at street or upper park level. The exact arch sightline from this boundary has not been field checked. [1–3]

## 5. A short visit

Begin at the open upper public approach. Read Tschirgi’s assessment of the former bridge, then the 1891 construction account. Compare the historical photograph with what is safely visible today. Land on **The second arch had to wait**, remembering the two building seasons and the local hands behind the stone. If current fences block the view, use the records remotely. [1–8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

The 1891 engineering report gives two clear spans of 84 feet each. The register nomination prints an entire length of 346 feet, HAER gives 246, the Iowa DOT summary calls it 189 and the rehabilitation engineers describe the double-arch structure as 204 feet. Endpoints, measurement context and a possible transcription issue are unresolved. Keep these values source-specific and take none as a field measurement. The city’s dated reopening concerns the bridge, while its park notice separately closes access around the wall and to the lower patio. Later release of that park restriction was not located. Original crew payroll and the original signed condemnation report were unverified. [1–8]
