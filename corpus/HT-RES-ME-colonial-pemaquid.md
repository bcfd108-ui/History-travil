# HT-RES-ME-colonial-pemaquid — Four Forts on One Point: The Town Beneath the Grass at Pemaquid

Division ME seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On a grassed peninsula at the mouth of the Pemaquid River in New Harbor, Maine, the state preserves an early English town and the ground of four successive forts. The stone tower a visitor climbs today is a reconstruction — the western bastion of Fort William Henry, rebuilt in 1908 from a 1699 drawing, on the original foundations. The genuine survival is quieter: fourteen excavated village foundations, a burial ground, buried paved streets, and more than 40,000 artifacts from ground burned and abandoned three times in seventy years.

## 1. Trip gate

Colonial Pemaquid State Historic Site is owned and managed by the Maine Bureau of Parks and Lands, part of the Maine Department of Agriculture, Conservation and Forestry, with assistance from the Friends of Colonial Pemaquid. The site's mailing address is P.O. Box 117, New Harbor, ME 04554, and its phone number is (207) 677-2423. It stands off Route 130 in New Harbor, in the town of Bristol, thirteen miles south of Damariscotta.

The Bureau's park page gives the operating pattern plainly: the museum, the reproduction of a 17th-century fisherman's dwelling, the Fort House, and the forts are open 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. daily from Memorial Day to Labor Day, and a fee is charged. Visitors may use the park grounds in the off season between 9:00 a.m. and sunset, but the buildings are closed then. The museum page adds that the museum is staffed from Memorial Day weekend through September 1, and that admission is part of the Colonial Pemaquid day-use fee, which includes the Fort, the Fort House, and the Museum.

The Bureau's day-use fee schedule lists Colonial Pemaquid / Ft. Wm. Henry at $3.00 for an adult Maine resident, $4.00 for an adult non-resident, and $1.00 for a senior non-resident, per person per day; children 5 to 11 are $1.00, children under 5 are free, and Maine residents 65 and older are free. Bureau staff host interpretive tours in season. The grounds carry a picnic area and a trailered boat launch, and visitors fish from the dock.

The visitor boundary is the boundary of an archaeological site. The foundations, streets, and deposits under the grass are the resource itself: the National Historic Landmark nomination records that roughly 90 percent of the fort area and half of the known town deposits remain in situ, undug, to be viewed from the paths and the marked, stabilized exposures — not walked through or dug in.

## 2. The story

Pemaquid is part of Sagadahoc, the Eastern Abenaki homeland, where the Bureau's history page counts at least seven thousand years of Native American life before Europeans came. No European is known to have stayed permanently until English colonists built their first houses here around 1628: a Massachusetts Bay Company patent of February 28, 1631, granting Pemaquid to Robert Aldworth and Gyles Elbridge, states that settlers had been living at the place for three years, and the Landmark nomination takes that as the first unequivocally documented record of permanent English settlement here.

The settlement was a trading post before it was a town. Merchants like Abraham Shurt imported goods from England and carried on a commerce the nomination describes as extending from Virginia to Newfoundland — and, periodically, firearms and other contraband sold to Native customers and French Acadian rivals in defiance of Boston's prohibitions. Around 1630 Shurt built a fortified trading post — the excavation record's Structure 4, a truckhouse with a stack of cannon balls found beside it. In 1632 the English renegade Dixy Bull pillaged the settlement. In August 1635 the 250-ton galleon Angel Gabriel was wrecked by a hurricane at anchor in Pemaquid Harbor. The trade went on anyway: by 1664 the community held fewer than thirty houses; by 1673, when Massachusetts reasserted authority, its magistrates found 150 to 200 colonists in over forty frame houses, with a tavern (licensed to John Earthy in 1674), a forge, and a wharf.

War ended the first town. English slave raids along the Kennebec brought relations to the edge; a 1674 treaty at Pemaquid, at which John Earthy promised to end the raids, averted war once. When King Philip's War spread north in 1675 and Boston ordered Indian firearms confiscated, the settlers, isolated and surrounded, abandoned Pemaquid in August 1676, and the abandoned buildings were burned. New York, which then claimed Sagadahoc, reoccupied the point in 1677 and built a wooden redoubt named Fort Charles, with a customs house. In 1689, shortly after King William's War broke out, a large Native force supported by the French attacked, took, and destroyed Pemaquid and its fort.

Massachusetts answered in stone. In 1692 Governor Sir William Phips built Fort William Henry on the site — 2,000 cartloads of stone, walls 10 to 22 feet high, a bastion rising 29 feet, nearly twenty cannon, and a garrison of sixty, in the Bureau's fort page's accounting. The Landmark nomination records that the fort consumed two-thirds of the provincial budget. It was the principal English bastion on the northern New England frontier, and it did not hold. A truce was signed at Pemaquid in 1693, but in 1694 the post commander seized the Native leader Bomaseen under a flag of truce, and on February 16, 1696, soldiers at the fort killed the leaders Edgeremmet and Honquid and another man, and captured a fourth, at a prisoner-exchange discussion under a flag of truce. That August — the nomination's chronology dates the fall to August 13, 1696 — a force of 500 to 600 Eastern Abenaki warriors accompanied by the Baron de St. Castin and supported by three French warships took the fort. The Bureau's fort page adds the structural verdict: the mortar was poor, the interior buildings could not stand bombardment, the water supply lay outside the walls, and Captain Pasco Chubb surrendered. The victors razed the fort and burned the settlement.

Pemaquid lay nearly empty for thirty-five years. In 1729 David Dunbar brought several hundred Scotch-Irish settlers to produce naval stores and erected Fort Frederick on Fort William Henry's foundations, hoping for a Crown charter for his colony of Georgia. Massachusetts forced the claim down by 1732; a garrison stayed until the fort was abandoned in 1759. On May 30, 1775, Bristol town leaders directed the militia to demolish the old fort so British troops could not use it. Through the 1800s farmers farmed over the ruins, toppling stone walls and filling cellar holes to clear their fields — and in doing so, the Landmark nomination notes, often sealed and protected the deposits below.

Antiquarian interest returned with John Johnston's research in 1873. John Henry Cartland promoted the site and excavated fortress foundations; the state acquired the fort site in 1903. In 1908, state workers used Colonel Wolfgang William Romer's 1699 drawing of the ruins to erect a reconstruction of the fort's large western stone bastion atop its foundations — the tower that stands today. In 1923 Warren K. Moorehead dug here looking, unsuccessfully, for Viking remains, uncovering portions of paved streets and at least five cellar holes. Systematic archaeology began in 1965 under Helen B. Camp, whose excavations ran through 1974 and recovered the foundations of fourteen structures and more than 40,000 artifacts. Fort William Henry was listed on the National Register on December 1, 1969, and the wider site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1993 as the Pemaquid Archeological Site.

## 3. Then / now

Then, Pemaquid was a working town packed onto a small peninsula: forty or more frame houses by 1673, a customs house, a tavern and trading post, a forge, a jail, paved streets, a wharf, and a burial ground — all of it on the contested borderland between New England and French Acadia, and all of it burned in 1676, again in 1689, and again in 1696. Four forts stood here in turn across a century and a half: Shurt's fortified trading post, Fort Charles, Fort William Henry, and Fort Frederick.

Now, the peninsula is a grassed state historic site of lawns, low stone walls, and labeled foundation lines. The one tall object is the reconstructed bastion of 1908 — a reconstruction, built from a 1699 drawing, standing where the 1692 bastion stood. The Fort House, a late-18th-century building, works as the site's archaeology laboratory and research library, and the museum holds the objects the ground gave up. What has changed is everything above the sod; what survives is below it — about half the town deposits and ninety percent of the fort ground still in place, unexcavated, under the grass a visitor walks on.

## 4. What's still there

The reconstructed bastion is the site's landmark, and this dossier names it as the records do: a reconstruction. The National Register nomination describes a circular stone replica about 35 feet in diameter and about 25 feet high; the Landmark nomination, citing Beard and Bradley, describes a 25-foot-high circular flanker, 35 feet across, surmounted by a crenellated parapet, built from Romer's 1699 drawing of the ruins. It is not 1692 fabric. Inside are interpretive panels and artifact exhibits; the roof gives the view over the harbor that explains the site. Beside it, low stone walls define the large rectangular area of the 1692 fort.

The village archaeology is the genuine survival. Excavations since 1965 have exposed and stabilized the stone foundations of fourteen structures, identified in the excavation record as far as the evidence allows: the fortified trading post of 1630 (Structure 4), a customs house attributed to the New York building of 1677 (Structure 1), John Earthy's tavern and trading post (Structure 2), a forge (Structure 3), a probable jail (Structure 6), a town administration building (Structure 7), and a run of seventeenth- and eighteenth-century dwellings. The massive foundations of both later forts have been excavated at their documented locations, and buried stone-paved streets survive below the sod. The artifact assemblage — more than 40,000 objects, from Bellarmine stoneware and a five-legged iron kettle cast at the Saugus Iron Works to a Massachusetts pine tree sixpence of 1652 — is curated in the Fort House Archaeological Laboratory, with a display collection in the site museum: the Bureau's park page singles out exotic china patterns, a 400-year-old trunk, a German Bellarmine jug, and the tools of the village's trades.

The burial ground stands on the site with gravestones the Bureau dates well into the early 1700s, and the Bureau judges it likely the burying place of settlers back to the first English arrival in the 1620s. The Fort House is original fabric of a later chapter: a restored Federal-style home the Bureau dates to 1790, built, by the park page's account, by Alexander Nickels Jr., son of Fort Frederick's last commander; its first floor holds a period room, the research library, an exhibit room, and the archaeology lab. A reproduction 17th-century fisherman's dwelling also stands on the grounds, labeled here as one, like the tower.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Begin at the museum, because the objects make the grass legible: the diorama gives the town its shape — houses, streets, wharf, forts — and the artifact cases its contents: pottery, coins, musket balls, hardware, the Bellarmine jug.

From the museum, walk the village foundations: the customs house ground, Earthy's tavern, the forge with its slag, the dwellings with their burned strata — the charcoal layers are the record of a town burned three times. Stop at the burial ground and read the stones as the Bureau presents them — early-1700s markers over what is likely a burying place as old as the settlement.

Then the Fort House: the period room for the site's last domestic chapter, in the same building that stores the archaeology lab and the artifact collection. Finish at the fort. Walk the low stone walls that define the 1692 rectangle at ground level first. Then enter the reconstructed bastion — named a reconstruction on the site's own interpretation — climb through its exhibit floors, and take the roof view last: the harbor mouth, Johns Bay, and the river, the whole reason four forts stood on this point. The dock and boat launch close the visit at the water the town lived by.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The reconstruction's year is not stated consistently by the operator. The Bureau's history page states that the stone structure was built in 1907, as part of the 300th Anniversary of Colonial Pemaquid; the Bureau's own fort page states that the bastion was built in 1908. The Register nomination prints "replica built 1908," its asset record carries 1908 as the significant year, and the Landmark nomination likewise dates the work to 1908. This dossier follows the Register records and the fort page in dating the reconstruction to 1908, and prints the history page's 1907 here, unresolved.

The first fort carries two names. The 1969 Register nomination calls it Fort Pemaquid, a stockade that fell to Dixy Bull in 1632; the Landmark nomination's excavation record identifies the same ground as Shurte's Fort, built in 1630, pillaged in 1632, and destroyed in 1676. This dossier uses Shurte's Fort, following the excavation-based record.

The settlement's start is also phrased two ways: c. 1628 in the Landmark nomination, on the 1631 patent's evidence, against the Bureau's "mid-1620s."

The cost of Fort William Henry is phrased three ways in the primary record. The Landmark nomination's narrative states that the fort's erection consumed two-thirds of the province's budget of £30,000. Its chronology states that Phips expended two-thirds of the total provincial government budget, £20,000 — as does the Bureau's fort page. Whether £20,000 names the budget or the share spent on the fort depends on which page is read.

The burial ground's stones are dated differently. The Bureau's history page states that gravestones there date back well into the early 1700s. The Landmark nomination describes the Pemaquid Cemetery as holding nearly seventy graves marked with stones dating from the late 18th to early 20th centuries, plus an undetermined number of unmarked graves. Both stand.

One visitor fact conflicts inside the Bureau's own publications: its day-use fee schedule carries the note "Children under 12 are free at Colonial Pemaquid State Historic Site," while the Department's own event releases charge $1.00 for ages 5–11. Unresolved.

Finally, the 1969 nomination disagrees with itself about the fort's position: its coordinate table carries 43° 52′ 34″ north latitude, 69° 31′ 25″ west longitude, while the map label attached to the same form reads 43° 42′ 34″ north. The coordinates in §10 are converted from the table, not the map label.

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** Eastcoasteditor26's photograph of the reconstructed Fort William Henry bastion at sunset, taken from the fort ground looking west across the grass. The round stone tower stands right of center in near-silhouette: a crenellated parapet along its roof, narrow window openings, a dark doorway at its base, and flagpoles rising from the roof with flags catching the light. A low stone wall runs across the middle ground — the wall that defines the 1692 fort's rectangle on the site today. At the left, past dark trees, the harbor water lies flat and bright under the last of the sunset; at the right, evergreens close the frame. The sky takes the upper two-thirds of the portrait frame, grading from orange at the horizon to deep blue overhead. The tower in this frame is the 1908 reconstruction on the 1692 foundations; nothing in it is seventeenth-century fabric.
