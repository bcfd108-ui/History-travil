# HT-RES-WI-villa-louis — A fur-trade fortune in cream brick, standing on a mound above the Mississippi
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On St. Feriole Island at Prairie du Chien, a cream-brick Italianate mansion stands on an ancient Indigenous mound where two forts stood before it — built first as a fur agent's brick house in 1843, rebuilt as a villa in 1870, redecorated in British Arts and Crafts splendor in 1885, and named, after its builder's sudden death, for the son who made it: Villa Louis. It is a National Historic Landmark and a Wisconsin Historical Society site, restored to its 1880s–1890s appearance.

## 1. Trip gate
Villa Louis is a museum site operated by the Wisconsin Historical Society. The Society's own site history page (checked November 26, 2026) records that the property opened to the public as a Society site in April 1952 and that the site today comprises, in the Society's words, a "25-acre site" with "over a dozen historic structures." The Society's published address for the site is 521 N Villa Louis Road, Prairie du Chien, WI 53821, and its published phone number for the site is 608-326-2721 (Wisconsin Historical Society site and group-tour pages). The mansion and grounds are the Society's to open and close: this entry asserts no hours, no admission price, and no tour schedule, because none was verified from the operator's own visit pages in this pass — a visitor takes those from the Wisconsin Historical Society before traveling, and §6 records the omission plainly rather than filling it from secondary listings. What the operator's record does establish, and what this entry relies on, is the nature of the visit: a restored house museum and grounds, presented by the Society as the Dousman family's estate, with the mansion "returned to its 1880s–1890s splendor" by the documentary restoration the Society completed in the 1990s (see §2).

The ground itself sets one boundary that no ticket changes. The mansion stands on a large Indigenous mound — the nomination describes it as approximately 20 feet high with a 200-foot base, "believed to be a Hopewell site from the first millennium, A.D." The mound is the site's first artifact. This entry directs no one off the paths and public ground the operator maintains.

## 2. The story
The island's recorded layers begin long before the house. The Society's history states that Indigenous peoples lived on the grounds over 2,000 years ago and that Native Nations of the upper Mississippi gathered on the island to trade. The Society's page also carries a layer older accounts left out: the trade attracted free Black settlers to Prairie du Chien, and U.S. officers and agents brought enslaved men and women to the prairie, some of whom lived at Fort Crawford and other structures that once stood on land now within the site; research and restoration continue to expand that part of the story.

The ground the house stands on was a fort's ground twice. The nomination records that the mound on the north end of St. Feriole Island was "also the site of early Fort Shelby, 1814 (later McKay), and the First Fort Crawford, 1816-29" — Fort Shelby being the American post besieged and taken by British forces in the War of 1812 battle fought at Prairie du Chien. Overlooking the east channel of the Mississippi about 200 yards west (nomination), the mound was already one of the most layered pieces of ground in Wisconsin when a fur trader came to build on it.

Hercules Louis Dousman arrived in Prairie du Chien in the mid-1820s (Wisconsin Historical Society property record) and became the American Fur Company's major agent on the upper Mississippi — in the nomination's words, agent for "the Astor-owned American Fur Company which monopolized trade in the Northwest Territory after 1817." His 1868 obituary, quoted by the Society, judged that a biography of the man "would not fall far short of a history of Wisconsin and Minnesota." As the fur trade declined he expanded into steamboats, railroads, timber, and land, and acquired one of the region's first fortunes.

In 1843 Dousman built his house on the mound: "a large, and by frontier standards, luxurious, Georgian red brick house" (nomination) — the House on the Mound, two and a half stories, gable-roofed, with a glassed-in wooden porch extending around at least several sides of the first story, on the site of the old fort's southeastern blockhouse, its remains cleared away. The Society's history page dates the first house's building to 1843–1844. Dousman lived in it until his death in 1868, when the estate passed to his widow Jane and his son, H. Louis Dousman.

In 1870 Jane and Louis contracted with Milwaukee architect E. Townsend Mix to build a new residence in the fashionable Italian Villa style (Society history page; the nomination gives the specific date 1870 and the builder/architect as Edward Townsend Mix). The House on the Mound came down — some parts recycled into the new building — and the present mansion rose in almost the identical space: a two-story cream-brick Italianate house with widely overhanging bracketed eaves and stone surrounds at its arched windows, incorporating, in the Society property record's words, "a modern ventilation system, central heating, call bells, and indoor plumbing" — luxuries that announced exactly what the family had become.

Jane Dousman lived in the new house while Louis tended his family and business in St. Louis. When Jane died — the Society's history page gives 1882; its property record says only "the late 1880s," and the conflict prints in §6 — Louis moved back and remade the estate as the Artesian Stock Farm, breeding Standardbred trotting horses. The Society's property record inventories the build-out: a stable and carriage house, a half-mile oval racetrack, trainer's quarters, and the estate office expanded from one room to a five-room, two-story structure — for almost one hundred head of horses. In 1885 Louis and his wife Nina turned to the house itself: the Chicago designer Joseph Twyman redid the interiors in British Arts and Crafts color, texture, and pattern (Society property record; the history page describes Twyman's affiliation differently — see §6).

The work was barely finished when, in January 1886, Louis Dousman died at 37 — the Society's history page gives the believed cause as a ruptured appendix, and records that his five children ranged from two to ten years old and his widow Nina was 33. Within a few months the horses were sold, and the estate took the name it still carries: **Villa Louis**, given by Nina, in the Society property record's words, as "an enduring memorial to her husband." Nina remarried in 1888, and the family returned to Villa Louis in the late summer of 1893 and remained until 1913 (Society history page).

The museum chapter is the family's own doing. In the 1930s Louis's daughters — Virginia Dousman Bigelow and Violet Dousman Young, granddaughters of Hercules — undertook a restoration, and the property was handed to the City of Prairie du Chien as a house museum in 1935 (Society history page). The heirs first offered the house to the Wisconsin Historical Society, which declined during the Depression; a decade later the offer was renewed and accepted: the Society took title on January 1, 1950, court proceedings ran until April 1952, and Villa Louis opened to the public as a Society site that month (Society history page; the nomination compresses this to the Society assuming ownership in 1951 — see §6). The heirs also donated the house's original furnishings and thousands of family letters, records, and photographs, and on that documentation the Society based the restoration begun in 1994 — over $2 million of private and public funding — which returned the house to its 1880s–1890s appearance. The Society's verdict: "Today the Villa Louis is the finest example of a British Arts and Crafts interior in a rural setting in the United States."

The designations: Villa Louis was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1960 — the National Archives' landmark file carries the Secretary's notification letters dated October 9, 1960 — and it was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (National Register reference 66000123), significant for its association with Hercules Louis Dousman and for the architecture of E. Townsend Mix (National Park Service).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a prominent Indigenous mound on St. Feriole Island — a Hopewell site of the first millennium A.D. by the nomination's account — carrying in turn Fort Shelby (1814) and the first Fort Crawford (1816–29). **Now:** the same mound carries an 1870 cream-brick mansion, and the mound itself is the oldest structure in the site's story.

**Then:** the House on the Mound (1843), a Georgian red-brick house for the American Fur Company's chief upper-Mississippi agent, built where the fort's blockhouse had stood. **Now:** that house is gone — demolished by the agent's own son — and the Villa Louis of 1870 stands in almost the identical space, some of the first house's fabric recycled into it (Wisconsin Historical Society).

**Then:** in 1885, a working horse farm of almost one hundred Standardbreds, with its half-mile oval track and a mansion being redecorated room by room in Arts and Crafts color. **Now:** a 25-acre Society historic site of over a dozen structures, the mansion restored to that same 1880s–1890s moment — the farm's year, frozen on purpose.

**Then:** a private family's house, emptied in 1913. **Now:** a public museum since the family handed it to the City of Prairie du Chien in 1935 and then to the State — the Dousmans' own furnishings and papers furnishing both the rooms and the evidence for restoring them.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its source attached:
- The Villa Louis mansion of 1870 — two-story cream brick, Italianate, by E. Townsend Mix, with its bracketed eaves and stone window surrounds, restored to its 1880s–1890s appearance in the Society's 1990s documentary restoration (Wisconsin Historical Society)
- The mound beneath it — approximately 20 feet high with a 200-foot base, a believed Hopewell site of the first millennium A.D., previously the site of Fort Shelby and the first Fort Crawford (National Register nomination)
- The Artesian Stock Farm's surviving estate structures — over a dozen historic structures on 25 acres, among them the stable and carriage house and the expanded estate office of the 1880s build-out (Wisconsin Historical Society)
- The Dousman furnishings and archive — original furnishings, and thousands of letters, business records, and photographs donated by the heirs, the documentary basis of the restoration (Wisconsin Historical Society)
- The British Arts and Crafts interiors of 1885–86 — Joseph Twyman's reworking of the mansion's rooms, the layer the Society's restoration took as its target and the one it calls the finest rural example of its kind in the United States (Wisconsin Historical Society)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the conditions are the operator's: the site is the Wisconsin Historical Society's to open, and this progression assumes a visit on the Society's terms, taken from the Society before traveling:

1. **At the island ground first:** Read the mound before the mansion. Forts Shelby and Crawford stood here, on a Hopewell mound above the east channel — the house is the third occupation of the same high ground, not the first.
2. **At the mansion's front:** The 1870 house in the 1843 house's footprint — Mix's Italian Villa for a fur fortune's second generation, standing where its Georgian predecessor stood.
3. **Inside, in the Arts and Crafts rooms:** The 1885 layer — Twyman's color and pattern over Mix's Italianate shell, restored from the family's own papers. This interior, not the façade, is what the Society calls the site's distinction.
4. **At the estate office and outbuildings:** The working estate — the office Hercules Dousman's enterprises ran from, and the stock farm buildings of Louis's 1880s reinvention.
5. **From the grounds, toward the river:** The §7 photograph's view in reverse — the mansion above its lawn, 200 yards from the east channel whose trade built everything behind you.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Jane Dousman's death year conflicts inside the Wisconsin Historical Society's own record. The Society's site history page states plainly that Jane died in **1882**, and Louis's return and stock-farm venture follow from it. The Society's National Register property record says instead that "upon his mother's death in **the late 1880s**, Louis and his wife Nina transformed the estate into the Artesian Stock Farm" — a dating that cannot stand with the same record's account of the farm being largely complete in 1885 and Louis dead in January 1886. §2 uses 1882, from the history page, and prints the property record's form here as the error it appears to be — flagged, on the record, rather than silently corrected.

The museum transfer dates compress differently across sources. The Society's history page gives a three-step sequence — title taken January 1, 1950; court proceedings unresolved until April 1952; public opening that month. The nomination's continuation sheet compresses the same transfer to "in 1951 … the State Historical Society of Wisconsin assumed ownership." The 1935 city handover also appears elsewhere as a 1936 museum opening. §2 prints the Society's fuller sequence and flags the compressions here.

The new mansion's completion year is not fixed by the primary pages checked. The nomination gives the specific date 1870 and names Edward Townsend Mix; the Society's history page gives the 1870 contract. A completion year of 1871 circulates in secondary lists; no primary source checked states it, so 1871 prints only here, as unverified in this pass. The same discipline applies to the first house: the nomination dates it to 1843; the Society says "between 1843 and 1844." Both forms print.

Joseph Twyman's affiliation prints two ways in the Society's own pages: the property record calls him "a British designer with the Chicago firm of John J. McGrath," while the history page calls him "a Chicago-based designer from the famed William Morris Company of London." Both are the Society's words; §2 carries the property record's version.

Visitor terms are this entry's open gap: no hours, admission prices, or tour schedule were verified from the Society's own visit pages in this pass, so §1 prints none. Secondary listings publish such figures; under this entry's rule they do not print without the operator behind them. The gap is stated, not filled.

Finally, the acreage: the Society's history page describes a 25-acre site; a smaller figure (17 acres) circulates in Register-derived lists from the older paperwork. §1 and §4 use the operator's current 25 acres; the older figure prints here only as a discrepancy.
