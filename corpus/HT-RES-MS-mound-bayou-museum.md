# Mound Bayou, a town built to govern itself

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP19*

## 2. Story

In 1887 Isaiah T. Montgomery and Benjamin T. Green bought land in the Delta for a Black settlement. Both had lived under enslavement connected with Joseph Davis’s plantations. At Mound Bayou, the early residents established a town where voting, education, landholding and running a business could be more local decisions. The Smithsonian interpretation names both founders, and the original historic district nomination gives 1887 for settlement and 1898 for incorporation as a village. Montgomery and Green operated a sawmill and cotton gin. A school in Montgomery’s home, the formation of churches and a post office filled out the ordinary civic work. [4][5]

As a child at Davis Bend, Montgomery was able to read from the Davis family library. The nomination quotes his later account: “I read a great deal, but it was without method.” Those are his recalled words as reprinted in the nomination, not a reading of his manuscript. That biography helps explain how the first school or a locally managed business could change what was available to a child or a family. Education and civic control depended on more people than town founders alone. The original district file names particular institutions and buildings that preserve pieces of their work. [5]

The independence Mound Bayou pursued did not remove the statewide attack on Black voting. The nomination records Montgomery as the sole Black delegate at Mississippi’s 1890 constitutional convention and describes the restrictions on the franchise that he supported. It also records competing explanations for his actions, including an attempt to protect the new colony and a political compromise. The LOC holds a separate 1890 speech item. Without the actual manuscript, this dossier does not put a motive into his mouth. In the later town, the Knights and Daughters of Tabor built a hospital that gave Black patients access to care within a segregated system and attracted clinicians including T. R. M. Howard. The neighborhood structures and the new museum each tell a different part of this wider history. [4–6]

## 3. Then and now

The 1887 settlement grew into an incorporated town. The current historic district contains former commercial, religious, residential and healthcare buildings. The public museum is now based at the former JFK campus. Its public displays are the anchor for this dossier. The Montgomery House, old bank and former Taborian Hospital are distinct resources with their own unresolved access. [1][4][5]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

Enter the staffed museum when confirmed open and read its account of founding, business and schooling. Read any district exhibit alongside the statements about self-government and voting. **Strongest story moment — Choosing what a town could run for itself.** The gain in local agency and the shrinking of statewide voting rights can both be true in the same history. Read the founding story with room for the paradox. [1][4][5]

## Connected ground — Emmett Till — Mississippi, 1955

During the September 1955 trial at Sumner, this town sheltered Mamie Till Bradley, Black reporters, and NAACP members under guard at Dr. T. R. M. Howard's home, as recorded in Presidential Proclamation 10602.
