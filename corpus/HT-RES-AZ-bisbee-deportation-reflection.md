## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** expulsion can be described with a number. A suitcase makes it a household story: someone still had to recover the clothes and help a displaced person begin again.

The University of Arizona identifies the **July 12, 1917** expulsion of striking copper workers and other Bisbee residents as an illegal vigilante action. Its collections include court evidence and personal recollections, not a single uncontested town narrative. [5]

**Fred Watson**, a tool nipper, recalled the event in a taped interview with **Robert Houston on February 12, 1977**, published in *Journal of Arizona History* 18 (Summer 1977), pp. 171–184. The readable republication says the narrative was rearranged for coherence without changing his words. [6]

Watson remembers his future wife bringing a suitcase of his clothes after his displacement. He describes marriage, work elsewhere and eventual return, while insisting that those left behind also suffered. His short published voice is **“I'll forget it when I die!”** [6]

This is a worker's retrospective testimony, read in a republication, not audio heard or a journal facsimile inspected. His wife's actions and reluctance to discuss the past come through his account; this is not an independently recorded statement by her. [6]

**Original interpretation:** recovery is not simply the end of an emergency. Ordinary possessions, relationships and the possibility of returning home can remain unsettled long after an event ends. The suitcase is a narrative focus, not an artifact claimed to survive in this museum.

## 3. Then / now

The museum presents copper's role in building a community. [3] Pairing that institutional frame with a displaced worker's recollection asks a different question: who could continue to call the community home? This is a proposed interpretive pairing, not a claim that the museum currently juxtaposes these exact sources.

A second evidence layer is the Arizona Memory Project's catalog entry for **Watson's April 12, 1920 reimbursement affidavit**. It describes a request for **$60** toward dependent-family support while he served as a witness. The catalog is evidence of a specific documented aftermath, but the original affidavit was not inspected because direct retrieval returned 403. Do not silently substitute its description for a transcription. [7]

The material contrast is between public historical interpretation today and records of exclusion then. No surviving private building is made accessible by being mentioned in testimony. No reenactment, route tracing or role-play is necessary to understand that contrast.
