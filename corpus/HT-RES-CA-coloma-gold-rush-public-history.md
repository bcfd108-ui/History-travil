## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** The excitement of a discovery can fill the frame so completely that the people already living there disappear from it. Read a witness’s small observation beside a Tribal-published account of what followed.

**Named firsthand record, with its transmission stated:** The Church Historian’s Press scholarly edition of George Q. Cannon’s journal reproduces Henry William Bigler’s 24 January 1848 diary words in an editorial note: “This day some kind of metal that looks like gold was found in the tail race.” The edition cites *Utah Historical Quarterly* 5(3), 1932, pp. 95–99 for Bigler extracts and discusses variant diary copies. This is a firsthand diary passage read through a later scholarly transcription, not an original manuscript inspected here. Cannon’s own 17 October 1849 entry records a conversation with Bigler about the discovery; Cannon was not thereby an eyewitness to the January event. [3]

The California Geological Survey independently identifies Bigler’s diary as important evidence for dating the discovery to 24 January 1848. That corroborates the dating role of the record, not every later claim about priority or consequence. [4]

**Tribal-published regional counter-record:** The Colfax-Todds Valley Consolidated Tribe’s website identifies its community as (Nisenan) Maidu and Miwok. It publishes historical material connecting the Gold Rush to dispossession and violence, with some passages explicitly credited to outside writers. Its republished 18 November 2005 Carol Feineman news article quotes Lola Gilbert Prout on family continuity and Richard Prout on public recognition. Richard says, “Our goal is to educate people of Colfax about us and work with the city.” This is named reported testimony republished by the Tribe, not a firsthand 1848 account or proof that the family spoke for Coloma. [5]

Keep scope honest: Colfax is a regional comparative voice, **not an added stop**. Nisenan, Maidu and Miwok terms do not erase distinct communities, and the Colfax-Todds Valley community must not be equated with Shingle Springs Band of Miwok Indians, Nevada City Rancheria or every descendant community connected to Coloma. No claim of universal representation or federal recognition is made.

**Interpretive synthesis:** A precise date can be well documented while a commemorative story remains incomplete. The question is not only who saw metal first, but whose lives and voices become visible afterward.

## 3. Then / now
The diary’s uncertainty belongs to its moment. The museum’s name belongs to later public commemoration. Neither is a complete account of the transformation of the region.

California State Parks’ *Reexamining Our Past* page explicitly says interpretation is expanding beyond a formerly narrow discovery focus through Tribal consultation, archival work and engagement with connected families. It identifies African American history among newer museum interpretation. [6] This is the operator’s account of its program, not independent verification of every current label or a blanket claim that all communities approve its interpretation.

The same page explains that the earlier sawmill replica was removed in 2018 and replaced by a new exhibit. A reconstruction must not be described as an intact 1848 mill. [6] No route to either location is provided and neither is necessary to this indoor reading sequence.
