# Presidio La Bahía — two accounts of who the prisoners were
Research draft · Texas OP04 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

### The moved presidio

Several sites carried the name La Bahía. The Texas Historical Commission dates the presidio on this site to 1749. The NPS describes the nearby Mission Espíritu Santo and the presidio as a pair moved to the San Antonio River, part of the Spanish overland network. The fort and the mission were separate institutions. Their relationship is useful for reading the landscape before reaching the 1836 records [1, 3].

### The words people carried into Goliad

Joseph H. Barnard, a physician who joined the Texan side, recalled hearing of the revolt while practicing in Chicago. He described its cause as “Republican principle and popular institutions.” Those are the words in J. A. White’s compiled transcription, checked against its digital page image. Barnard’s account establishes his own reasoning for volunteering. The Handbook identifies him among those spared for medical service [4–5].

### The order recast captured soldiers as pirates

The March 23 order from Antonio López de Santa Anna, received by the commandant at Goliad on March 26 according to the transcribed communication, directed that foreign armed men “shall be treated as pirates.” In the same collection José Nicolás de Portilla wrote to José de Urrea on March 26 that after receiving definitive orders he planned to execute prisoners the next morning. These are English renderings reproduced in the collection, with Spanish originals not inspected. The order’s own initial number, 234 whom it said had been sent by Urrea from the action of March 19–20, had a narrower scope than later estimates of the prisoners at Goliad [5–6].

Barnard volunteered as a doctor and survived. On March 27, Palm Sunday, most prisoners were killed. The Handbook separates those marched from the fort, those unable to march, some prisoners who escaped and people spared for particular duties. Preserved testimony comes disproportionately from those who lived or retained official authority [4–6].

## 3 · Then / now

**Historic place.** The 1749 presidio site and the places of imprisonment and killing described in the 1836 records are distinct historical locations [1, 5–6].

**Lawful physical anchor.** Presidio La Bahía State Historic Site public museum/check-in at 217 Loop 71, Goliad. Drive up and walk a short distance from approved parking. Remain at designated public museum entry and interior interpretation. ## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Use the signed museum entrance. Orient first to the 1749 site and the geographically separate colonial mission [1, 3].
2. At the public exhibits read Barnard’s sentence about why he came, then read the Mexican government’s designation as translated in the documentary collection [4, 6].
3. **Strongest story moment — Two definitions, one prisoner.** Set down the records after reading Portilla’s March 26 response to Urrea. Give the names of the people who remained at the presidio a moment of silence. The landing stays in the museum [4–6].

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Portilla reported 445 prisoners excluding Miller’s eighty men; the Handbook gives Texan sources’ 407 excluding Miller’s men and a separate reconstructed 342 executed. The Handbook also gives approximately 40 wounded killed inside the presidio while it attributes an 80–90 estimate for that subset to Peña. Keep denominators separate and leave unnamed and uncertain cases open [5–6]. The UNT journal’s main title prints March 27, 1835; the actual title on the digitized transcription and the added metadata title print March 27, 1836 [4]. UTSA’s original-surrender item displayed a minimal landing response without legible manuscript text; read original correspondence remains an open gap [9].
