# Brices Cross Roads and the infantry that held a retreat together

*On Old Ground · Regional March · Mississippi OP15 · Labeled research draft · Reflection only*

## 2. Story

On June 10, 1864, Nathan Bedford Forrest's Confederate command defeated a Union force led by Samuel D. Sturgis in northern Mississippi. Roads, weather and supplies mattered to the campaign. So did the work of soldiers whose names and roles can disappear behind a general's name. The federal heritage-area interpretation identifies the 55th and 59th United States Colored Infantry, under Edward Bouton, as regiments committed to the fighting during the Union withdrawal. This links the result to men still fighting after the campaign's outcome had turned against their command. The 55th's unit service record lists Sturgis's expedition in June. [3–5]

One Confederate participant wrote about the same day much later. William George Pirtle begins his 1907 memoir's section with his unit arriving in camp and describes disputes around command rank in the company. “company commander’s duties are hard and irksome,” he wrote in the Filson Historical Society's transcription. It is a recollection written decades afterward. His record tells the reader what a company officer thought his work was like. It cannot supply the voice of a Black infantryman killed, wounded or captured on the other side. [6]

The fight bears the name of a crossroads. The museum in Baldwyn is separate, and its exhibit covers the June battle alongside later action around Tupelo and Old Town Creek. Treat the exhibit title and source captions as separate occasions. Bouton's brigade composition for July is documented in his Tupelo report, while the June Federal unit and heritage records name the two regiments above. A uniform or named regiment is evidence for one part of this story only when the date is attached. [1–5, 7]

## 5. Public progression

Start with the dated exhibit identifying the June action. Read the account of the 55th and 59th beside the museum's memorial wall. The strongest story moment is **The men who stayed with the retreat**, a quiet reading of what it meant to continue serving when communications and organized movement were falling apart. When the museum is closed, stay with the remote records. [1, 3–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The park title names a federal commemorative site, while Ohio State's battle account locates the wider field across Prentiss and Union counties and the federal register identifies its particular property in Lee County. Point and campaign geography have different scopes. The location given here for travelers is only the separate museum. [1–3, 8]
- Pirtle's typescript is an edited historical-society transcription of a 1907 recollection. The 1864 official unit timelines and the heritage exhibit do different work. A page bearing an ordinary soldier’s contemporaneous account of the withdrawal and a scan of Sturgis's full report were not retrieved. No claim is based on later rhetoric of decisiveness alone. [3–7]
- Staffed museum hours come from the operator. The NPS hours of the commemorative acre are separate and do not establish access to the independent indoor gallery. Holiday exceptions require operator confirmation. [1, 2]
