# Fort Hall · Trading post, replica and the public museum in Pocatello

History Travel · Idaho · Draft for Dash QC · Desk review 18 November 2026

## 2. The story

The most revealing surviving part of Nathaniel Wyeth’s business fits on a shelf. At the Oregon Historical Society, Mss 938 contains three ledgers of his Columbia River Fishing and Trading Company. In one are instructions to Robert Evans, the agent he left in charge. Another holds the 1834–35 transactions. A third includes Joseph Thing’s accounts of the sale to the Hudson’s Bay Company. This was a Boston-backed enterprise intended to trade in fish as well as furs. The ledgers give a researcher a way to follow the transfer from one company to another, even though a traveler can no longer see the trading post itself. [3]

Wyeth built with cottonwood poles in 1834. The Hudson’s Bay Company bought the post in 1837 and enclosed the older fort in adobe. Its customers changed as trapping declined and more overland travelers stopped to rest livestock, repair wagons and buy supplies. The people who left diaries remembered what a trading ledger could hardly express. Margaret Frink wrote in 1850, “The poor horses whinnied all night, from their bites.” Mosquitoes made a resupply camp beside the river a trying place for the animals on which the journey depended. The 1849 Hudspeth Cutoff drew some California traffic away. NPS dates the trading post’s closure to 1856 and the flood loss of its last remains to 1863. [1, 3]

The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes describe the reservation in relation to the Fort Bridger Treaty of 1868. Their account traces successive reductions from the original land base through a survey and later legislation and allotment. The Tribes publish the treaty itself. The post’s disappearance and the continuing existence of a tribal homeland are different histories with different records. When Pocatello planned a centennial reconstruction, the Tribes requested that it be built outside the reservation. Today a closed original and a public institution elsewhere still express that choice. [1, 4, 5]

## 3. Then / now

A cottonwood trading post acquired adobe walls and served emigrants as well as the fur trade. Floods removed the original buildings. The modern Pocatello replica interprets the later Hudson’s Bay Company plan. The Bannock County Historical Museum is a separate building within the complex. They have different winter gates. [1, 2, 6]

## 4. What’s still there

- **Not accessible** — original post site and commemorative monument on the Fort Hall Reservation. Historic register and photographs are reading references only. [1, 7, 8]
- **Not accessible in winter** — Fort Hall Replica and Pocatello Junction at the historical complex. An April 2026 city record identifies a separate closure of the replica mezzanine. [2, 6]
- **Public interior** — Bannock County Historical Museum at the Pocatello complex during posted museum hours. The imported image of the original site and the company records can be read on a phone. No claim that either is physically exhibited. [1–3, 8]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Begin at the public historical museum, 3000 Avenue of the Chiefs, Pocatello, during open hours. Identify the three places in the story on the NPS page — original, replica and museum. [1, 2]
2. Open the Oregon Historical Society finding aid. Read the three ledger descriptions in order. Compare the account of sale with Frink’s account of resting horses. In the operating season the replica is an optional interpretation of the adobe phase after the operator confirms admission. [1–3]
3. **Strongest story moment** — finish at the museum or at home with the 1958 NPS photograph of a marker in open country. Read the Tribes’ account of their land and NPS’s record of their siting request. There is no onward leg to the photographed ground. [1, 4, 8]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The city project brief calls the replica completed in 1964. Minutes record a presenter calling it built in 1963. NPS dates the construction effort to 1963–67. These could refer to different project milestones. No dedication or completion certificate in the material read resolves them. April minutes refer specifically to a closed mezzanine. A July report describes restoration and continuing roof needs. Neither overrides the operator’s current winter closure or proves a mezzanine reopening. [1, 2, 6, 9, 10]

The old replica website redirected to a suspended-account page. Current hours above come directly from the historical complex. The OHS finding aid was readable. Its digital journal item returned HTTP 403, so no account-line transcription, sale price or purported Wyeth quotation is supplied. [2, 3, 11, 12]

## 7. Photo record

**National Park Service, 1958**, historic landmark photo set, negatives 601 and 602. The dated captions identify the subject as the monument marking the trading-post site. Neither is a view of the operating fort. The marker and the wider landscape can be read online. No public Photo Point is possible on the original ground. A modern image of the Pocatello replica heads the NPS visitor page and must not be substituted for the original. [1, 8]

## Connected ground — The Snake River Emigrant Corridor

Fort Hall was the resupply stop on the Snake River main stem between the Hudspeth Cutoff junction at Soda Springs to the east and Massacre Rocks and Three Island Crossing to the west.
