# HT-RES-AK-independence-mine — Independence Mine: the Hatcher Pass gold camp that became a state historical park
Division AK seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

In the Talkeetna Mountains above Hatcher Pass stand the stabilized buildings of Independence Mine — at its 1941 peak a camp of roughly 200 men and about 22 families, and Alaska's second-largest hard-rock gold producer after the Juneau district. The War Production Board closed the gold mines in 1942; the operation shut for good in 1951, and the ground is now Independence Mine State Historical Park.

## 1. Trip gate
Independence Mine State Historical Park is reached by vehicle on Hatcher Pass Road (from either the Palmer or Willow side). The park entrance and parking are open seasonally; visitors walk the maintained trails and viewing areas among the remaining buildings. Do not enter closed or unstable structures. Confirm current road and park status with Alaska State Parks before travel; the road is not plowed in winter for vehicle access to the mine site.

## 2. The story
Gold in the Willow Creek district was first noted in placer deposits in the late 1890s. Hard-rock lode mining began when Robert Lee Hatcher staked the first quartz-vein claim in 1906. Over the following decades several small operations worked the veins that cut the Willow Creek Pluton (a Late Cretaceous granitic body) and adjacent schists in the Talkeetna Mountains. In 1938 the Alaska Free Gold Mine and the Independence Mine were consolidated under the Alaska-Pacific Consolidated Mining Company. The combined property became Alaska's second-largest hard-rock gold producer after the Juneau district.

At its 1941 peak the company employed roughly 200 men and supported a small residential cluster (sometimes called Boomtown) of about 22 families, including a territorial school. Workers drove nearly 12 miles of underground workings and processed ore in a gravity mill. The U.S. War Production Board ordered gold mines closed in 1942 as non-essential; limited work resumed after the war, but the operation shut permanently in 1951. The site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1974 and transferred to the State of Alaska, which established Independence Mine State Historical Park in 1980.

## 3. Then / now
- 1897–1906: placer activity, first lode claims.
- 1938: consolidation into Alaska-Pacific Consolidated.
- 1941: peak employment and production.
- 1942–1945: wartime closure.
- 1946–1951: brief post-war operation, final shutdown.
- 1974: National Register listing.
- 1980: state historical park established.
- Today: visitor center in the former manager's house, self-guided and guided walks among stabilized buildings, interpretive signs, and alpine trails.

## 4. What's still there
Several wood-frame buildings remain (manager's house/visitor center, bunkhouses, shops, warehouse remnants). Tram and mill structures are partially collapsed. Equipment and foundations are visible. The setting is alpine tundra above treeline, with views of the surrounding Talkeetna peaks.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Drive Hatcher Pass Road to the park entrance. Park and begin at the visitor center (former manager's house) for orientation and any scheduled tours. Walk the Hardrock Trail and other paths past the surviving structures. Interpretive panels describe hard-rock methods, camp life, and the wartime shutdown. Optional short hikes continue onto alpine ridges. Return by the same road.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Sources differ on total gold production (one 1941 figure is 34,416 ounces; cumulative estimates range higher). Exact peak population numbers also vary slightly across secondary accounts. Primary production records and company ledgers were not consulted for this entry. The precise dates of individual building construction are not uniformly documented in the sources examined.
