# Lindsborg — the miller’s promise

Kansas OP04 · Draft research for Dash · November 19, 2026 Pacific

## 2. The story

### Two mills and a printed promise

In an advertisement reproduced by the Smoky Valley Historical Association, miller Theodore Teichgraeber promised “FIRST Class Custom & Commercial Work”. The facsimile prints the Smoky Valley Roller Mills and Crescent Flour Mills together above his name. Its text is a business promise, and its two headings matter. The individual newspaper issue and precise publication date for this advertisement were not independently retrieved. A contemporary advertisement lets the miller tell a traveler what he was selling. It does not establish how much flour any one building ground. [3]

The register nomination traces the water mill’s career through changing owners and names. It credits Teichgraeber, a German immigrant with experience at other mills, with an 1889 purchase and construction of the extant brick mill in 1898 after a fire. The local historical association describes a conversion to roller operation and says he took charge in May 1890. Its account of the rebuild names John Nelson of Salina as mason and A. Almquist and the Palmquist brothers for carpentry. The nomination’s output figure of 125 barrels a day in 1902 is a stated capacity. The association’s two-mill account and the postcard promoting a different elevator and mill are separate commercial evidence. [3–5]

### The machine and its work

The building uses a vertical industrial sequence. Turbines and changing power sources supplied the machinery. Rollers reduced grain and other equipment moved and sifted stock through the building. The museum describes intermittent water flow, supplementary steam and conversion to electricity in the 1930s. It says annual Millfest operation is a dry demonstration that runs the machinery without processing wheat. The operator’s special-event age and access restrictions apply. The everyday visit is the interpreted mill, within posted visitor limits. [1,2,4,6]

The local account identifies succession within the Teichgraeber family, followed by the Runbecks. The main nomination’s compressed chain calls William Hagstrom operator after 1907 and gives the Runbecks a purchase in 1927 and operation until 1955. The museum and local association identify a shutdown from 1927 through reopening in 1934. The gap changes what a visitor might infer about an unbroken working life. [2–4]

## 3. Then / now

The extant 1898 mill is the factory at the historic riverbank site. The museum includes other buildings, some moved and interpreted separately. The lawful site anchor is the staffed museum entrance at 120 East Mill Street, via Mill Street. The First Street/Välkommen bridge is closed to motor traffic. The operator’s drive instructions use Highway 81/Harrison, McPherson Street, First Street and Mill Street. Use the ordinary public walk to open museum spaces. Keep to signed grounds and permitted mill floors. River, dam, machinery and work areas are outside the public standing scope. [1,2,4,6]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The nomination uses 1889 for purchase. The local history uses May 1890 for taking charge. The original acquisition instrument and dated operating accounts were not retrieved. [3,4]
- The operator and local history give the 1927–1934 lapse. The nomination’s ownership-to-last-operation summary does not identify the interruption. The 1902 rated capacity is not a production ledger. [2–4]
- The operator’s FAQ gives general Monday–Saturday hours. Its more specific Plan Your Visit page gives the off-season Tuesday closure. Major holidays are excepted. A dated Thanksgiving exception was not confirmed in an operator notice. [1,2]
- Newspaper advertising was read from the reproduced image. Individual mill hand payroll and verified present step-free access to upper floors remain open. [1–4]
- The operator warns that some navigation apps still direct drivers over the Välkommen bridge, closed to motor vehicles since 2016. Use its ordinary Mill Street approach. [1,2]
