# HT-RES-WY-wyoming-frontier-prison — Thirteen years from cornerstone to first prisoner, then eighty years of use behind a concrete wall in Rawlins
Division prisons-asylums-tours-001 • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Wyoming authorized a state penitentiary at Rawlins in the territorial years, laid a cornerstone in 1888, and then could not find the money to finish it. When the first forty prisoners finally came down from Laramie by train in December 1901, they entered a stone prison that had no electricity, no running water, and heating the operator's own history calls very inadequate. The building outlasted the conditions: it held prisoners until 1981, and the same sandstone complex — administration building, three cell blocks, a 1916 kitchen-auditorium and hospital-death house, walls and guard towers — is now the Wyoming Frontier Prison museum.

## 2. The story
The penitentiary was a statehood project planned before there was a state. In 1886 the Wyoming Territorial Legislature made provision for the territory's major public buildings, and Rawlins sought a $75,000 appropriation for a penitentiary. It had to wait. In 1888 a territorial building act authorized a penitentiary at Rawlins at a cost not to exceed $100,000, with $30,000 in bonds approved for initial costs, and that year the commissioners purchased the Rawlins tract and the cornerstone was laid. Wyoming became a state in 1890 with the building unbuilt. The first state appropriations came only in 1893; the first construction contract was awarded that May, and by December 7, 1894, all stonework on the combined administration–cell block building was complete, at a first-stage cost of about $70,000. Reported complete two years later, it had sustained damage and sat in custodial status; completion bids were advertised in June 1898. Not until the end of October 1901 — nearly thirteen years after the cornerstone — was the state ready to receive prisoners.

The design was Salt Lake City architect Walter E. Ware's: a massive Romanesque administration building of rock-faced local sandstone, with Cell Block A attached on its west side. The block was built on the nineteenth-century Auburn Plan — cells stacked back to back in the center of the block, in tiers — and it opened, on the operator's account, with 104 cells, no electricity, no running water, and inadequate heating. On December 12, 1901, on the nomination's account, the first group of forty prisoners was brought from the territorial prison at Laramie on the Union Pacific Railroad, and a second group of forty arrived the next day; rumors that George Curry's gang meant to hold up the train came to nothing. (A newspaper-based account dates the first group's arrival December 15; see §6.) Eleven women were held here in all, on the operator's count, in a separate ward until 1909, when the last was transferred to Colorado.

For its first decade the state did not run its own prison. From 1901 to 1911 the penitentiary was operated under contract by private parties — most notably N. K. Boswell, a Laramie lawman, and Otto Gramm, a Laramie businessman and lawyer — who hired out prison labor in the community. In 1911 the state terminated the lease and the Board of Charities and Reform took direct control. Under board management, convict labor built public roads — the beginning, the nomination notes, of the first state highway system — the Wyoming Industrial Institute at Worland was built so young inmates could be segregated from adults, and a prison newspaper, the *Wyoming Pen*, was established. The plant changed most in 1914–1915, when a concrete wall replaced the wooden stockade and running water and electricity were installed for the first time. In 1916 two Mission-style buildings followed: a kitchen-auditorium and a hospital-death house.

Work was the prison's economy and its recurring flashpoint. The first industry was a broom factory (1901–1917 on the operator's account); it burned — the fire is tied in the records to the October 1912 uprising, in which, on WyoHistory's account, inmates burned the broom factory, twenty-seven prisoners escaped, and one Rawlins resident was killed in the aftermath (see §6 on the factory fire's dates). A shirt factory followed, run under contract. Under Warden Frank Hadsell (1920–1927), the Reliance Manufacturing Company reported production per man up 30 percent since his appointment, and Hadsell's years brought a prison farm — first near Saratoga, then, after a $50,000 appropriation in 1921, a 720-acre farm near Riverton — expanded night school, and a rehabilitation program in step with the national movement. The Hawes-Cooper Act's restrictions on interstate commerce in prison goods and the 1929 crash ended the shirt factory's expanding business. In 1935 the factory became a woolen mill, which won the Navy "E" award in 1942 for its military blankets; in 1949 production changed a last time, to license plates, made until the prison closed.

Discipline in the early decades was severe, and the operator's own history records it plainly: a dungeon, several forms of solitary confinement, and a "punishment pole" to which men were handcuffed and whipped with rubber hoses. Executions were carried out across most of the prison's working life: fourteen death sentences in total — nine men hanged and five executed in the gas chamber — on the operator's count, which WyoHistory's account matches. The first two hangings used the traveling Julien gallows, the gallows used to hang Tom Horn at Cheyenne in 1903. The 1916 death house held six death-row cells and an indoor version of the Julien gallows; when Wyoming chose lethal gas to replace hanging in 1936, the gas chamber was installed in the same building.

After the Second World War the reform programs withered with their funding, facilities deteriorated, and on July 16, 1955, the prisoners rioted over living conditions. The 1960s and 1970s brought restored funding and programs — pre-release orientation, an honor camp, work release, full-time instructors — and two late cell blocks: Cell Block B, with hot running water and solitary cells (the operator dates it 1950; the nomination dates its completion to 1960 — see §6), and a 36-cell maximum-security block, Cell Block C, completed in 1966 on the operator's account. Hot water did not reach Cell Block A until 1978. By then penologists judged the early-twentieth-century buildings unacceptable for housing prisoners in the 1980s. Prisoners were relocated to a new facility south of Rawlins — WyoHistory dates the relocation to 1980 — and in 1981 the old penitentiary was abandoned after eighty years of use. About 13,500 people had been incarcerated there, on the operator's count.

The afterlife was quick by this site's standards. The district was listed on the National Register on May 26, 1983 (No. 83003360). In 1987 the empty prison was a film location for the low-budget movie *Prison*. In 1988 a joint powers board assumed ownership, named it the Wyoming Frontier Prison, and established it as a museum; the operator reports about 15,000 visitors a year.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a six-acre rectangular inner compound inside a roughly 65-acre complex, its buildings grouped tight behind first a wooden stockade and then, from 1914–1915, a reinforced-concrete wall 12 to 18 inches thick — the west wall 745 feet long — with guard towers rising ten feet above the 18-foot walls. **Now:** a 30-acre National Register district within that ground, holding the complex the nomination inventoried in 1982–1983: fourteen contributing buildings against seven noncontributing, the town grown up around it on three sides and a rocky bluff still closing the north end.

**Then:** December 1901 — a cell block with 104 cells, no electricity, no running water, heat that barely took the edge off a Wyoming winter, and prisoners arriving by train in groups of forty. **Now:** the same Cell Block A attached to Ware's administration building, its stonework repointed, read today on a guided tour.

## 4. What's still there
- The Administration Building (contributing): Ware's four-story Romanesque sandstone block, built in stages in the 1890s — semicircular entrance arch on short columns with foliated capitals, vermiculated sills, two narrow turrets with conical roofs, a massive iron gate shielding the front door, and original leaded and stained-glass sidelights and transom; pressed-metal ceilings and a softwood balustrade survive inside, on the nomination's description
- Cell Block A (contributing): the original block attached to the administration building's west side, about three stories high, its Auburn Plan interior of back-to-back cells in four tiers; the nomination's inventory describes 135 cells including 32 double cells on the west end, most cells about 5 by 7 by 7 feet (on the opening count of 104 cells, see §6)
- The 1916 Mission-style pair, both by the Cheyenne firm of Baerresen and Weiger: the kitchen-auditorium (stucco, parapeted gables, ceramic tile roof; kitchen and dining below, a ramped 200-seat auditorium above) and the hospital-death house (two buildings joined by a second-story passageway; it housed the hospital, death row, and the gas chamber)
- Cell Blocks B and C: B a four-story poured-concrete block of 144 cells; C a three-story maximum-security block with eighteen main-level cells, classrooms and chapel above
- The laundry (1901–1902, built as a boiler house, converted to a laundry in 1922), the 1923 vocational shops (200 by 130 feet), the commissary, and the guard quarters–powerhouse complex (1922, William DuBois; stone, with battlements along the roofline)
- The penitentiary walls and guard towers (contributing): the 1914–1915 reinforced-concrete walls, the double-gate sally port in the east wall, and six towers — five octagonal concrete towers and the square wooden Tower No. 1 over the sally port
- The water tower (contributing): 130 feet high, 75,000 gallons, painted silver with "Wyoming State Penitentiary" on the tank's north and south sides, probably erected in the 1920s on the nomination's dating
- The deputy warden's house (1930, Frederick H. Porter; built as the warden's residence), the prison cemetery, and the museum exhibits, including the operator's exhibit on the current Wyoming State Penitentiary

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the museum building, 500 West Walnut Street:** Start with the free museum exhibits — including the operator's exhibit on the present-day penitentiary — so the old prison reads against the institution that replaced it, then join the guided tour; the prison itself is seen only on the tour.
2. **At the administration building front:** Read Ware's facade against the nomination's description — the semicircular arch, the foliated capitals, the conical turret roofs, the barred windows — before going through the iron gate into the compound the building controls.
3. **In Cell Block A:** The Auburn Plan made physical — back-to-back cells in four tiers, each about 5 by 7 by 7 feet — and the 1901 conditions the records state: no electricity, no running water, inadequate heat.
4. **At the 1916 buildings:** The kitchen-auditorium and the hospital-death house as a pair, in Mission stucco against the older Romanesque stone.
5. **In the yard and along the walls:** The 1914–1915 concrete wall, the sally port and its wooden tower, the water tower lettered with the institution's name, and the path out to the prison cemetery.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The first transfer's date does not agree across the records. The nomination states that on December 12, 1901, the warden took the first group of forty prisoners to Rawlins by rail, with another forty the next day. Phil Roberts's 1982 account, built from the contemporary Rawlins and Laramie press (the *Carbon County Journal* and the *Boomerang*), dates the first group's arrival to December 15, 1901, the second group following the next day. Both agree on the month, the railroad, and the two groups of about forty; the day differs by three, and both print here unresolved.

Cell Block A's cell count is a two-layer record. The operator, WyoHistory, and the State Archives marker all give the opening block 104 cells, and the operator gives the 1904 west-end addition 32 more. The nomination's inventory, describing the block as it stood in 1982, counts 135 cells in four tiers including 32 double cells on the west end — a post-addition count that does not reconcile exactly with 104 plus 32 — and dates the addition's design to William DuBois in 1905, against the operator's 1904 completion date. No merged figure is printed in §4; the inventory count is labeled there as the nomination's.

Cell Block B's date is a clean disagreement: the operator's history and WyoHistory both date the second cell block to 1950, while the nomination's inventory describes Cell Block B as completed in 1960 and classes it noncontributing. Cell Block C layers similarly: the operator and WyoHistory give a 36-cell maximum-security addition completed in 1966; the nomination describes C's lower story as concrete with its upper two stories completed in 1969. Section 2 follows the operator for the blocks' opening dates and flags the nomination's later dates here rather than choosing silently.

The broom factory's fire is dated three ways. The nomination states in one passage that a broom factory was built to replace one destroyed by fire in 1912, and in a later passage that a fire destroyed the broom factory in 1923, after which a structure housing a shirt factory took its place. The operator's history runs broom production 1901–1917 and says inmates burned the factory down during a riot; WyoHistory ties the burning to the 1912 uprising. Whether there were two fires (1912 and 1923) or one fire variously dated is not resolved in the records checked, and §2 prints the 1912 tie as WyoHistory's account, labeled as such.

The beginning is also layered. The operator and WyoHistory date the cornerstone to 1888; the nomination records the 1888 land purchase and authorization but dates the first construction contract to May 1893 and the stonework's completion to December 7, 1894 — and the NPS asset record carries 1894 as the district's significant year. "Cornerstone 1888, building contract 1893" is the most the records jointly support. The joint powers board's takeover is dated 1988 by the operator's history page, while the operator's own Guards' Quarters page says the board gained ownership "in the early 1980's"; 1988 is used in §2. Finally, WyoHistory's figure of 250 people dying at the prison rests on that single account in the records checked; the execution total of fourteen is corroborated by the operator, and the larger figure is not carried into §2.

## 8. Standing on the ground
What a visitor can check against the record on site, in plain terms:

- Stand in front of the administration building and compare the facade with the nomination's description: the semicircular entrance arch, the short columns with foliated capitals, the vermiculated sills, and the conical turret roofs are all named there and all visible from the street side.
- In Cell Block A, pace a cell against the nomination's dimensions — about 5 feet wide, 7 feet long, 7 feet high — and note the back-to-back, four-tier arrangement the nomination identifies as the Auburn Plan.
- Compare the building materials in sequence: rock-faced sandstone for the 1890s buildings, painted stucco and ceramic tile for the two 1916 Mission buildings, poured concrete for Cell Block B — the nomination's three families: Romanesque, Mission, and utilitarian.
- From the yard, read the water tower's lettering — "Wyoming State Penitentiary" — against the nomination's inventory entry for the tower, and check the wall: reinforced concrete, with the wooden Tower No. 1 over the sally port standing out against the five octagonal concrete towers. The nomination counts both the water tower and the cemetery inside the district's boundaries — the district is the working prison's ground, not only its cell blocks.
