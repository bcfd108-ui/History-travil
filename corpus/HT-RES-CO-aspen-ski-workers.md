## 2. The story

**Named firsthand worker and household voice: Gretl Uhl**, interviewed by Judy/Judith Gertler in 1995, Aspen Historical Society oral history **1995.048.0001 / C137**. The published transcript was read; audio was not heard. Before ski teaching, she recalls hotel housekeeping constrained by limited English and childcare: eight months, 42 beds daily and 78 cents hourly, all remembered figures rather than payroll-verified totals. She later confirms beginning ski instruction around 1955 and remembers satisfaction in teaching. [6]

Short primary voice: **“I loved to teach, I really loved it until the day I stopped.”** [6]

**Original interpretive voice:** A visitor's holiday and a worker's ordinary day occupy the same town, but they are not the same experience. Before the remembered instructor comes a mother trying to earn, care for a child and be understood. That sequence changes what counts as a ski-town story. It is not only skill on snow or famous guests. It is the less visible work that makes leisure possible.

Keep pride and constraint together. Do not present a remembered wage as typical of all women, compare it to today's buying power without research, or treat recollected childcare arrangements as advice. This is a bounded personal account, not a complete employment history or a universal model of migration.

## 3. Then / now

Then: the testimony connects household responsibilities, paid service work and a later teaching career. Now: public archival interpretation offers access to that history without revisiting workplaces. [6]

The museum occupies an older house with its own history, not Uhl's home or job site. The operator records a period of Hotel Jerome employee housing after its 1945 sale. That is a distinct building-level labor connection, not evidence that Uhl lived there. [1]

This entry's specific contribution is the transition between care, hotel service and ski teaching as remembered by a woman worker. It is not a repeat of a general mining-to-ski-resort chronology or the Park City dossier. The named account is retained with its uncertainties rather than merged into a generic success narrative.
