## 2. The story
**One-minute opening:** Begin with a person who chose to speak, not with a building's architectural description.

**Firsthand testimony actually read:** **Dr. Ramona Klein**, identified in the congressional record as an enrolled member of the **Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa Indians**, testified on **May 12, 2022** about being taken to Fort Totten as a child. The hearing record includes her spoken-testimony transcript on printed pages **18–20** and a separately labeled prepared statement beginning on page **20**. [2]

In the prepared statement she recalls separation from her parents, fear and hunger, and describes becoming an educator who sought to affirm students rather than strip away their dignity. Her concise declaration is: **“You will not take my dignity.”** [2, p. 20] These passages were read in the published record; the hearing audio was not heard. The printed PDF image could not be rendered by the web reader, so no visual signature inspection is claimed.

**Respect the distinctions:** Klein's Tribal citizenship is not changed to Spirit Lake because she attended school there. Nor does her testimony purport to speak for every student. Her public account provides a named survivor's evidence, not permission to seek her private records or ask for more trauma testimony.

**Separate Nation-published context:** Spirit Lake Nation's own history identifies Sisseton, Wahpeton and Cut-Head Yanktonais contexts and dates the reservation treaty to **1867**. [3] This is the Nation's institutional history, distinct from Klein's personal testimony and from the state's operation of the historic site. None substitutes for the others.

**Interpretive synthesis:** The narrative should leave the survivor as an adult educator and public witness, not freeze her forever as a suffering child. Listening accurately means preserving both the account of harm and her chosen emphasis on dignity. It does not require the visitor to imagine performing an abusive practice or to reproduce graphic details.

## 3. Then / now
The operator distinguishes the former military post from its later institutional uses. Its chronology includes transfer to the Bureau of Indian Affairs in **1891**, a **1935–1939** preventorium period, and resumed day/boarding-school use from **1940** before the **1959** move. [1] These phases should not be flattened into one unchanged institution.

Klein's testimony is a **2022 recollection**, not a childhood diary recorded at the time. That difference identifies the source form; it does not erase the authority of her lived experience. The state site's present interpretive purpose is also distinct from the historical agency that operated the school.

No modern classroom or dormitory is identified as the exact room in her testimony. No present window is assigned to a remembered moment. A surviving building can hold historical significance without supporting a precise, personalized location claim.

## 4. What's still there
The operator lists **seventeen original buildings**, museum interpretation and limited accessibility. [1] Original fabric is not a guarantee of unchanged interiors, unrestricted admission or an exact match to every school period. A current room-by-room exhibit inventory and accessibility condition were not independently inspected.

The public congressional hearing remains the secure documentary anchor. [2] Its prepared statement and transcript are separate textual forms and should remain labeled. Neither is an invitation to display private student records or identify descendants.

The State Archives' boarding-school subject guide identifies collections and published oral-history resources. [4] This is a discovery aid, not evidence that every listed collection is digitized, unrestricted or immediately available. No restricted file was requested or accessed.

No burial evidence, object location, archaeological feature or sensitive record is proposed as a visitor feature. The limits of this dossier are intentional, not missing scavenger opportunities.
