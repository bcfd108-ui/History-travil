## 2. The story
A textile and a newspaper page make a useful pair. The quilt connects political organizing to skilled material work and fundraising. It interrupts the familiar picture of suffrage as only speeches by celebrated leaders. OHS identifies both maker and intended purpose, without claiming a particular amount raised. [3]

The **Sunday Oregonian, December 1, 1912, page 14**, reported the proclamation ceremony the day before. It quotes **Abigail Scott Duniway** after signing: **“It's a fact at last.”** The newspaper says she prepared the document in stages as illness limited her writing, and that Governor **Oswald West** had requested her participation. This is contemporary reported speech, not an audio recording or an independently recovered personal diary. The page image and OCR were both inspected; OCR has conspicuous numeral errors. [4]

That intimate scene matters, but a signature is not the whole campaign. **Oregon Black Pioneers** describes **Harriet “Hattie” Redmond** organizing and leading the Oregon Colored Women's Equal Suffrage Association in 1912, speaking at Black churches and working through community networks. It contrasts her later obscurity with the recognition given prominent white suffragists. Redmond's paid janitorial work and organizing belong in the same account rather than treating her as a footnote to Duniway. [5]

The Secretary of State's Redmond exhibit explains that white women's organizations excluded her and that she organized separately. Its reproduced 1912 newspaper statement is a reporter's attributed description, not a recovered speech transcript; this dossier does not pretend to have Redmond's private words. [6]

Historian **Kimberly Jensen** identifies a broad 1912 coalition, including Redmond and **Katherine Gray**, Chinese American suffragists, and **Esther Pohl Lovejoy's** effort to include wage earners. Duniway was ill for much of that campaign. Jensen also identifies the class restriction in the failed 1910 taxpayer proposal, which many wage earners opposed. [7]

**Interpretive conclusion:** celebrate the achievement without making Duniway its sole author. The winning coalition was not socially uniform, and the franchise gained in 1912 was not universal citizenship or the end of racial exclusion.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Begin with work:** at the museum's verified public entrance, enter under current admission conditions. If the quilt is on view, study it from the permitted visitor position; otherwise read the operator's object caption online. Ask what skilled labor and resources a political movement required.
2. **Read the public victory:** use the newspaper record, keeping its date visible. Distinguish what the reporter quotes from what later historians conclude. Do not seek the historical private ceremony location.
3. **Widen the frame:** read Redmond's community-organizing account and the coalition context. The point is not to replace one lone heroine with another, but to recover the network.
4. **Finish with the limit:** consider who still lacked effective access to the ballot. That is the strongest final moment: a genuine victory and an unfinished equality project can both be true.

This is a quiet reflection sequence, not a staged rally, signature reenactment or canvassing activity. If the museum is closed, all four steps can be completed remotely without entering substitute property.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Not all women enfranchised:** Jensen identifies continuing barriers tied to race and citizenship, particularly for Asian immigrants and Native people. Avoid the simplified claim that every Oregon woman could vote after 1912. Detailed citizenship pathways and exceptions are outside this dossier; 1924 must not be treated as proof all barriers ended. [7]
- **Recognition is uneven:** Redmond's organizational work is documented in Black-history and state interpretation, but no complete firsthand Redmond speech was recovered here. [5–6]
- **Tally discrepancy:** the state exhibit prints 61,265 yes and 57,104 no, but gives a 4,162 margin. Subtraction gives **4,161**. Do not silently reproduce the incorrect margin; no final canvass reconciliation performed. [8]
- **OCR is not the original:** newspaper OCR misreads years and constitutional article numerals. The date and brief quotation were checked visually against the page, not copied blindly. [4]
- **Scope of source:** the newspaper celebrates a prominent figure; its focus cannot demonstrate that absent campaigners contributed little.
- **Still unverified:** current quilt-case position, any dedicated suffrage installation beyond the confirmed exhibition listing, field approach conditions and commercial image permissions.
