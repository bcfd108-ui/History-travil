# HT-RES-MI-historic-mill-creek — The sawmill that built an island, forgotten for a century, and dug back up
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Around 1790 a Scottish trader named Robert Campbell dammed a mainland creek three miles from Mackinaw City and built a water-powered sawmill to feed lumber to Mackinac Island — the Straits of Mackinac's first industrial complex. The mill and its farm ran until 1839, passed to Mackinac Island merchant Michael Dousman in 1819, then went quiet: the ground was quarried for limestone, reverted to state forest, and forgotten until a local teacher and amateur archaeologist found the mill site again in 1972. The archaeological site was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1978 as the Campbell Farm Site — its address restricted in the Register record — and the site opened to the public in 1984 with a reconstructed dam and sawmill. In 2025 the operator renamed it Dousman's Mill; in 2026 it did not open at all, held closed by construction delays, with reopening set for May 2027.

## 2. The story
The creek ground was working ground before the mill. The operator's published chronology of the site records that in the 1770s the property was part of the reservation surrounding Fort Michilimackinac and was used for procuring firewood — the mainland shore feeding the fort across the straits (see the Fort Michilimackinac entry in this catalog).

In 1790, per the operator's chronology, Robert Campbell established a sawmill and farm at the site, to provide sawn lumber for nearby Mackinac Island. The island — limestone, garrisoned, and building — had the demand and not the timber; the mainland creek had the fall of water and the forest. Campbell's operation grew past the mill: the operator records that he also ran a farm with hay fields, cattle, and an extensive orchard, and that a gristmill was eventually added. In 1795 the property lay within the land ceded to the United States by the Chippewa in the Treaty of Greenville (operator chronology). Campbell died in 1808; his heirs presented a claim to 640 acres, citing the improved cultivated land, the sawmill, the gristmill, and the large orchard — the claim known as Private Claim 334 — and John Campbell continued operations (operator chronology).

In 1819 the mill and farm were sold to Michael Dousman, a wealthy Mackinac Island merchant, who continued operations through the 1830s (operator). Published park histories add the texture the chronology omits: the Mill Creek sawmill held a dominant share of the Straits' cut-timber supply in the fur-trade years, a millwright's house was built about 1820 near the sawmill for its operator, and in 1793 the mill had contracted with Fort Mackinac to repair the soldiers' barracks — the mainland mill literally maintaining the island fort. Those details are carried in secondary accounts and are flagged as such in §6; the operator's own chronology carries the frame: establishment 1790, sale 1819, and, in 1839, "Mill operations cease."

What followed was a century of other uses and forgetting. Dousman died in 1854; Private Claim 334 was sold in 1856 and subdivided repeatedly through the rest of the nineteenth century; limestone quarrying ran on the property from 1865 to 1920; and in the 1950s the property reverted to state ownership, incorporated into the Hardwood State Forest under the Department of Natural Resources (operator chronology). The mill buildings rotted and disappeared, and the site was, in the operator's word, forgotten.

In 1972 it was found again. The operator's chronology: "Ellis Olson discovers mill site." The operator's February 2026 announcement gives him his shape: "amateur archaeologist and teacher Ellis Olson rediscovered the mill site." Archaeological work began in 1973 (operator chronology). The dig's names multiplied with its records — the site was called the Filbert Site, the Mill Creek Site, and the Campbell Farm Site in the archaeological literature — and on January 31, 1978, the National Park Service listed the archaeological site on the National Register of Historic Places as the Campbell Farm Site, reference number 78001496, with its address restricted in the Register record, as archaeological sites are (NPS Register record and NPGallery asset record). The property had already been transferred to the Mackinac Island State Park Commission in 1975 (operator chronology), and in 1984 the Commission opened the site to the public — the operator's chronology says "as Old Mill Creek," a name form the operator's other pages do not use, and the conflict is printed in §6. The creek dam and sawmill were rebuilt for the opening; the sawmill was restored in part for the 2007 season (published park histories).

The most recent chapter is a renaming and a closure. In 2025 the site name changed to Dousman's Mill (operator chronology), honoring the owner of 1819–1839 rather than the founder of 1790 — a choice visitors have argued about in print, though the argument is not this entry's to settle. Construction on the David A. Armour Visitor's Center and restrooms began in September 2025, ran late, and the operator closed the site for the whole 2026 season. It is scheduled to reopen in May 2027 under the new name.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a working industrial farmstead — dam, water-powered sawmill, gristmill, farm, orchard, and later a millwright's house — cutting the lumber that built and repaired the settlement and fort on Mackinac Island (operator chronology; park histories). **Now:** a 625-acre historic site and nature preserve (published park descriptions) on the same creek, where the reconstructed sawmill still demonstrates the cut, on the archaeological ground the Register lists as the Campbell Farm Site.

**Then:** in 1839 the mill stopped, and the ground went to subdivision, limestone quarrying (1865–1920), and state forest — the mill forgotten under its own woods. **Now:** the site is interpreted precisely as that recovered thing: an excavated and reconstructed mill, opened 1984, renamed Dousman's Mill in 2025.

**Then:** the property was part of Fort Michilimackinac's reservation, cut for the fort's firewood in the 1770s. **Now:** it is one of the Mackinac State Historic Parks family with the fort's reconstruction — the operator itself says a visit here ties its Mackinaw City sites together.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The archaeological site — the Campbell Farm Site, listed on the National Register January 31, 1978 (ref. 78001496), its address restricted in the Register record; the NPS record gives the site's periods of significance as including 1750–1799, 1800–1824, and 1825–1849, with areas of significance in industry, military, architecture, and prehistoric and historic archaeology (NPS NPGallery asset record)
- The reconstructed water-powered sawmill and dam on Mill Creek — the operator's own February 2026 announcement calls the sawmill "the reconstructed water-powered sawmill" and the site's highlight; published park histories date the rebuilding to the 1984 opening and a partial restoration to 2007
- The Millwright's House, interpreted on site; the operator's 2026 announcement promises an upgraded Millwright's House exhibit for 2027. Published park histories date the original house to about 1820 and its rebuilding, with the British Workshop, to the 1990s
- Mill Creek itself, running through the park from the Dingman Marsh side toward Lake Huron, and the site's trails and natural-history programming (operator history page)
- The David A. Armour Visitor's Center and restrooms — present, and as of the operator's February 2026 announcement unfinished, with a new history exhibit, renovated theater, and museum store planned for the 2027 reopening (operator)
- As of November 26, 2026: a closed site. The operator does not operate it in 2026; reopening is published for May 6, 2027 (operator seasonal-hours page)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — and the first condition is the operator's: the site does not operate in 2026. The progression below is written for the reopened site, on the operator's public ground only:

1. **At the Visitor's Center:** Start with the new history exhibit the operator has promised for 2027, and with the name problem in hand — this ground has been Campbell's mill, Dousman's mill, a quarry, a state forest, the Campbell Farm Site, Old Mill Creek, Historic Mill Creek, and Dousman's Mill. The names are the site's stratigraphy.
2. **At the reconstructed sawmill:** Watch the water do the work. The mill stands where the archaeology put it, over the creek that powered the original; the demonstration cut is the same motion that supplied Mackinac Island from 1790 to 1839.
3. **At the Millwright's House and workshop ground:** The mill was a workplace with a household attached — the operator's people lived beside the machine. Read the farm, orchard, and gristmill claims of Private Claim 334 against this ground.
4. **On the trails, along the creek:** End with the forgetting. Between 1839 and 1972 this was quarry and forest; the creek outlasted every use, and the site exists as a park because one local teacher went looking for it.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The rediscoverer's name divides the record. The operator — in both its site chronology and its February 2026 announcement — names Ellis Olson, "amateur archaeologist and teacher." A separate line of secondary accounts names a Cheboygan High School teacher, Donald Heldman, as the 1972 rediscoverer. The two tellings agree on the year, the place, and the teacher shape of the man and disagree on his name; this entry follows the operator, the site's own record-keeper, and prints the Heldman form here as an unreconciled conflict rather than silently choosing. (The operator's spelling is "Ellis Olson" in both its pages checked.)

The 1984 opening name also prints two ways inside the operator's own record: its site chronology says the site "opened to the public as Old Mill Creek," while its February 2026 announcement says only that the Commission "opened it to the public in 1984," and published park histories generally give the opening name as Historic Mill Creek State Park. The name sequence this entry can stand on is the operator's: the site was renamed from Historic Mill Creek to Dousman's Mill in 2025; what it was called on opening day in 1984 is carried here as the operator's two forms plus the histories' third, unreconciled.

Distances vary by source. The operator — in both the February 2026 announcement and the seasonal-hours page — puts the site three miles east of Mackinaw City on US-23, and that figure is used in this entry. Published park descriptions put it at 3.5 miles southeast (Parks Directory of the United States) and at five miles southeast (the figure carried in the site's encyclopedia summary). The spread is printed, not averaged.

The millwright's house date (about 1820), the British Workshop, the 1793 Fort Mackinac barracks-repair contract, the sawmill's "dominant market share," and the saw marks used to reconstruct the mill machinery all come from published park histories and secondary summaries, not from the operator's chronology or the NPS record checked in this pass; they are attributed as such wherever they appear. The 625-acre park figure likewise comes from published park descriptions. The Michigan State Historic Site designation is consistently reported for the Campbell Farm Site; the specific date, March 20, 1984, is carried by published summaries of the state register and was not verified against a Michigan SHPO page in this pass, so §10 prints the designation with that basis stated.

No coordinates print in §10, and the reason is the record's own: the National Register listing for this site is an archaeological site whose address the Register restricts ("Address Restricted" — NPS). The operator publishes the site's road location — US-23, three miles east of Mackinaw City — and that is the public anchor this entry carries. Secondary catalogs publish coordinates for the restricted site; under this entry's rule, and out of respect for the Register's own restriction, they are not reproduced here.
