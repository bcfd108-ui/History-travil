## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A museum label can look authoritative and still be wrong. The people whose heritage it describes must have a role in correcting it.

**The Tribal museum's published voice:** In its own Google Arts & Culture exhibition, A:shiwi A:wan describes a museum established by Zuni members in 1992 with Pueblo leadership support. It explains collaboration with outside collections as a way to correct representations and regain control over knowledge associated with Zuni material. This is attributed museum-authored interpretation on a third-party platform, not a Google-written general history or a Tribal resolution. [2]

**Named primary collaborative account:** A case study titled *Setting the Record Straight* is credited jointly to **Cynthia Chavez Lamar and Jim Enote**, representing the School for Advanced Research and A:shiwi A:wan Museum & Heritage Center. It describes their 2008 initiative to review SAR's Zuni collection and share catalog records with Zuni. It identifies **Octavius Seowtewa**, head of the Zuni Cultural Resource Advisory Team, among the participating experts. [4]

The case study attributes a 2010 personal communication to Enote: **“we want Zunis to have the right information about these things in the future.”** The quotation is taken from the published coauthored account, not from an interview conducted or recording heard here. Its emphasis is on what young Zunis learn about their own heritage. [4]

**Publishing distinction:** The named account is museum-community coauthorship hosted by the School for Advanced Research's collaboration project. The directly Pueblo-hosted 2021 newsletter supplies a parallel institutional account of the museum's corrective work but does not name an individual author for that article. These different kinds of authority are retained, not merged into a falsely attributed government statement. [3,4]

**Interpretive synthesis:** The important shift is not just adding another label. It is recognizing whose knowledge can change the catalog and who should help decide how information circulates. The record becomes more useful when a source community is treated as an authority rather than only as a subject.

This is specifically a **Pueblo of Zuni** account. It is not interchangeable with Acoma's museum, government or visitor permissions. The collaboration webpage also contains an Acoma case study; only the separately titled Zuni case is used here. [4]

## 3. Then / now
The 2008 collaboration and Enote's attributed 2010 remarks are dated evidence of a method, not proof that every museum catalog has since been corrected. [4] The museum's current public online account continues to explain that purpose. [2]

The comparison is between outside documentation and community participation in its interpretation. It is not a physical route tracing how objects were removed or where they are now kept. No artifact's current condition, storage location or availability for inspection was independently verified.

A public-facing digital exhibition can be encountered now without treating every mapped story, old photograph or collection reference as a direction for travel. No historic/current camera alignment is claimed.

## 4. What's still there
The museum-authored online exhibition, partner institutional profile and Pueblo-hosted newsletter are available for reading. [1–3] The newsletter describes the museum as a forum for Zuni history and art; it is an institutional record from 2021, not a live exhibit inventory. [3]

The online exhibition also presents the ethical problem of older documentation made for audiences other than Zuni. [2] This does not authorize copying the associated imagery or seeking its original locations. Public interpretation and unrestricted collection access are different things.

No archived recording, winter story or ceremonial account has been played or transcribed for this dossier. No cultural narrative is used to infer a physical destination. The public account of museum practice is sufficient for the present research purpose.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Remote preparation progression only; no physical stop is activated:**

1. Begin with the museum's own description of who it serves. Keep the institution's name and Pueblo affiliation beside the material.
2. Read the named collaborative case study. Notice the distinction between an outside catalog record and the knowledge supplied by community experts.
3. Return to the museum's public statement of purpose. Acknowledge that adding information and limiting circulation can both be responsible acts of preservation.
4. End with the strongest story moment: a young person should not have to learn their own heritage from an uncorrected outside description.

It would not include the places represented in art, migration accounts or historical photographs. No cultural landscape is converted into a scavenger route.
