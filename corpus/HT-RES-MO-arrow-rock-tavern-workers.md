# Arrow Rock — The house that learned to feed a road

Missouri OP02 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

**The crossing came before the dining room.** Merchandise and people moving toward Santa Fe had to get across the Missouri. In the 1911 reprint from the *Missouri Intelligencer*, traveler Meredith Miles Marmaduke's 1824 journal opens, “Crossed the Missouri river at Hardiman’s ferry.” The printed page gives May 16, 1824, and six miles above Franklin. Editor F. A. Sampson spells the name Hardeman and presents testimony that calls this a distinct crossing. A later federal study instead summarizes the party as crossing at Arrow Rock. The journal and editor give the more specific accounts, but they establish neither a present visitable landing nor a route to one. [5–6]

**A household took on customers.** State Parks dates Joseph Huston Sr.'s four-room brick house to 1834 and describes the domestic building acquiring meals, rooms and a mercantile store as traffic passed. The historic-site nomination identifies Huston as a county judge and town commissioner as well as host. HABS records the building as a tavern and describes changes and a rear extension. Work behind the front door included lodging, food preparation, cleaning and storekeeping. The sources retrieved identify Huston and the business, without enough evidence to name early kitchen or domestic workers. [2, 7–9]

**A guest book preserves one side of the counter.** The State Historical Society catalog describes C1090 as an alphabetical index and copy of the tavern register for 1859–61, made by the Elizabeth Benton Chapter of the D.A.R. C1091 contains the town trustees’ proceedings, accounts and expenditures. Neither catalog establishes particular names in the kitchen. The business had several later names, including the Neill House and Scripture House. That succession matters when searching a ledger or newspaper. [8–10]

## 3. Then and now

The historic location is the **J. Huston Tavern, south side of Main Street, Arrow Rock**. The nomination records subsequent commercial activity on the street and the loss of the town’s old river relationship as the channel changed. The tavern remains on its own site. The historic crossing locations and earlier banks are a different problem from locating a surviving brick building. [6–9]

The public anchor is **Arrow Rock State Historic Site Visitor Center and the J. Huston Tavern's public Main Street frontage**. Use visitor parking and posted public paths to the center, then stop at the open public walk fronting the tavern. No deck, paid dining room, kitchen or closed path is needed. The signed main approach is a drive-up and short walk, subject to weather and field verification. [1–4, 8, 11]

## 5. A short visit

Read the regional context at the visitor center. Use the signed public approach to Main Street. Look at the tavern frontage and read Marmaduke’s crossing entry, remembering that it precedes the brick building by ten years. Finish with **The house that learned to feed a road**, where the known proprietor can be named and the archive’s silence about the people serving the rooms and meals is visible. If the weather closes the public walk, use the open center or the facsimile remotely. [1–9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

The federal Santa Fe study collapses the 1824 crossing to “Arrow Rock.” The journal reprint specifies Hardiman’s ferry, and Sampson’s note uses Hardeman and discusses a distinct landing. Hold exact ground. The HABS data says a large rear dining room and kitchen were added in 1923. The operator dates a large dining room and modern kitchen to 1955. This can reflect two building campaigns, but continuity of the footprint is unverified. The operator’s continuous-service claim is qualified by Missouri DNR’s documented 2024 closure and reopening plan. [2, 5–9, 12]

The tavern domain was inaccessible in the web tool, then returned raw HTTP 200 and a current-hours heading. The listing is restaurant service, separate from the public route. An original guest register or image of its D.A.R. copy was unavailable through the catalog, and early workers' own voices remain a research gap. [4, 9–10, 13–15]
