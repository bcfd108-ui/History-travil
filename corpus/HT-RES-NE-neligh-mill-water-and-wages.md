# Neligh Mill — water, grain and the people who kept the mill running

DRAFTED · Nebraska OP03 · Pending independent Dash QC

## 2. The story

### What made the first flour

John D. Neligh began the building in 1873. The National Register nomination says financial reverses forced a sale to Lambert and Gallaway, who completed the mill. In the 1882 printed county history, the mill ground “within a fraction of 7,500 bushels of wheat” during its first two and a half months. The digitized transcription dates the start to October 10, 1874. The figure gives scale in wheat received and ground during an interval. It is different in kind from later pounds of flour per day. The book identifies the mill's brickmaker as John J. Crawford and describes local clay. The nomination's middle initial differs. Both tie the building itself to work done nearby before it could mill a bushel. [2–4]

### A river was part of the machinery

The state account describes W. C. Gallaway improving the brush dam, rebuilding the flume and installing a horizontal turbine. The nomination describes the cable that carried power to the mill. Follow that sequence from water to drive works to grinding. When the river gave either too little or too much water, the state history says production could be limited. It describes the 1886 replacement of the stone burrs with seven stands of Allis rollers and gives a capacity of 140 barrels of flour a day at that stage. The nomination records later additions and gas or diesel engines so the mill could use power beyond the river. It says a severe flood destroyed the concrete dam in 1920. [3,4]

### The people outside the production numbers

The published historic photo of employees in 1911 gives the operation human scale. Read it with the early account's named trades and the state history's story of flour sales and commercial orders. One can reasonably infer that keeping processes in sequence required people on the floor, but the evidence here cannot assign an individual shift or payroll amount. The state flour display describes a peak of 98,000 pounds per day during 1900–20. Read that as the operator's stated peak. The earlier 140-barrel capacity is for another configuration. The original staffing books and wage sheets remain open. [2,4–6]

## 3. Then / now

The brick mill, grain-processing equipment, and office give a visitor several parts of an industrial workflow. The state identifies the flume and penstock as reconstructions and the dam as remnants. The nomination documents the 1959 end of flour milling, while feed milling and grain storage continued at a reduced level. Public interpretation is operated as a historic site. [1,3,4]

## 5. Public trip-shaped progression

1. Confirm staffing before going. Begin at the office with the milling chronology and exhibits. [1,4]
2. Trace the water path in the site's interpretation and compare it with the surviving power and processing equipment where accessible. [1,3,4]
3. **Strongest story moment — The people in the 1911 frame.** Look at the employee photograph and then toward the floor where water power became flour. Finish by separating named trades from wages the record has yet to establish. If conditions or staffing close the visit, use the published sequence and photographs remotely. [2–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Andreas prints John J. Crawford. The nomination prints John H. Crawford. No signed brickmaking record was retrieved. Andreas and the nomination describe different ownership and machinery stages. The state account focuses on Gallaway. [2–4]
- Wheat bushels over the startup period, 1886 barrels per day and later peak pounds per day are distinct measurements. No complete ledger or signed wage schedule was retrieved. [2,4,6]
- The nomination treats Gold Medal brand origin as reported. The state flour page gives payment and exclusivity in May 1940. The brand transaction record was not retrieved. [3,6]
- The individual NPGallery item returned an internal error. The official NPS 2010 weekly list independently prints 69000128 and distinguishes the boundary and component entries. The nomination PDF was retrieved separately. [3,8,9]

## 7. Historical photo records

NSHS publishes images of the mill about 1885 and 1909, the waterwheel about 1900, the dam around 1910, the 1914 flood, and the 1911 employees. Read the employee photo as an archival group view. Dam and water-system alterations and a lack of a confirmed public repeat position prevent a visitor Photo Point from this batch. [3,5]
