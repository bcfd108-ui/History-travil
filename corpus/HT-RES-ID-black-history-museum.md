## 2. The story

This is a story of people making a place to belong, not simply a notable old building. St. Paul's own history names **Rev. William Riley Hardy**, a carpenter, **trustee Louis Stokes** and other craftspeople in the 1921 construction. [5]

**Named primary community voice, republished by the congregation:** Mary Buckner, identified with the church's *35th Anniversary Souvenir Program*, recalled: **“Services were started by meeting from house to house. We enjoyed the services and our leaders.”** Original program pages were not retrieved; this is a republished transcription, not authenticated audio. [5]

The 1982 nomination describes the church as a gathering place for Black Boiseans before other social organizations developed. Its research sources include a February 1981 interview with Dr. Mamie Oliver and a May 25, 1921 *Idaho Daily Statesman* item. Neither underlying source was independently read here. [6]

The later National Register civil-rights context adds a consequential use: St. Paul's let Black airmen from Mountain Home sleep on its pews when racial discrimination prevented hotel accommodation. It also describes the congregation's social and cultural support, and members' civil-rights work. This is a historian's documented synthesis, not a quotation from an identified airman. [7] A room built for worship could also become practical shelter. That is the strongest story moment, but no surviving individual pew is claimed to be the one used.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** the church stood on Broadway. The nomination records **124 Broadway** and original-site status in 1982; that is a historical observation, not today's location. [6]

**Now:** the preserved building is a museum in Julia Davis Park. Idaho Heritage Trust, which records grants in 1997, 1998 and 1999, confirms the relocation from Broadway. [8] The congregation itself continues elsewhere: its own account dates its move to **1320 W. Bannock Street** to October 9, 1994. [5] Museum reuse does not mean the community ceased to exist.

The interpretation should connect the building to people who sustained it, rather than making a statewide “first” claim the available sources do not consistently define. This is not interchangeable with Hayman House or a generic Boise civil-rights biography. Each has its own household, institution and geographic story.

## 4. What's still there

- **Museum building, public visitor use conditional on opening:** the operator identifies the surviving 1921 structure as its home. [2] Staff-only areas are not visitor space.
- **Historical fabric, documented rather than newly surveyed:** the nomination describes frame construction, clapboard, bracketed eaves and stained-glass windows; it also describes an earlier added entry. Do not label every visible component untouched 1921 fabric. [6]
- **Current exhibits, not inventoried in this check:** no guarantee that a particular object, panel or pew is currently displayed. Read installed labels rather than transferring old press inventories into a promise.
- **Julia Davis Park setting:** use only signed public paths and permitted public visitor areas. The relocated building is not evidence that historical Broadway streetscape survives here.
- **Present St. Paul church, separate active worship property:** not an automatic museum annex or walk-in heritage tour. A published worship welcome does not authorize photography, exploration or interruption. [10]
