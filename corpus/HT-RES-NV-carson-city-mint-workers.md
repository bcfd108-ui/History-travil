## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** coins preserve a government's mark; employment records can preserve the people whose names the coins omit. This entry asks who was counted, who was promoted, and whose work is easy to lose when a building's history becomes only a founder's biography.

**Named woman, institutional evidence:** the Nevada State Museum identifies **Elvira Curry Cowen**, Abe Curry's daughter, as an adjuster who became forewoman in **1877**. The U.S. Mint's historical essay likewise dates the first woman supervisor at Carson City to 1877, but spells her name **Elvira Cowan**. Neither page supplies her original appointment document or her personal voice. Retain both spellings with attribution instead of silently settling the archival name. [2–3]

**Primary documentary object, with provenance limits:** a publicly reproduced payroll headed **“U. S. BRANCH MINT CARSON CITY, NEVADA”** and dated **July 1866** survives in an auction record. The linked photograph was inspected; its printed title, table and handwritten entries are visible. This is a construction-era payroll, not proof that coin production had begun. Holabird's lot description identifies **Ah Hong** among named Chinese construction laborers and describes payment through an agent. That name is catalog-transcribed here, **not independently deciphered from the faint image**. The commercial catalog is a source about the object, not a neutral archival authentication. [4–5]

The same catalog reports a racial wage differential. Do not convert that single claim into a comprehensive pay scale without higher-resolution document review and corroborating records. No invented worker quotation fills the gap.

**One-minute hook, original:** Before this place made coins, people built it. Later, women worked here and one became a supervisor. The money carries a mint mark; what evidence carries their names?

## 3. Then / now
The payroll belongs to construction, while Elvira's documented-in-institutional-history promotion belongs to a later workforce. These are separate chapters, not evidence that she appears on the 1866 sheet. The present museum is a third chapter.

The museum describes its opening on **October 31, 1941**. That is not the opening date of the mint. Its historical page distinguishes building completion in **December 1869**, a public opening in **January 1870**, and first coinage in **February 1870**. Those milestones should not be flattened into one date. [2]

**Original reflection:** a surviving building can give an impression of continuity stronger than the employment record allows. Treat a change of use as a question about which stories were preserved, not as proof that a nineteenth-century workroom remains untouched.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Cowen/Cowan:** operator and U.S. Mint spellings differ. No appointment sheet retrieved to settle them. [2–3]

- The museum broadly says anti-Chinese sentiment prevented use of inexpensive immigrant construction labor; the catalog describes Chinese names on a July 1866 mint construction payroll. This is a substantive conflict requiring document/authenticity and scope review. Do not resolve it by erasing the workers or declaring the museum wholly wrong. [2,4]
