# CSKT Bison Range: restoration means responsibility

Montana Division OP06 • checked 2026-11-19 • local research draft; no upload, contact, permit application, payment, or route activation.

## 1. Trip gate

**Visitor-center-first candidate; no winter drive clearance.** The Tribal operator lists November–April daily visitor-center and front-gate hours **8 a.m.–5 p.m.**, with the range closing at **6 p.m.** All hours are weather-dependent. Red Sleep Drive is winter-closed; Prairie Drive has a published winter window but is **not verified open today**. The gate closes earlier than the range. Thanksgiving, Christmas Day, and New Year's Day are closed; Christmas Eve gate hours are **8–2**. [1]

The operator separately requires **daylight-only access** and compliance with posted closures. Do not treat a clock schedule as permission to remain after dark or to pass a closed gate. [2]

The FAQ explicitly locates the visitor center at **58355 Bison Range Road, Charlo, MT 59824**. Operator telephone: **406-644-2211**. No call made. Federal recreation passes are not accepted. [2][3] The current regular personal-vehicle day pass is **$20**; fee classification is not approval for a commercial tour. [1]

Public invitation is limited to the operator's designated visitor areas. This draft adds no road numbers, private shortcuts, cultural places, or wildlife approach routes. **a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.**

## 2. The story

Begin with who cared for the buffalo before the federal refuge existed. The CSKT's own history describes **Little Falcon Robe**, a Ql̓ispé man, bringing orphaned calves to the reservation with Tribal leaders' approval during the 1870s. It traces subsequent stewardship through **Michel Pablo and Charles Allard**, and explains the later dispersal of the herd in the context of opening reservation land to non-Native settlement. This is the operator's published historical account, not an original nineteenth-century record inspected here. [4]

A named contemporary voice gives restoration its human meaning. In the **March 23, 2022 statement issued on behalf of CSKT**, then-Chairman **Tom McDonald** said: **“Our reunification with this specific buffalo herd means more to us than we can express.”** The same statement connects management with telling the Tribes' own history to visitors. His title is dated to that release, not asserted as his current office. [5]

The **Séliš-Ql̓ispé Culture Committee's 2023 essay**, published by PBS with permission, places buffalo within an enduring relationship of responsibility, not simply ownership of a wildlife attraction. It includes named elder recollections and translation credits. This dossier read the indexed published text; the direct PBS page returned 403, and no audio was heard. [6]

The interpretive point is not that a government changed the sign. It is that a community whose conservation work predated the refuge regained responsibility for a place taken from its homeland. The visitor's role is to listen to the Tribal account and respect the rules that make public access compatible with care.

## 3. Then / now

The CSKT history places the federal taking in **1908–1909**, despite Tribal objection, and describes decades of later efforts toward management agreements. [4] The federal release distinguishes **December 27, 2020 legislation**, the **2021 trust-land transfer**, and **January 2, 2022 full Tribal management**. These are different milestones, not competing dates for one instantaneous event. [5]

Now the **Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes** operate the range. The operator identifies the **Séliš, Qlispé, and Ksanka** peoples, also called Salish, Pend d'Oreille, and Kootenai. They should not be collapsed into a single generic “Plains tribe.” [3]

The physical landscape is therefore also a jurisdictional story. “Former National Bison Range” may be useful historically, but old federal-pass assumptions and inherited visitor rules are not current authority.

## 4. What's still there

- **Public visitor center:** the independently identified anchor, subject to weather, holiday, staffing, and actual opening conditions. Its access is separate from permission to drive a scenic road. [1][3]
- **Wildlife habitat:** not an open walking field. Operator rules prohibit leaving designated open roads by vehicle and restrict walking away from vehicles to designated visitor areas. [2]
- **Roads:** a published seasonal listing does not override maintenance, wildlife, weather, or posted closures. No drive is included in this dossier's proposed progression.
- **Wildlife:** no guaranteed sighting or photo distance can be promised. Keep at least **25 yards from bison and other nonpredatory wildlife** and **100 yards from bears, wolves, and mountain lions**; approaching animals is prohibited. These are minimum rules, not invitations to move closer. [2]
- **Cultural interpretation:** publicly shared material does not authorize ceremonies, private stories, access elsewhere on the reservation, or handling cultural objects.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

The visit begins — and in winter may end — at the Tribal visitor center at 58355 Bison Range Road, Charlo. The Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes operate the range, set its hours and rules, and tell its history there. [1][3]

That history starts before the federal refuge. In the 1870s Little Falcon Robe, a Ql̓ispé man, brought orphaned bison calves to the reservation with Tribal leaders' approval, and Michel Pablo and Charles Allard carried the herd forward from there. The herd was later dispersed as reservation land was opened to non-Native settlement. [4]

In 2022 the Tribes resumed management of the range and its herd. Then-Chairman Tom McDonald's statement that March put it plainly: “Our reunification with this specific buffalo herd means more to us than we can express.” The Séliš-Ql̓ispé Culture Committee's 2023 essay, published by PBS with permission, sets the herd inside a continuing relationship of responsibility, with named elders' recollections. [5][6]

Beyond the center, the range is working wildlife habitat, not open walking ground. Visitors stay on designated roads and in designated visitor areas, keep at least 25 yards from bison and other nonpredatory wildlife and 100 yards from bears, wolves and mountain lions, and no sighting is guaranteed. Red Sleep Drive is closed in winter, and the gate closes earlier than the range itself. [1][2]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The winter schedule contains an apparent editing error: beneath November–April it says winter hours end October 31. The labeled season and posted day-of instructions require confirmation; the contradiction is not silently fixed. [1]
- Published clock hours and daylight-only rules require a conservative daylight limit. No same-day weather, road, or gate clearance has been obtained. [1][2]
- Glacier Country tourism says the visitor center is closed in winter, contradicting the Tribal operator's winter opening. Use the operator as the planning authority, while retaining the live-check gate. [7]
- Charlo is printed by the operator; some tourism listings use Moiese for the same street address. This draft keeps the operator's address. [3][7]
- The operator's Public Law page has a resource heading saying **115-260**, while its narrative says **116-260**, and retains an outdated statement about the official law not yet being released. Do not use that heading as a verified legal identifier. [8]
- The 2022 release and PBS essay were available as indexed source text, but direct opens failed. Neither an original recording nor a full original legal case file was inspected. [5][6]
- Current exhibits, mobility access, and the location/display status of Big Medicine were not verified. No promise to see a particular mount or cultural item.
- No commercial operation, photography use, or paid outing permission has been established.
