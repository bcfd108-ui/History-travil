## 2. The story

**Named firsthand worker: Bonifacio Anaya.** WHEELS Museum publishes Monika Ghattas's excerpts from his **June 1999 oral interview**. The text connects his Barelas childhood to later railroad employment in the late 1930s and wartime. His recollection includes workmates' social lives, serious workplace losses and the value of safety improvements. He recalls: **“We all got along pretty well”**. The complete recording was not heard; the published edited text was read. [2]

A second excerpt, **Victor Castillo, October 2000**, relates prolonged night work and family time lost to it. His home was west of the river, not necessarily in Barelas; do not relocate his household to fit the neighborhood label. This is corroborating regional worker experience, not the same person's story. [2]

**Original interpretive voice:** A railroad's clock does not stop at its gate. It reaches the dinner table, the hours when children see a parent, and the evenings available for friends. Barelas belongs in this index not just because important work happened nearby, but because people made lives around that work. Machinery is the most conspicuous survivor. Time is the harder thing to display.

The entry treats deaths as reflection only. It does not reproduce accident mechanisms, stage incidents or turn suffering into a hunt. No claim is made that either man's memory represents every worker, ethnicity, household or generation.

## 3. Then / now

These are retrospective interviews, selected and introduced by an author, rather than unmediated recordings or contemporary timecards. Their value is personal perspective; exact dates and rates require separate documentary checking. Do not convert recollection into an operating specification or a universal employment condition. [2]

The NHCC's **Barelas Photo Collection, 2005-03**, preserves community records and describes a separate oral-history collection. Its inventory discusses a historical exhibition. Neither statement proves that the exhibition is still installed or that the WHEELS excerpts are the same recordings held there. [3]

Today's public research setting is not the former shop floor. Studying a workplace in a library or gallery is a complete and legitimate visit, not an inferior substitute that requires trespassing to finish.
