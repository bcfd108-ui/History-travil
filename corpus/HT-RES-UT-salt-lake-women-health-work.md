## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** becoming a physician was not the end of the work. Training other women and earning a household living were part of the same career.

The family association publishes **Romania B. Pratt's memoir dated March 19, 1881**, with later annotations distinguished from her text. She describes returning to Salt Lake City in 1877, offering free lectures, then teaching anatomy, physiology and obstetrics. Her summary is direct: **“my life was that of a busy practitioner and teacher of medical classes.”** [5]

This is a named worker's firsthand written account, not an interview recording. The online transcription was read; the original manuscript and attached medical report were not. Her words connect professional work to supporting her family, without reducing that work to a celebrity biography. The account also explains that professional demands displaced another organizational responsibility. [5]

**Original interpretation:** this is a story about creating a workforce as well as serving one patient at a time. A class, a lecture and an administrative task can be part of health work even though they look different from a clinical encounter. The visitor need not inspect anyone's case history to understand that distinction.

A published **1882 Deseret Hospital dedication report** identifies different jobs: **Ellen B. Ferguson**, resident physician and surgeon; **Mary F. VanSchoonhoven**, clinical registrar and dispensary clerk; **Mary Ann McLean**, matron. [6] These names broaden the labor frame. They are documentary role listings, not firsthand testimony from those women or a license to imagine their daily routines.

## 3. Then / now

The historical **Deseret Hospital** is not the present Church History Library, Museum of Utah, or a currently operating hospital with a familiar-sounding name. Its dedication was in 1882; the documentary edition says it had closed by 1894, while its note preserves **1893 or 1894** as the uncertain closure date. [6] No uninterrupted institutional succession is asserted.

The contemporary archive preserves records; the public museum interprets state history. Neither is represented as Pratt's original office, classroom, home or workplace. The story is anchored through documents rather than a surviving ward or treatment room.

The source edition also cautions against equating privately run medical classes with a hospital-run school. [6] A teaching physician, a hospital association and a modern nursing institution must not be merged into one continuous organization without evidence.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Set the gate before departure.** Choose either confirmed public research time or the online version. During the ambiguous holiday window, do not make a dedicated archive journey on the assumption that the published weekday pattern wins.
2. **Begin with the worker's own paragraph.** Read the short memoir passage about practice and teaching before looking at institutional names. Keep its date and transcription provenance visible.
3. **Add the organizational layer.** Compare a public, published role list with the first-person account. Notice that different kinds of work have different kinds of surviving evidence.
4. **If at a public institution:** use only what is actually displayed or legitimately supplied. A staff-assisted catalogue search may establish a next source, but a reference is not proof of a document's contents or access status.
5. **End with the strongest story moment:** ask what it meant to be responsible for both doing skilled work and enabling someone else to learn it. Leave with the worker as a person making professional choices, not with a reconstructed patient story.

This progression works within one nonclinical public setting. The optional museum is not a mandatory second stop, and no walking route between institutions is activated.

The hospital closure and class-affiliation qualifications above must remain in travel copy. Public institutional memory can emphasize leaders while leaving less prominent workers thinly documented; the role list does not fill that gap with invented experiences.
