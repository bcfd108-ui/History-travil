# HT-RES-NY-erie-canal-lockport — Five stone steps up the escarpment, and the two concrete locks that replaced their twins
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A staircase of five stone locks climbs the Niagara Escarpment in the middle of a city that took its name from them — the most famous structure on the Erie Canal, still standing beside the two large electric locks that do its work today.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a working lock complex, not a ruin: the Lockport Locks in the city of Lockport, Niagara County, New York, where the Erie Canal crosses the Niagara Escarpment. Two systems stand side by side on the same ground — Locks E-34 and E-35 of the present New York State Canal system, in service, and the surviving north flight of the enlarged Erie's combined Locks 67–71, the "Flight of Five," kept as a bypass flume and, in the Historic American Engineering Record's words, an in-situ outdoor museum. The operator of the canal is the New York State Canal Corporation. The Canal Corporation's boating-hours page states the terms for the water side, and they are printed here as that page carries them: for the 2026 navigation season the system was scheduled to open Friday, May 15, 2026, and remain open through Wednesday, October 14, 2026; there are no tolls or fees for recreational use of the canal system; standard lock hours are 8:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. daily, with Lock E-34/35 at Lockport among the structures on extended hours, 8:00 a.m. to 9:00 p.m., through the peak season. Those are vessel hours, printed because they are the operator's own current statement; the locks district on land is public ground around the forebay at 80 Canal Street, the address the Canal Corporation's own September 2025 bicentennial flotilla advisory gives for the Flight of Five forebay. The locks are contributing structures of the Lockport Industrial District, added to the National Register of Historic Places on November 11, 1975 (NRHP reference no. 75001211, per the National Park Service's National Register record).

## 2. The story
The Erie Canal had to get boats from the level of Lake Erie down the Niagara Escarpment — the same rock ridge that makes Niagara Falls — and at Lockport the ground rises about 60 feet in a single steep pitch. The HAER history of the locks (HAER No. NY-61) states the problem plainly: the size of a lock gate one person could work limited how much lift a single lock could take, and the rise was too abrupt for locks spaced out along a channel. Engineer Nathan S. Roberts's answer was a double set of five combined locks — five chambers stepped directly into one another, one flight for boats climbing west toward Lake Erie and one for boats descending east, each lock 90 feet long by 15 feet wide with a lift of about 12 feet, the five together making the 60-foot rise. West of the locks, the canal itself had to be cut through the crest of the ridge in a deep rock channel, the Deep Cut.

The Lockport flight was the last piece of the original canal finished. The HAER history records that the flight's capstone was laid on **June 24, 1825**, months before the last portion of the Deep Cut was done; that when the finished section between Buffalo and Tonawanda Creek was filled in August 1825, the water flooded the Deep Cut workings and delayed the excavation until a drain could be dug; and that Zenas P. Thrall, who had come to Lockport for canal work in 1821, was appointed the first lock tender, his appointment letter written three days before the opening. The canal's grand opening came on **October 26, 1825**, when a flotilla headed by the *Seneca Chief*, carrying Governor DeWitt Clinton, left Buffalo for New York City and the ceremonial "wedding of the waters." The town that grew at the locks took its name from them.

Success outgrew the ditch almost at once. The first enlargement of the Erie Canal ran, system-wide, from 1836 to 1862, under engineer Thomas Evershed at Lockport: the canal prism was widened and deepened and the Lockport locks were rebuilt at 110 feet by 18 feet, raising boat capacity from seventy-five tons to two hundred forty, per the HAER summary. At Lockport the work raced the state's finances. The HAER addendum dates the extant north flight — the surviving Locks 67–71 — to **1838–1842**, and the HAER history records that the north flight was ready when the 1842 navigation season opened, just before the Stop Law of 1842 halted enlargement work across the state; the canal commissioners reported the tier "constructed in the most substantial and perfect manner." The enlarged canal as a whole was not declared complete until 1862.

The third canal replaced half the staircase. Between 1909 and 1918 the Erie was rebuilt as the New York State Barge Canal. At Lockport, the HAER record states, the southern tier of the combined locks was removed and two electric lift locks were built in its place — concrete chambers with steel gates, 310 feet by 45 feet, with a combined lift of 49 feet. These are today's Locks 34 and 35, built 1910–1918 per the HAER addendum. The northern tier was left standing: its miter gates were removed so the old flight could pass surplus water around the new locks, the duty it still performs. The HAER addendum's significance statement gives the ground its due: Locks 67–71 are a rare surviving example of multiple locks connected directly into a flight and of cut-stone lock construction, "the most complete surviving artifact from the enlarged Erie Canal," and Locks 34 and 35 are an early example of concrete, electrically powered lock design whose methods were also used on the Panama Canal. The site is unique, the record concludes, in having the two designs together.

The National Register followed the engineers. The Lockport Industrial District, including both sets of locks, was listed on November 11, 1975. In fall 2005 HAER began a new documentation project at the locks, laser-scanning Locks 67–71 and re-photographing the complex — the survey this entry's first photograph comes from. Restoration of the Flight of Five has proceeded chamber by chamber in recent years; a local marker record notes the restored locks reopened in 2014.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** two parallel stone staircases, ten chambers in all, each 90 by 15 feet in 1825 and 110 by 18 feet in the enlargement, lifting boats 60 feet through the escarpment rock, with a deep rock cut carrying the canal west from the top lock. **Now:** one staircase survives — the north flight of the enlarged canal, Locks 67–71, its gates gone, carrying spill water — and beside it the two 310-foot concrete chambers of Locks 34 and 35 lift today's boats the same pitch in two steps.

**Then:** a canal town built around a construction camp, named for its locks, its first lock tender appointed three days before the canal opened. **Now:** the locks remain the center of the city of Lockport, with the flight, the forebay, and the lock-tender story interpreted on the ground where the work happened.

**Then:** a HAER photographer in 2006 framed the flight from the railroad bridge — stone chambers stepping up under the Pine Street bridge, the modern lock and its powerhouse in the foreground (see §7). **Now:** the same view is the visitor's introduction: the working canal in front, the nineteenth-century staircase rising behind it.

## 4. What's still there
- The north flight of the enlarged Erie's combined locks — Locks 67, 68, 69, 70, and 71 — cut-stone chambers stepped into the escarpment, extant 1838–1842 fabric per the HAER addendum, now serving as the bypass/spillway for the modern locks
- Locks E-34 and E-35 (built 1910–1918), the concrete electric lift locks in daily service on the present Erie Canal, 310 by 45 feet with a combined lift of 49 feet per the HAER summary
- The Deep Cut alignment west of the locks, the rock channel blasted through the crest of the ridge in 1825
- The Pine Street bridge crossing above the flight and the lock complex's former power plant building, which the HAER photo captions identify as the Erie Canal Museum building in the survey's later frames
- Three of the five arched foot bridges that crossed the north flight, per the HAER history (the other two were removed after the Barge Canal locks opened)
- The Flight of Five forebay at 80 Canal Street, the public ground at the foot of the locks used for canal events, per the Canal Corporation's 2025 advisory

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the forebay, 80 Canal Street:** Start at the foot of the escarpment, on the public ground the Canal Corporation itself uses as the Flight of Five address, and take in the whole pitch of the ridge at once.
2. **Along the north flight:** Walk the surviving staircase — Locks 67 through 71 — reading the cut-stone chambers as one connected machine, each gate recess and chamber wall at the enlarged canal's 110-by-18-foot scale.
3. **At Locks E-34 and E-35:** Watch a modern lockage beside the old flight — two concrete chambers doing in two lifts what the stone flight did in five, on the ground where the southern tier stood until 1910.
4. **Under and over Pine Street:** Take the HAER photograph's own vantage (§7) — the flight stepping up under the bridge, the modern lock in the foreground — and match the frame to the ground.
5. **West toward the Deep Cut:** End at the top of the flight, where the canal leaves the locks through the rock channel whose flooding in August 1825 nearly delayed the canal's opening.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The enlarged flight's dates do not agree across the records, and the disagreement is printed rather than resolved. The HAER addendum dates the extant structures precisely: Locks 67–71 (north flight), 1838–1842, and the HAER history confirms the flight was ready for the 1842 navigation season. Local marker texts at the locks carry the combined locks' rebuilding as 1836–1847. The National Register record for the Lockport Industrial District lists 1859 among its significant years, and district summaries describe the northern tier as constructed in 1859. The HAER 1983 summary dates "the first enlargement" 1836–1862 — the system-wide enlargement window, not the Lockport flight's own construction. Each figure is carried here as its source states it; this draft does not reconcile them, and no single construction year for the surviving flight is asserted beyond the HAER addendum's 1838–1842.

The original flight's completion day is split by one day. The HAER history records the capstone laid on June 24, 1825. The Lock Construction marker at the site states the original locks were completed on June 25, 1825. Both print.

Two lift figures circulate for this ground and are easily conflated. The stone flight's total rise was 60 feet (five locks at about 12 feet each, per the HAER summary). The modern Locks 34 and 35 have a combined lift of 49 feet (same source). Modern summaries that give the Flight of Five itself a 49-foot rise have borrowed the concrete locks' number; the figures belong to different structures and are kept separate here.

The HAER record carries no latitude or longitude for the locks, and the National Register record consulted for the district carries none either, so the locator below prints none — coordinates are not taken from lead-list sources.

No land-side admission fee or visitor-center hours are printed: the operator pages consulted (the Canal Corporation's boating pages) state terms for vessels, not for visiting the locks district on foot, and no fee is asserted for ground the sources describe as public.
