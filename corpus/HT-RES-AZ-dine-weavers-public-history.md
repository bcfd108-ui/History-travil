## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A textile may be labeled by a region or trading post. That can hide the person who imagined it. Begin with a weaver speaking for herself.

**Named first-person source:** The U.S. Department of State's Art in Embassies artist page publishes **Marilou Schultz's** first-person account. She identifies herself as Navajo, describes learning from her mother, **Martha Gorman Schultz**, and remembers weaving during her education for financial support. Her artistic purpose is explicit: **“I weave because of my love of weaving and the challenge of creating unique weavings.”** [3]

This is an artist's published statement hosted by a federal arts program, **not a Navajo Nation government publication**, not a new interview and not audio heard in this research. Its undated present-tense employment and residence details are not treated as current. The usable evidence concerns her own described learning, livelihood and creative ambition.

**Separate museum interpretation:** The *Twilight* exhibition describes how individual weavers' identities could be overshadowed by trading-post names and how artists developed their own markets and artistic presentation. It specifically names **Calvin Toney** and **Florence Riggs** among the Diné artists represented. That is the museum's interpretive framing, not Schultz's own description of this exhibition. [2]

**Collection corroboration:** The Heard's textile collection page identifies Schultz's **Infusion: Contemporary Wearing Blanket (2013), 4951-71**. It also distinguishes Diné and Pueblo textile traditions rather than treating them as interchangeable. [4] The collection record connects a named maker to a documented work, but does not establish that work's present gallery location.

**Interpretive synthesis:** Family learning and individual invention are not opposites. Schultz's statement allows both to remain visible, alongside the economic work of making art. The visitor need not interpret sacred meaning, reproduce a design or claim expertise in a technique to recognize authorship. No weaver here is presented as speaking for all Diné people, and no museum statement stands in for sovereign Tribal authority.

**Now:** the artist's first-person statement and the museum's named collection record make it possible to foreground an individual creator. [3,4]
