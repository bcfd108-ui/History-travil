# Historic Fort Snelling — The Dakota families held below the bluff
Research draft · Minnesota OP01 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

**What happened after the fighting.** The Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community’s published timeline says that after the U.S.–Dakota War, “almost all of the Mdewakanton were forcibly removed from Minnesota.” At Fort Snelling, the civilian detention camp stood on the Minnesota River flats below the military post. A surviving federal record indexes Assistant Adjutant General R. C. Olin’s letter forwarding the list of people in the camp. MNHS attributes the winter census to Lt. William McKusick and describes its households recorded by band, with heads of families, family sizes and property. The report’s contents list and the state archive’s description were read. The original handwritten names and full federal appendix were not obtained in this pass. [3–6]

## 3 · Then / now

**Then — detention in the flats, military power above.** The 1970s landmark documentation describes the fort occupying the bluff. The state archaeological inventory puts the fenced 1862–63 camp on river bottomland below. MNHS records approximately 1,600 people detained there, most noncombatants, and forced relocation beginning in spring 1863. The Community’s timeline records removal as part of the consequences that followed war and treaty breaches, and separately records the 1865 federal executions of Ṡákpedaŋ and Medicine Bottle at Fort Snelling. The winter detention and those later executions are separate episodes of loss. [3,7–9]

**Now — a visitor building with room to read.** MNHS operates the Plank Museum & Visitor Center on the historic-site campus. The visitor center is a lawful anchor for learning about the people held below. The dossier keeps movements to the historical detention or memorial ground outside the trip. [1–2,9]

## 4 · What’s still there

- **Public interior when open.** Plank Museum & Visitor Center and its regular exhibits. [1–2]
- **Public exterior only in designated areas.** Signed arrival path within the visitor campus. The operator’s winter guided tour rules apply at the upper fort. [1–2]
- **Not an access destination in this dossier.** River-flats confinement, removal, burial, memorial and treaty-related ground. The camp photograph represents detention ground, separate from the public reading anchor. [2,7–10]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Historic location.** The former camp on the river flats below Fort Snelling. The military post occupied the bluff. The sites are distinct in the archaeological and landmark records. [7–9]
2. **Lawful anchor.** Drive up to the signed Plank Museum & Visitor Center, then take the short visitor path. Stay in the public building and its designated approach. If it is closed, use the readings remotely. [1–2]
3. **Strongest story moment — Families in a column of names.** In the public interpretation area, read the Community’s sentence, then the archive’s description of how families were counted. Pause there for the people whom the columns record. [3–6]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

MNHS’s FAQ describes nearly 300 deaths and mentions illness and attacks by soldiers. Its Peace Party treatment gives an illness death range of 130–300. These are estimates with differing stated causes, not two counts to add. The state file reports approximately 1,600 held and compares the December camp census to a smaller count upon arrival at Crow Creek. The count difference is not an independent death total, and timing and population bases differ. The 1863 report scan retrieved here indexes No. 156, without the appendix itself. No graves, camp circuit, removal path, or exact present sightline is established in this dossier. [4–8,11]
