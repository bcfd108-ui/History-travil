## 2. The story
Read the treaty alongside the Nations' accounts, not in place of them. The federal document is dated **December 26, 1854** and calls its location **She-nah-nam or Medicine Creek**. It records land cession, reserved tracts and continuing fishing and other resource provisions. Article III's wording includes **“The right of taking fish at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations.”** [4] That short quotation identifies an enduring subject of the document; this dossier is not legal advice or a present fishing authorization.

The **Puyallup Tribe's own history** names the place **šxʷnanəm**, and explains that McAllister Creek is a later settler name. Its account says people arrived on December 24 expecting a potlatch, encountered documents asking them to surrender lands, and faced poorly communicated negotiations. It reports continuing belief that some marks were forced or forged. [5] Attribute that account to the Tribe instead of presenting a smooth consensual transaction as the only history.

The **Nisqually Tribe's own history** likewise states that whether **Leschi** agreed to the treaty is heavily disputed. It identifies the original reservation as 1,280 acres and places this within the history of a fishing people whose life was bound to the watershed. [6] A name or mark in the surviving government record is not, by itself, proof of informed consent. The differing evidence belongs in the interpretation, not in a footnote that excuses a celebratory story.

The Puyallup account follows the conflict into the Treaty Wars and the **1856 Fox Island Council**, naming **Chief Squatahan** in the subsequent negotiations. [5] This gives a visitor a way to understand that the 1854 document did not settle the consequences for communities in one afternoon. Do not collapse separate peoples, leaders, councils or later reservation changes into a single event.

At the chosen anchor, the Squaxin Island museum tells its own history through the relationship of the People of the Water to seven South Puget Sound inlets. Its page quotes **Rhonda Foster**, identified there as Cultural Resources Department director: **“This museum is the heart of our community.”** [2] This is a published community voice, not a newly recorded interview or confirmation of a current officeholder. The public museum is a place to encounter living interpretation, not to consume someone else's loss as a spectacle.

## 3. Then / now
Then, government officials produced an English-language treaty instrument within unequal negotiations. Tribal accounts preserve questions about what was represented, understood and agreed. Today, the surviving document can be studied publicly, while the Nations continue to speak for themselves. [4–6]

The museum's contemporary building is not a relic from the negotiations. Its relevance is interpretive authority: it places treaty history among continuing relations with water, resources and community. The historical ground, current community land, public museum and digital archive are four distinct things. None of them grants automatic access to the others.

This is a reflection stop concerning dispossession, disputed consent and continuity. It is neither a reenactment nor a hunt for the exact spot where an individual supposedly stood.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Before arrival, establish the museum gate.** Check the current opening status, particularly winter weather and the November holiday schedule. If closed, retain an archive-only version rather than improvising a cultural-land visit.
2. **Begin with the museum's own framework.** Let its public interpretation establish the People of the Water and the seven inlets before narrowing to a treaty date.
3. **Compare two kinds of record.** Read the treaty's rights language beside the Puyallup and Nisqually accounts of negotiation and disputed consent. Ask what the official instrument records and what it cannot establish on its own.
4. **End with the strongest reflection: the document is old; the people and obligations are not past tense.** Pause within the museum's offered public space, without making a grave, sacred object or exact signing location a required destination.

Optional question for an available interpreter: **What does the museum most want visitors to understand about the difference between the treaty's written terms and the community's experience of them?** Accept a boundary or referral. Do not solicit restricted teachings, demand a performance, photograph visitors as cultural examples or interrupt a community event.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- December 24 is the arrival date in the Puyallup narrative; December 26 is the instrument's date. They are different stages, not competing dates for one action. [4,5]
- Spellings differ among the original document, later transcriptions and Tribal publications. Preserve **She-nah-nam**, **šxʷnanəm**, Medicine Creek and McAllister Creek with their source framing; do not invent a standardized pronunciation or translate them without evidence. [4,5]
- The Nisqually account's dispute over Leschi's agreement is unresolved by merely reproducing a signature page. This dossier does not adjudicate the authenticity of individual marks. [6]
