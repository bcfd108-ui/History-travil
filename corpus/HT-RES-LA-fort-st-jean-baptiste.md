# Fort St. Jean Baptiste State Historic Site replica

**HT-RES-LA-fort-st-jean-baptiste · Louisiana OP01 · Labeled research draft · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate
Use the staffed Louisiana State Parks site at 155 Rue Jefferson Wednesday–Sunday, 9 a.m.–5 p.m. The operator closes Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year’s Day. Use the open entrance and designated replica paths only in passable weather. Checked November 20, 2026. [1–2]

## 2. Story
The expedition account attributed to Pénicaut describes going out from Mobile Bay with trade goods and twenty men, “of whom I was one.” This is the English translation printed in Bolton and Barker’s 1904 source reader, whose editors identify Margry’s edition and an earlier English translation. The phrase puts a person in the boat and tells us the kind of testimony we are reading. Pénicaut describes an expedition carrying goods toward an overland market, rather than a post already furnished and established. [3, pp. 50–51]

The account distinguishes lodging from storage. It describes two early structures made with local participation and a contingent left behind to watch the merchandise. Those modest workspaces help explain why a post made sense to French authorities. They also warn against reading the substantial replica as a model of those first buildings. Pénicaut wrote from within the French enterprise. His account does not give the full perspective of the Natchitoches Caddo people whose homeland and trade networks the expedition entered. The historical encounter belongs in the account, without any route to a Tribal community or activity. [2–3]

The replica offers a later architectural question. Louisiana State Parks says reconstruction began in 1979 under Samuel Wilson Jr., using plans by Ignace François Broutin and archival research. The timber palisade and hardware are modern interpretive fabric. Their materials and construction can help explain the organized work needed to create the later post. They cannot establish what the 1714 expedition saw or the location from which a visitor could view the original foundation. [1–2]

## 3. Then and now
Pénicaut’s translated account describes an early expedition, storehouse and lodging. The operator places the modern state historic site a few hundred yards from the original fort. The staffed entry and designated replica enclosure are the only visit anchor. The original footprint is unresolved for public routing. [1–3]

## 4. Surviving features and access
- Signed visitor entrance and public interpretation, staffed interior only where open.
- Replica palisade and signed visitor path, designated public grounds during operating hours and safe conditions.
- Original fort footprint, shore, battlefield ground and Tribal community or activities, documentary context only. [1–2]

## 5. Public progression
Drive to the signed state historic-site entrance and start with the operator’s exhibits. Follow the short marked inland approach into the open reconstruction. At an authorized viewing place, compare a replica doorway or enclosure with the simple functions described in the translated account. Finish with **One building to sleep in, one to store goods**. The strongest story is the work of bringing and holding supplies, without staging anyone’s conflict or ceremony. [1–3]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
The NPS trail page calls the fort built in 1714 on St. Denis’s orders; Louisiana State Parks differentiates the 1714 expedition from Dutisné’s 1716 garrison assignment. The translated Pénicaut passage identifies two structures and leaves the precise construction sequence of the later formal fort open. The Gallica catalog request for a potential early plan returned HTTP 403. The web tool rejected the source-reader scan with a content-length error; its 18,314,994-byte Commons mirror was obtained directly and read. Precise footprint, primary manuscript inspection and an identified original-plan sheet remain pending. [1–4]
