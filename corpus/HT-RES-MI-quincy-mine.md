# HT-RES-MI-quincy-mine — Old Reliable's No. 2 ground

Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Above Hancock, on the brow of Quincy Hill, stands the surface plant of the Quincy Mining Company: the 1908 No. 2 shaft-rockhouse, its roof pitched at the lode's own angle, and the 1918 No. 2 hoist house, built to hold what the National Register nomination calls the largest hoisting engine in the world. The Quincy Mining Company Historic District — some 779 acres from Portage Lake to the hill's brow, a National Historic Landmark district — preserves the whole apparatus of a copper mine as work: seven shafts and their surface works, the smelter on the lake, and the company neighborhoods below the hill. The National Park Service tells the mine's story on its Keweenaw National Historical Park pages, and the ground itself is kept and toured by the Quincy Mine Hoist Association. This entry treats the mine as industry: the hoist, the shaft, and the rock's path from a mile underground to the train cars.

## 1. Trip gate
- **The visit is a guided tour.** The operator, the Quincy Mine Hoist Association, runs tours from its ground on US-41 above Hancock. The full tour runs about two and a quarter hours; an above-ground-only hoist tour is also offered. There is no self-guided underground access, and this entry describes none.
- **Prices as the operator prints them** (checked November 26, 2026): Full Tour — adult $40; youth (7–17) $20; college student with ID $20; children 6 and under free with a paid adult. Above Ground Only Hoist Tour $15, covering the steam hoist, museum access, and Shaft House #2.
- **Underground conditions are stated by the operator and are part of the gate:** the workings hold 43 degrees year-round and are damp; the full tour walks about a mile on wet gravel; waterproof boots are recommended; a hardhat is worn at all times underground; visitors are asked to arrive 20 minutes early. A transportation vehicle for visitors needing mobility assistance is available underground — the operator asks that the gift shop be contacted before booking.
- **Animals:** only service animals on guided tours; supervised pets are welcome on the grounds.
- **Contact as the operator prints it:** (906) 482-3101; info@quincymine.com; 49750 US Highway 41, Hancock, MI 49930.

## 2. The story
The Quincy Mining Company was organized in 1846, in the first American copper boom, and it outlasted every other venture of that boom — the National Register nomination's judgment is that of the numerous mining ventures spawned by the nation's first copper boom, Quincy alone survived. The early Keweenaw mines worked native copper in fissure deposits; the Pewabic mine was begun in 1853 and its amygdaloid lode found in 1856 (ASME landmark brochure), and Quincy was the first company to recognize the limits of fissure mining and shift to the amygdaloid beds on which the district's future depended (nomination). The Historic American Engineering Record's summary is plain about the result: Quincy was Michigan's second largest copper producer, and one of national and international importance — a mine extremely long-lived and profitable.

The Civil War made it first. Between 1862 and 1868 Quincy ranked first nationally in copper production, supplying the raw material for brass buttons, copper canteens, bronze cannon, and naval sheathing; when the war began in 1861, Michigan produced 89.5 percent of United States copper and the Quincy mine accounted for 56 percent of that figure (nomination). The company earned its name in the decades after: "Old Reliable," the nomination records, for a fifty-four year sequence of dividends paid to its stockholders and its ability to keep mining through hard times when all but the giant Calumet and Hecla had shut down. The ASME brochure puts the dividend record in figures: first declared in 1862, more than $27,000,000 paid out, and dividends annually without interruption from 1867 to 1921.

Quincy's real subject is the machinery of depth. The company replaced its primitive ladders with man-engines as early as 1850, introduced the first power drills in its Pewabic mine shortly after the Civil War, and in 1873 introduced the shaft-rockhouse itself — the building type that consolidated breaking, sorting, and cleaning the rock at the spot where it was dumped from the skips, and that served as a model for the Michigan copper industry (nomination). The hoists grew with the shafts: in 1894 a 2,500-horsepower hoist from E. P. Allis & Company, the biggest Allis had ever built, raising skips at 2,500 to 3,000 feet per minute; in 1917 the company ordered its largest compound, condensing steam hoist from the Nordberg Manufacturing Company. The Nordberg operated at 3,200 feet per minute and could lift ten tons of copper rock per trip — the largest steam hoisting engine in the world (nomination). The No. 2 hoist house was built in 1918 to house it, a building the nomination describes in the terms of its finish: brick veneer, Palladian windows, and a green tile roof, over the revolutionary engine inside.

The No. 2 shaft-rockhouse above it is the third building over its shaft, rebuilt in 1908. The National Park Service's account gives the structure its working logic: rock was already being processed as it was hoisted — skips dumped through grizzlies, the rock falling to crushers and into train cars below — and the roof's 54-degree angle matches the angle of the lode itself. The shaft below it became the longest in the Keweenaw: by 1931 the No. 2 shaft had reached 9,009 feet, the deepest mine in the United States (nomination). The Park Service gives the human measure of that depth: miners at the bottom levels spent a ten-hour shift more than a mile underground.

Around the plant, the company built a town in pieces. The nomination counts the district's housing subdivisions below the hill — Limerick, Singsing, Frenchtown, Hardscrabble, Pewabic, Franklin, Backstreet — with the earliest, Lower Pewabic, dating from 1899, and the Pewabic mines themselves acquired in 1891. By 1905 some 1,400 employees worked at the mine location; the Cornish had been the dominant group of the nineteenth century, and by then Finns accounted for a third of the foreign-born (nomination). The labor history is part of the record and is not softened in the nomination's telling: in July 1913 the district's underground workers struck with the Western Federation of Miners, Quincy answered with evictions and 1,200 imported strikebreakers, and the strike of 1913–14 ended in an unqualified victory for the owners.

The end came in stages, and the sources do not stage it the same way (§6). The nomination's own narrative: the drop in copper prices in 1931 closed down operations; the company geared up again after 1937 for rising prices and the demands of World War II; in 1943 it opened a reclamation plant to work the old stamp sands; in 1957 mining operations ceased, and the reclamation plant produced copper for another ten years. The company's land passed largely to the Quincy Development Corporation, which still owns most of it (nomination). Preservation came from the Quincy Mine Hoist Association, which holds a 99-year lease on the No. 2 properties: the Association resheathed the shaft-rockhouse, the Nordberg engine was restored to mint condition for public exhibition, and in 1984 the No. 2 Hoist was designated a National Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark by the ASME. The Park Service records the rockhouse's rehabilitation in 1987 — and its present distinction: it is the only Keweenaw shaft-rockhouse accessible to the public, as part of the Quincy Mine tour.

## 3. Then / now
- **Then:** A working surface plant — seven shafts along the hill, skips rising at thousands of feet per minute, rock crushed and loaded to train cars at the No. 2 rockhouse. **Now:** Every shaft-rockhouse but No. 2 has been removed; the other shafts remain evident, fenced off and covered with steel grating (nomination). No. 2 stands rehabilitated and toured.
- **Then:** The Nordberg hoist of 1917–20, the largest steam hoisting engine in the world, raising ten tons of copper rock per trip. **Now:** The same engine in the same hoist house, restored to mint condition, a National Historic Mechanical Engineering Landmark (1984), and the centerpiece of the operator's above-ground tour.
- **Then:** A company landscape entire — mine, smelter, and named neighborhoods of company housing below the hill, 1,400 employees in 1905. **Now:** A 779-acre landmark district, most of its land still held by the Quincy Development Corporation, its housing neighborhoods become private Hancock streets, its plant kept by a nonprofit association on a 99-year lease.
- **Then:** Miners descending to levels more than a mile underground for ten-hour shifts. **Now:** Visitors descend a 2,000-foot adit into the 1860s workings of the seventh level, in hardhats, for just over an hour — the operator's measured sample of the same underground.

## 4. What's still there
- **The No. 2 shaft-rockhouse (1908):** the third rockhouse over the No. 2 shaft, its roof at the lode's 54-degree angle, overlooking Portage Lake; rehabilitated 1987; per the National Park Service, the only Keweenaw shaft-rockhouse accessible to the public.
- **The No. 2 hoist house (1918):** brick veneer, Palladian windows, green tile roof — built for the Nordberg compound condensing steam hoist, which remains inside with its condensing equipment, restored to mint condition (nomination).
- **The earlier hoists in the record:** the nomination's resource list counts the No. 2 Hoist (1918–20), the No. 2 Hoist (1894), and the No. 2 Hoist (1882) among the mine location's contributing resources.
- **The other shaft sites:** the remaining shafts of the seven, fenced and steel-grated, their rockhouses gone (nomination) — visible ground, not visitor ground.
- **The surface plant along US-41:** the mine location's shops, offices, dry houses, and captain's buildings as the nomination's inventory lists them, parallel to the old Calumet Road.
- **The Quincy Smelting Works (1898):** on Portage Lake at the foot of the hill, with its cupola and reverberatory furnace buildings and the 1920 Walker casting machine — the nomination calls it possibly the only essentially unaltered smelting complex of its era left in the United States. It is district fabric, described here as the nomination carries it; the operator's tours in §5 are the mine tours.
- **The neighborhoods:** Limerick, Singsing, Frenchtown, Hardscrabble, Pewabic, Franklin, Backstreet — company housing subdivisions, now private residences (§10).

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
The operator's full tour is the progression; its shape is printed as the operator gives it.
1. **Arrive twenty minutes early** (operator's instruction) at the US-41 ground above Hancock.
2. **Above ground, 30–45 minutes:** the surface plant first — the hoist house and the largest steam hoist ever constructed, then Shaft House #2, the rockhouse over the shaft that reached more than 9,000 feet. The above-ground-only tour ($15) is this portion, with museum access.
3. **The descent:** the tour enters through a 2,000-foot adit into historic workings dating to the 1860s — the seventh level.
4. **Underground, over an hour:** about a mile of walking on wet gravel at a year-round 43 degrees, hardhat on, through the stopes and drifts of the level the 1860s miners cut — read against §2's ten-hour shifts at depths this tour does not reach.
5. **Back on the surface:** the rest of the hill reads differently after the level — the fenced shafts, the shops and dry houses along the highway, and the neighborhoods below, where the men who worked the No. 2 lived.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **The No. 2 shaft's length is given four ways.** The nomination's significance text: a shaft that "eventually reached 9,000 feet." The nomination's history text: by 1931, 9,009 feet. The National Park Service's story page: "over 9,000 feet along the angle," with an overall depth of 6,225 feet. A Park Service teaching title gives 9,240 feet; secondary sources give 9,260. This entry prints the nomination's 9,009 feet for 1931 in §2 and does not reconcile the rest. The vertical depth likewise prints as the Park Service's 6,225 feet; rounder secondary figures (6,200) are not carried.
- **The Nordberg hoist carries three dates.** Ordered in 1917 (nomination). The hoist house built in 1918 to house it (nomination). The nomination's resource list dates the No. 2 Hoist "(1918-20)," and the ASME landmark brochure titles it "Quincy No 2 Mine Hoist (1920)." All print; the entry does not pick an installation year.
- **The end of operations does not reconcile.** The nomination: closed down in 1931, geared up again after 1937, a reclamation plant from 1943, mining operations ceased in 1957, reclamation for ten years more. The ASME brochure: the mine "inoperative since 1945." Park Service teaching material likewise dates the stop of copper mining to 1945. The nomination's period of significance closes at 1931. The likeliest reading — deep mining ended in the 1940s while reclamation ran on — is an inference, and this entry does not print it as fact.
- **The dividend run is stated two ways.** "A fifty-four year sequence of dividends" (nomination); "dividends annually without interruption 1867–1921," first declared 1862 (ASME). A secondary form, 1868–1920, is not carried.
- **The rockhouse's height** — 120 feet, with a 12-foot-diameter sheave — rests on the ASME brochure alone among the sources checked; it prints here as the ASME's figure, not as surveyed fact.
- **The district's boundary record carries no single point.** The nomination's §10 gives multi-point UTM boundary references for a 779-acre district, and the scanned figures come through OCR-degraded; no verified point for the No. 2 ground was established from a primary source in this pass. §10 leaves the coordinates blank rather than convert or borrow one.

No people are in the frame. It is the largest steam hoisting engine in the world as the HAER documented it in place in 1978 — the mine as work and industry, photographed by the federal survey inside the building that was built to hold it.
