## 1. The story

Bayhorse is most legible as a system: ore, fuel, furnace, transport and the households that kept them working. Its stone charcoal kilns are not decorative ruins beside a mining town. They explain a recurring industrial problem: finding rich rock was not the same as processing it economically. This is the dossier's interpretive frame, inferred from the connected mining and preservation records below, not a resident's quotation.

The USGS regional map report dates active mining to an 1877 discovery of lead-zinc replacement deposits on Bayhorse Creek. It describes subsequent mining as waxing and waning rather than ending permanently with the town's apparent abandonment. Its geographic scope is the larger Bayhorse area, not just the present state-park townsite. That distinction matters: a ghost town date is not a last-production date for every mine in the district. [B2]

The Idaho Heritage Trust's conservation account connects improved ore reduction with profitable production, emphasizing the 1882–1890s period. It dates construction of nine stone charcoal kilns to 1882 and identifies their purpose as fuel production for the smelter. The Trust records multiple preservation grants, including work associated with the 1882 Wells Fargo Building. The surviving place is therefore an industrial remnant under continuing care, not an untouched scene abandoned yesterday. [B3]

**Index-sourced, full nomination unread:** the indexed National Register nomination description records a narrow canyon settlement along one winding road, with log and frame buildings, clapboard and board-and-batten siding, steep roofs and false fronts. Its 1885 inventory lists three stores, a hotel, a restaurant, a meat market, a lodging house and five saloons, with two additional saloons by 1889. It counts nine original kilns but says only five or six survived in good condition when surveyed. That deliberately imprecise historic count must not become a modern claim that exactly six intact kilns await the visitor. [B4; index-sourced, full nomination unread]

A family-memory source opens a less visible history of labor and movement. In her 2008 account, reproduced by the Basque Museum & Cultural Center, Flora Urresti Aspiazu wrote: "The Basques employed in the Bayhorse, Livingston, Clayton, Goldberg and Patterson mines were mostly single men." Her account also describes ore being trucked to Mackay and sent by rail to Utah smelters. These are recollections about a wider regional mining world, not an eyewitness description of Bayhorse's 1882 furnace or evidence that a particular surviving cabin belonged to a named Basque worker. The museum marks its own additions in brackets, which must stay distinct from her words. [B5]

An Idaho Geological Survey presentation adds the forest labor behind the stone kilns: it reports 48 men supplying 180,000 bushels of charcoal to sustain smelting and identifies 1888 as the last full-production year. The slide does not settle the time interval for that charcoal figure. Do not convert it into a daily or annual consumption rate. It directs readers to Victoria E. Mitchell's *History of the Mines in the Bayhorse Area, Custer County, Idaho*, Staff Report S-99-8 (1999), which remains a necessary next full-source read rather than a document this dossier pretends to have completed. [B6,B7]

## 2. Then / now

Then, the settlement was crowded close to the processing works and creek. The indexed nomination description, not a completed full-document read, describes wood transported by flume to supply the kilns. Now, the state park preserves the town as a visitor landscape, while the surviving kilns provide a physical link between mining and the surrounding forest. The nomination's descriptions of occupied houses and occasional mining are observations from its survey period, not a statement that anyone lives there now. [B4; index-sourced, full nomination unread]

The preservation challenge includes more than keeping roofs upright. The engineering firm responsible for remediation describes lead and arsenic impacts from historic milling and smelting and work for Idaho Parks to make the site suitable for public recreation. This is a contractor's direct project account, not a current independent exposure assessment. It supports a strict no-collecting/no-disturbance visitor design; it does not justify declaring every surface safe to handle. [B8]

## 5. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Reopening date unresolved (desk QC check, November 21, 2026):** Idaho Parks lists the townsite closed for the season from October 26, 2026; its current park page publishes interpretive-center hours but no Bayhorse reopening date. Secondary sources describe the standing seasonal pattern — the townsite opens May 1 and closes in late fall as weather dictates, day-use only — and a current Idaho travel guide repeats the October 26, 2026 closure date. The pattern is not an operator-published date for the coming season, so this entry's reflection posture follows the closure until the operator publishes otherwise. [B1, B13, B14]
- An official tourism article says Bayhorse was established as a gold camp around 1864 and abandoned in 1915. The USGS report dates active mining to 1877; a 1976 BLM environmental statement says the Bayhorse mines ceased operation in 1925. These describe different scopes/events and are not reconciled by choosing whichever date sounds best. Keep “early prospecting,” “active mining,” “town abandonment” and “later mining” separate. [B10,B2,B11]
- The nomination's kiln count is historical and approximate. No fresh condition survey or count was performed. [B4; index-sourced, full nomination unread]
- The agency-produced oral-history video *Bayhorse, the early years* has been located, but not transcribed/listened to in this pass. No quotation has been invented from it. The lone prospector/bay-horse name story needs that direct review before being told as fact. [B12]
- No firsthand 1880s resident diary/newspaper scan was examined. Flora's later regional memoir supplies a real voice, but is not a substitute for the missing boom-era voices.
- No verified primary-source decimal coordinate, marker ID or archive ARK was obtained. These fields are blank, not guessed from tourism aggregators. A National Register listing is not permission to enter a building or mine.
