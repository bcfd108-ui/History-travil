# HT-RES-VA-yorktown-battlefield — The mile of road where one army marched out and laid down its arms
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A tobacco-port village on the York River, a ring of earthen redoubts, two siege trenches dug closer and closer in the dark — and, on October 19, 1781, a surrender field where the last major British army in the Revolutionary War grounded its weapons between two lines of its enemies.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a battlefield, not a single building: Yorktown Battlefield, the ground of the 1781 Siege of Yorktown, at Yorktown in York County, Virginia, administered by the National Park Service as the Yorktown unit of Colonial National Historical Park. The NPS's own fees page states the terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: all visitors age 16 and older need an entrance pass — a Standard Pass at $15.00, good for seven days' entrance to Yorktown Battlefield and the NPS areas at Historic Jamestowne, or the Colonial National Historical Park Annual Pass at $45.00; children under 16 need no pass. Passes are sold in person at the Yorktown Battlefield Visitor Center. The NPS's auto-tour page states the battlefield is open from 9:00 a.m. until sunset and open year-round, with possible exceptions for severe weather. The NPS directs visitors to begin at the Yorktown Battlefield Visitor Center, 1000 Colonial Parkway, Yorktown, Virginia 23690. Colonial National Historical Park was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000839, per the NPS's own National Register record).

## 2. The story
By the summer of 1781 the United States had been at war with England for over six years. The NPS's history of the siege sets the board plainly: General Sir Henry Clinton, the British commander in North America, held New York City; General Lord Cornwallis was campaigning through the southern colonies with an army that had already taken Savannah and Charleston; and the main American army under George Washington stood along the Hudson River above New York. In the summer of 1780, 5,500 French troops under the Comte de Rochambeau had landed at Newport, Rhode Island, and in the spring of 1781 Washington met Rochambeau to plan an attack on Clinton in New York, timed to a French fleet.

The fleet went to the Chesapeake instead, and Washington changed the war. To fool Clinton, he had his men build conspicuous camps and huge brick bread ovens within sight of New York and let false papers describing an attack on Clinton fall into British hands. Then, in mid-August, Washington and Rochambeau marched south. They paraded before the Continental Congress in Philadelphia in early September and reached Williamsburg, thirteen miles west of Yorktown, in mid-September.

Cornwallis was at Yorktown on Clinton's orders: during the summer he had been directed to provide a protected harbor for the British fleet in the lower Chesapeake Bay, and he chose Yorktown for its deep-water harbor on the York River, fortifying the town and Gloucester Point across the river through the latter part of the summer. The French fleet under Admiral de Grasse entered the lower Chesapeake at the end of August, landed 3,000 French troops to wait at Williamsburg, and on **September 5, 1781**, fought the British fleet in the naval engagement known as the Battle of the Capes. The British, their ships damaged, returned to New York; the French stayed and blockaded the bay. The naval battle decided the land battle before the siege began: Cornwallis was cut off by sea.

By the end of September, the NPS history records, approximately 17,600 American and French soldiers were gathered at Williamsburg, while 8,300 British soldiers — including a small number of German auxiliary troops — occupied Yorktown. Cornwallis asked Clinton for help, and Clinton answered that a fleet with 5,000 men would sail from New York on October 5.

Cornwallis had his men build a main line of defense around Yorktown: ten small enclosed forts, called redoubts, with artillery batteries and connecting trenches. On **September 28** the Americans and French marched from Williamsburg and began digging a first trench — the first parallel — some 800 yards from the British line. By **October 9** the trench was finished and the allied artillery moved up; firing continuously, the allies had virtually knocked the British guns out of action by October 11. That same period brought Cornwallis word that Clinton's departure from New York had been delayed.

On the night of **October 11** the allies began a second trench, 400 yards from the British works. The new line could not be completed without taking two of the British outer forts, Redoubts 9 and 10. On the night of **October 14**, 400 French troops stormed Redoubt 9 and 400 Americans stormed Redoubt 10, carrying both in less than thirty minutes; the NPS history records that nine Americans and fifteen French died in the assault. With the redoubts in allied hands, the second parallel was finished and allied guns stood at point-blank range of the British inner line.

On **October 16** the British tried twice to escape their position. Early that morning they attacked the allied center in an attempt to silence a French battery; the French cannon were firing again in less than six hours. Late that night Cornwallis tried to evacuate his army across the York River in small boats to Gloucester Point; at midnight a violent windstorm scattered the boats and forced him to abandon the crossing.

On **October 17** Cornwallis sent out a drummer, then an officer under a white flag with a note asking for a cease-fire. Notes passed between the two commanders that day, and on **October 18** four officers — one American, one French, and two British — met at the Moore House, the home of Augustine Moore about a mile outside Yorktown, to settle the surrender terms. On **October 19, 1781**, most of Cornwallis's army marched out of Yorktown between two lines of allied soldiers, Americans on one side and French on the other, stretching for more than a mile, to a field where they laid down their arms. The NPS chronology of the siege adds that the Gloucester Point garrison underwent the same ceremony an hour later, and that the surrender took in one-third of all British forces in North America. That same day, unknown to the men on the field, Clinton finally sailed from New York with 5,000 troops — too late.

The war did not formally end at Yorktown, but its center of gravity did. The NPS history records that the British still held 26,000 troops in North America, yet in March 1782 Parliament resolved that Britain should not continue the war against the United States; provisional articles of peace followed later that year, and in September 1783 the final treaty was signed, ending the war and acknowledging American independence. The ground itself was kept much later: Colonial National Monument was authorized on July 3, 1930 — in time for the sesquicentennial of the victory the following year — and was later redesignated Colonial National Historical Park, with the National Register listing following on October 15, 1966.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a British main line of ten redoubts, batteries, and connecting trenches around Yorktown, faced by a first allied parallel 800 yards out and then a second at 400 yards. **Now:** the same ground kept as a National Military Park unit — the NPS auto tour's Red Route runs six named stops across it: the British Inner Defense Line, the Grand French Battery, the Second Allied Siege Line, Redoubts 9 and 10, the Moore House, and Surrender Field.

**Then:** on October 18, 1781, four officers met in Augustine Moore's private house, a mile outside the town, to draft the terms on which an army would surrender. **Now:** the Moore House stands restored on the battlefield's eastern side, a stop on the driving tour, its surrender room the hinge between the siege lines and the field.

**Then:** on October 19, 1781, a British army marched more than a mile between two lines of allied soldiers and grounded its arms in an open field. **Now:** that field is Surrender Field, the last stop of the Red Route — open grass, kept as the room where the ceremony happened rather than built over with it.

## 4. What's still there
- The battlefield ground itself: the British Inner Defense Line, the Grand French Battery, the Second Allied Siege Line, and Redoubts 9 and 10, all named stops on the NPS's Red Route auto tour
- The Moore House, Augustine Moore's home, where the surrender commissioners met on October 18, 1781 (NPS siege history and auto tour)
- Surrender Field, where the British garrison laid down its arms on October 19, 1781 (NPS chronology and auto tour)
- The Yorktown Battlefield Visitor Center, holding a 15-minute orientation film, "The Siege at Yorktown," museum exhibits on the 1781 siege and the Battle of the Capes, the campaign table used by Cornwallis during the siege, and George Washington's campaign tents (NPS visitor centers page)
- The York River frontage and Gloucester Point across the river — the water Cornwallis needed, de Grasse closed, and the storm of October 16–17 made uncrossable (NPS siege history)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Yorktown Battlefield Visitor Center, 1000 Colonial Parkway:** Start where the NPS directs visitors to start — the entrance pass, the orientation film, and the museum's siege exhibits, including Washington's campaign tents and Cornwallis's campaign table.
2. **On the British line:** Stop A, the British Inner Defense Line — the ring of redoubts, batteries, and trenches Cornwallis's men built through the late summer of 1781.
3. **On the allied lines:** Stop B, the Grand French Battery, and Stop C, the Second Allied Siege Line — the trench begun on the night of October 11, 400 yards from the British works.
4. **At the redoubts:** Stop D, Redoubts 9 and 10, stormed on the night of October 14 by 400 French and 400 Americans and carried in under thirty minutes — the action that let the allies finish the second parallel.
5. **At the Moore House:** Stop E, where the four commissioners met on October 18, 1781, to settle the terms.
6. **At Surrender Field:** Stop F, where on October 19 the garrison marched between the two allied lines and laid down its arms, with the Gloucester garrison's matching ceremony an hour later across the river.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The Moore House's construction date does not agree across the National Park Service's own publications, and the disagreement is printed rather than resolved. The NPS Historical Handbook for Yorktown describes the Moore House as "constructed about 1725." The NPS's Cultural Landscapes Inventory park report for the Moore House (updated 2025) states the house "was built around 1769" and that Augustine Moore purchased the property in 1768. Both NPS dates are carried here as their sources state them; this draft does not reconcile them, and no construction year for the house is printed elsewhere in this entry as settled.

Where the Articles of Capitulation were signed is also carried two ways in period and park sources. Gardner's *Photographic Sketch Book of the War* (1866), in its text for the Moore House plate, states that the commissioners met at the house but that the draft prepared there "was signed in the trenches of Yorktown, over a mile away," as was generally believed. The NPS Historical Handbook's battlefield tour instead places the allied leaders in Redoubt No. 10 five days after its capture, affixing their signatures to articles the British commanders had already signed. The two accounts are printed as they stand; this draft does not choose between the trenches and the redoubt.

The army figures in §2 — approximately 17,600 allied soldiers and 8,300 British — are the NPS siege history's own approximations and are printed as approximations. Strength and casualty totals for Yorktown vary considerably in the wider literature; figures appearing in lead-list sources were not used, and no total beyond the NPS's own (including its redoubt-assault deaths: nine Americans, fifteen French) is asserted here.

The NPS National Register record for Colonial National Historical Park (66000839) carries no latitude or longitude for the Yorktown Battlefield, so the locator below prints none. Coordinates carried in NPGallery for the park as a whole describe a multi-unit park running from Jamestown to Yorktown and are not scoped to this battlefield; coordinates appearing in lead-list sources were not used.

## 7. Photo record
**First photograph:** *Moore House, Yorktown, Virginia* — the albumen plate as bound in Gardner's *Photographic Sketch Book of the War*: the print sits centered on its album page above the printed caption "Moore House, Yorktown, Virginia," plate No. 15, dated May 1862 on the page. Within the print, the weatherboarded house stands at right, its tall brick chimney and broken wall surfaces showing the damage of a second siege of Yorktown eighty-one years after the first; bare trees cross the middle ground, and at the far left a small group of Union soldiers stands by a fence and a gun carriage. No dead and no suffering appear in the frame; it is the surrender house as the Civil War found it. Inspected from the downloaded file before captioning.
