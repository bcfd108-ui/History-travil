# Pharr, Bynum and Chickasaw Village through their distinct records

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP17*

## 2. Story

At Pharr, archaeologist Charles F. Bohannon’s NPS report describes eight earthworks and a distinct area of habitation. He and the investigation team did not excavate all eight. Archaeological comparisons with Bynum came from what they examined, interpreted alongside other work. Their estimate of what could be reconstructed also mattered. For example, the summary explicitly declines to say how many people were buried there. An excavation report is an account of research decisions and observations in its own period. It does not give a complete record of people’s lives or authority to tell their histories for them. [2][5][6]

At Bynum, NPS places initial construction in the Middle Woodland period. The agency reports six original mounds, five excavated in the 1940s, and rebuilding of the two largest. Those distinctions change what a reader imagines surviving today. The present contours carry both an older history and a modern reconstruction history. The historical field report and park interpretation also use classifications for particular evidence. A comparative archaeological label has a much narrower purpose than a name for a living people. [1][5–7]

The place the NPS calls Chickasaw Village has a separate record and different time depth. In an NPS transcript of a recorded interpretive program, Chickasaw Nation anthropologist LaDonna Brown says, “This is our community.” Pauline Brown, of the Chickasaw Language Committee and Chickasaw Nation Historical Society, adds memories and descriptions of shared work, conversation and children playing. These recorded voices belong to Chickasaw speakers and are presented by NPS. The Chickasaw Nation itself publishes a separate account of the work of protecting ancestral burial places and its relationship with NPS. Read the two records side by side to hear what a living community asks of public interpretation. [3][8]

## 3. Then and now

At Pharr, the operator describes the older earthworks within a wider landscape. At Bynum it names individual post-excavation reconstructions. At the village it describes outlines marked on the ground where houses stood. The outlines are interpretive aids. The three places are preserved together in this sweep record for comparison only, with their distinct meanings retained. [1–3][5]
