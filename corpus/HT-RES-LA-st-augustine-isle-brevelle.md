# St. Augustine Catholic Church and Cemetery — Isle Brevelle, Cane River
HT-RES-LA-st-augustine-isle-brevelle · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Isle Brevelle, the long river island community between Cane River Lake and its bayous south of Natchitoches, was built by one family and then by everyone who married into it. Its patriarch was Nicolas Augustin Metoyer, eldest son of Marie Thérèse Coincoin and Claude Thomas Pierre Metoyer, freed by his father in 1792 and granted 395 acres here in the mid-1790s; his brother Louis took the adjoining grant that became Melrose Plantation. By the 1830s the extended Metoyer family — 174 people in the nomination's count — held some 18,000 acres along this river and, the record states plainly, held 276 enslaved people. The surnames of the community today still run through the nomination's list: Antee, Balthazar, Chevalier, Christophe, Conant, Delphin, Dupre, Jones, Lacour, Llorens, Monette, Moran, Rachal, Roque, Sarpy. [1]

Community tradition holds that Augustin raised a first chapel on his plantation in 1803, after a trip to France with his father showed him villages gathered around a church. That date is the community's own telling, kept on the church sign and celebrated at a bicentennial in 2003, and it is labeled here as tradition. The written record begins a generation later. On July 19, 1829, Father Jean Baptiste Blanc blessed the Chapel of St. Augustine as a mission of St. François of Natchitoches, recording that it had been erected on Isle Brevelle on Augustin Metoyer's plantation through his care and generosity, aided by his brother Louis. Four couples were married in the chapel within four days of the blessing and a fifth two days after that. Elizabeth Shown Mills, the Cane River scholar whose work the 2014 nomination follows, found no record of a chapel on the Isle in 1803, that decade, or that quarter-century, and the nomination takes 1829 as the documented founding. Both dates are printed in this dossier because both are real — one as the community's memory, one as the surviving paper. [1, 2, 3, 6]

What is not in dispute is who built it and who paid. St. Augustine was established, funded and built on the land of free people of color, principally for their own worship, with Augustin's own written wish — the words are kept in the sacristy — that others of the Catholic faith have the right to attend. Families rented pews to support the church, with name boxes fixed at the pew ends reserving their places. The nomination is careful with the superlatives that gather around this church, and this dossier follows it: an older claim, from Father J. J. Callahan's 1954 parish history, that this was the oldest parish for people of color in the United States is, in the nomination's words, technically untrue — St. Augustine in New Orleans was a parish by 1841, and Isle Brevelle did not become a parish until 1856. What stands is narrower and stronger: a Catholic chapel established, financed and built by people of part African descent on their own ground, at a time when almost nothing of the kind existed in the South. [1, 3]

Burials gathered behind the chapel from the late 1830s. Augustin's wife Marie Agnes, who died July 7, 1839, is thought among the first buried here; Augustin, who saw his church made a parish by Bishop Auguste Martin's decree of March 11, 1856 and received its first resident pastor, Father François Martin, died that December and shares with her the prominent stuccoed brick tomb directly behind the church. His mother Coincoin's burial place is not recorded — tradition places her in the American Cemetery in Natchitoches, with no marker and no known record, and the nomination leaves it there. In 1836 Augustin sat for the portrait that still watches over the place: nearly life-size, gesturing toward the original chapel. The painting of St. Augustine from the first chapel still hangs in the church, and the bell in the tower is, by the parish's account, the one object from the original church still in daily use, still calling the parish to Mass. The companion painting of St. Louis was lost in the 1960s. The portrait of Augustin himself was nearly lost at the 1970 Melrose auction, where the parish priest, Father Norbert Rosso, told its story to the room, bid $2,000 into silence, and brought it home. [1, 3, 7]

The present church is the third building's heir, raised in a 1916 building campaign and dedicated at a solemn high mass on February 15, 1917: a Romanesque Revival church in white asbestos shingles, its bell tower rising through the front gable to a broach spire, round-arched windows, a barrel-vaulted interior and a choir loft. Around it the parish built its working buildings — a Queen Anne rectory, a Craftsman convent of 1917 for the Sisters of Divine Providence, and the schools. Education on the Isle is older than the parish: the Daughters of the Cross opened St. Joseph's school in 1857 in the house up the road now known as the Badin-Roque House, moved beside the church in 1860, closed through the Civil War years, and reopened in 1890 under the Sisters of Divine Providence with three pupils — seventy-five by year's end, 250 at the school's peak. The 1917 school building was demolished in the early 1970s and the present parish hall rose about 1973 on its ground, so that the gatherings the school once held — weddings, fairs, society meetings — continued in a new room on the same site. The school itself closed in the late 1960s. A 2001 renovation stripped 1970s paneling to expose the church's original beaded board, joined church and rectory with an annex, and was rededicated on February 25, 2001. The church and cemetery were entered in the National Register on September 24, 2014, as a traditional cultural property whose period of significance runs from 1829 to the present — because the significance is that the community never left. [1, 2, 3]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** About fifty-seven acres on the east bank of Cane River Lake along Hwy 484 on Isle Brevelle, Natchez vicinity, Natchitoches Parish — part of Augustin Metoyer's original plantation ground. The registered district holds four contributing buildings (church, rectory, convent, parish hall) and one contributing site, the cemetery; the surrounding acreage remains agricultural, leased today for cattle and hay. [1]

**Lawful physical anchor.** St. Augustine Catholic Church, 2262 Hwy 484, Natchez, LA 71456 — an active parish of the Diocese of Alexandria. The lawful visit is the public one the parish itself offers: Mass on the published schedule, and quiet presence on the church grounds and in the cemetery outside services. There is no admission, no tour program and no museum. The rectory, convent and hall are parish buildings, not visit stops, except as their exteriors read from the churchyard. Cane River Lake in front of the church is part of the setting; the nomination records that boat-wake erosion at the bank in front of the church is severe and has already taken a mature tree, a condition a visitor will see and should not worsen by leaving the grounds' paths. [1, 3, 5]

## 5. Public progression

Come as a guest of a working parish. If the visit can be a Sunday, the 10:30 a.m. Mass is the ground truth of this place — a congregation of the founders' descendants still worshipping in the church their family built — and a visitor's part in it is simply to be present and courteous. Outside services, walk the churchyard slowly: the 1917 church first, then the cemetery behind it, reading the names the nomination lists and the French inscriptions on the older stones, giving the Metoyer tomb its moment without treating any family's grave as an exhibit. End at the lake side of Hwy 484, where the church doubles itself in the water, and notice the eaten bank below the road. **Strongest story moment · The bell.** Of everything the first congregation built, bought and hung in 1829, one object still works: the bell that called Augustin Metoyer's family to Mass calls their descendants now, over the same graves, in the same river bend. Let the visit stay at that scale — quiet, brief, and grateful. [1, 3, 5, 7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Founding date: tradition against record.** The church sign, the parish and the community's 2003 bicentennial keep 1803; the parish's own history page states plainly that the written record does not always agree with oral tradition, and the 2014 nomination, following Mills, finds no record of an Isle chapel before the documented blessing of July 19, 1829, and sets the period of significance from 1829. This dossier prints the 1803 date only as tradition, labeled. [1, 3, 6]
- **"First" and "oldest" claims conflict across sources.** Secondary summaries variously call St. Augustine the first church in Louisiana built by and for free people of color, the oldest surviving Black Catholic church in the United States, and the second oldest of its kind. The nomination itself rejects the oldest-parish claim (New Orleans' St. Augustine was a parish in 1841; this church became a parish in 1856) and warns that firsts are difficult to prove and can obscure the significance. The nomination's supported claim — first Catholic chapel established, funded and built on the land of persons of part African descent in its context — is the one used here. A 2014 congressional support letter in the nomination file claims national-level significance and "first church in the United States established by a man of color"; the nomination as listed certifies local significance as a traditional cultural property. The letter is advocacy, and the listing governs. [1]
- **Louis Metoyer as chapel architect.** Tradition names Louis as the chapel's designer and builder; the nomination notes no primary source for this and suggests the story may derive from Francois Mignon's Melrose writings. The parish history repeats the tradition. It is printed here as tradition only. [1, 3]
- **Lost predecessor churches.** Popular summaries state that Union forces burned the first church during the Red River Campaign in May 1864 and that a second church burned in the early 1900s. Neither fire appears in the sections of the 2014 nomination inspected for this dossier, which says only that the 1916 campaign replaced the older buildings on the property. The burnings are therefore held as unverified accounts pending Father Callahan's 1954 parish history and parish records, not stated as fact. [1]
- **Church completion year.** The nomination dates the present church to a 1916 building campaign; the parish history gives dedication on February 15, 1917; Northwestern State's folklife account says the present church was built in 1917. This dossier prints 1916–1917. [1, 3, 6]
- **Pew seating order.** Summaries of the nomination report that surviving pew records placed the Metoyer family in the front seats with white planter families behind them. The parish history verifies the pew-rental system and its name boxes; the seating-order detail was not located in the nomination text inspected in this pass and is not stated as fact here. [3]
- **Mass and office schedule currency.** The schedule in the trip gate comes from parish bulletins posted on the parish website dated October 2023 through April 2024 — the most recent the site exposed to this pass — not from a November 2026 bulletin. Weekday Mass times differ between bulletin issues (Tuesday–Wednesday evenings, Thursday–Friday mornings in the later issues). Confirm before travel. The parish website's homepage names the current pastor; no personnel claim beyond that is made. [3, 5]
- **Coincoin's grave.** No record of Marie Thérèse Coincoin's death or burial is known; a marriage contract places her death by 1817, some scholars question that reading, and her burial in Natchitoches' American Cemetery is generational tradition without a marker or record. Nothing at St. Augustine marks her grave, and none should be expected. [1]
