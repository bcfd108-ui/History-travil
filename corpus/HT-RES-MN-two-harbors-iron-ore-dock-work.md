Drive to the signed public car parking and use the shore path only where open and practicable. The operator gives no winter pedestrian service schedule. Skip for posted closures, unsafe ice or wave exposure. Commercial rail, dock, ramp-working areas and breakwater are outside this itinerary. [1]

## 2 · Story — The railroad had to meet the deck

Ore arrived in a railroad car and went away inside a hull. The distance between them was the work of the dock. The state’s Lake Superior shipwrecks nomination describes how elevated tracks fed bins called pockets. A ship alongside could take ore through chutes placed over deck openings. An early dock made lumber, fastenings, railroad grade and the height of a freighter work together. [2]

The drafts of the Federal Writers’ Project preserved at MNHS name “Perk” Dodge as conductor and Thomas Owens as engineer of the first ore train in a passage written long after the event. The same passage describes the original dock as 552 feet long and reports 46 pockets. Those dimensions and names belong to this retrospective government text, pending a contemporary ledger or railroad roster. It adds that families followed construction laborers into rough temporary housing. By the 1930s the compilers described a town tied to the shops and docks for livelihoods. [3]

The 1927 Forest Service photograph’s original caption, reproduced with the scan, describes the return leg: “Boats coming in for a load of iron bring coal.” In the frame a line of rail cars, tracks and industrial equipment are as prominent as the water. It is a photograph of that working harbor, with no evidence here that the photographer stood on ground open today. [4–5]

## 3 · Then / now

**Then:** early wooden gravity docks preceded the 1909 steel Dock No. 6. The federal engineering survey calls No. 6 the first steel ore dock on the Great Lakes. **Now:** DNR advertises sightlines to working loading docks from a separate public landing. Cleveland-Cliffs’ 2022 technical report describes operating docks numbered 1 and 2, including a conveyor feed at No. 2 and bottom-discharge rail cars feeding pockets. Those operational numbers belong to that report and should not be assigned to an unverified structure in the 1987 view. [1–2,6–8]

## 4 · Surviving features and access

- **Public exterior:** DNR passenger-car parking and the signed waterfront walking path. The DNR record describes a launch facility and shore trail. The route stays off working ramps and boarding docks. [1]
- **View from public ground:** loading structures across the commercial harbor. The steel framework and separation between water and rail level explain the gravity arrangement even when no ship is loading. [1,6–8]
- **Not accessible on this visit:** tracks, ore pockets, conveyors, active dock parcels and the federal breakwater. A ship sighting is incidental. [1,8]

## 5 · Trip progression

Drive to the posted Two Harbors Protected Access passenger-car area. From the open land-side path take a short look across the harbor. Compare the visible top of a dock with the expected height of a vessel’s deck. **Story landing — The space between a railcar and a ship.** Read the 1927 caption alongside the nomination’s explanation of the gravity pockets. If the public shore path is icy or closed, use the archival frame remotely. [1–8]

## 6 · Gaps and conflicts

The shipwrecks nomination dates initial loading of the *Hecla* and *Ironton* to August 1, 1884. The Writers’ Project draft says August 18–19 for loading (spelling *Heckla*) and elsewhere gives August 19 as the first shipment’s departure. Loading and departure can differ, but the two printed loading dates still disagree. A contemporary manifest or dispatch log was not obtained. The HAER notes confirm the archival steel Dock No. 6. Its exact present-day surviving fabric from the public trail remains unverified. [2–3,6–7]

- **Adoption candidate for Dash:** Library of Congress, Historic American Engineering Record, HAER MN-33-1, Jet Lowe, November 5, 1987, digital 091418p. Federal survey, LOC rights advisory says no known restrictions on U.S. government images. The inspected frame shows water in the foreground, three aligned dock forms and a ship at center. The index gives the source direction as from the southwest, but an intercept at the modern public path is unproven. [6–7]
- **Candidate pending original catalog read:** National Archives, NAID 2127490, “Photograph of Loading Docks at Two Harbors, Minnesota,” U.S. Forest Service series, July 25, 1927, photographer unprinted. The accessioned scan was inspected via the NARA bot mirror, which classifies the federal work as public domain. Its original catalog URL was inaccessible to the web reader. The image shows track, cars and material-handling structures. No verified modern pair or Photo Point for either frame. [4–5]
