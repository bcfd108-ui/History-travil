## 2. The story
**Hook:** A railway connection becomes a town only when people can build a life around it.

**Named railroad-household evidence:** a grandson's essay, *The Life and Death of a Railroad Engineer*, appears in the San Jacinto Model Railroad Club's December 2020 *Derail*, pages 2–3, reprinting a March 2012 item. The printed byline reads **Richard Sutterlund**, while the family is called **Setterlund** in the text. Richard identifies **Charles Setterlund** as his grandfather and recounts his Great Northern employment in Minot, marriage to **Hilda Westlund** there in 1903, and their son **Sydney's** Minot birth in 1907. Those early events are inherited family history, not events the grandson witnessed. His personally remembered household life with Charles occurred later in **Grand Forks**, not Minot. This distinction is essential. [5]

**First-person Minot service-household voice:** **Henry Eiichi Suto**, interviewed by Janice Tanaka on June 17, 2008, remembers his father's US Café and partner Mr. Toyama. The JANM/Discover Nikkei published transcript locates it near Minot's railroad station and describes service to train travelers. Suto partly signals received information rather than direct observation. His emphasis is the household's effort: **“And I know they worked very, very hard”**. This is a restaurant-family account, not evidence of railroad employment. Transcript read; video/audio not watched or heard. [6]

**Original interpretation:** Put these two kinds of work beside one another without merging them. The railway employer supplied one family's occupation. Travelers supplied another family's customers. The human history lies in that distinction: the train did not perform all the labor that made a railway town work. A small room for public reading can make those relationships clearer than an impressive locomotive viewed without names.

## 3. Then / now
Then: the family essay connects employment, migration and household formation; the interview connects passenger movement to service labor. Their memories have different distances from the events. [5,6]

Now: these accounts are available as published evidence, not as a re-created household. The library offers a lawful place to study local history. Neither it nor the museum is demonstrated to exhibit either family. The present museum has an independently published visitor policy, but its winter schedule is not closed. [1–4]

Do not describe Minot as empty land before railways, claim comprehensive community representation, or infer that one immigrant family's experience stands for everyone. Indigenous history is not supplied by these two sources; a broader settlement narrative would require appropriate Tribal-published evidence.
