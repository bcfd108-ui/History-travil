# HT-RES-VA-mount-vernon — The house that grew in three campaigns, worked by a community that could not leave it
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A farmhouse on the Potomac that its owner rebuilt twice into a mansion, an estate of five farms and some 8,000 acres run by 317 enslaved people in 1799, and the house a women's association bought in 1858 because neither the nation nor Virginia would.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is an estate, not a single room: George Washington's Mount Vernon, the mansion, outbuildings, gardens, tomb, and grounds on the Potomac River in Fairfax County, Virginia. The operator is the Mount Vernon Ladies' Association, which owns and runs the site. The Association's own pages state the current terms, and they are printed here as those pages carry them: general admission is $30 for adults ages 12 and up, $16 for youth ages 6–11, and free for children ages 0–5, with members receiving free daytime admission. Mount Vernon is open daily from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. April through October and 9 a.m. to 4 p.m. November through March. Entry to the Mansion is by guided tour only; the Association recommends reserving a tour time in advance online, and a $2 per-person fee applies to an online mansion-tour reservation, while tour tickets reserved at the gate are free. The address the Association publishes is 3200 Mount Vernon Memorial Highway, Mount Vernon, Virginia 22121. The estate was designated a National Historic Landmark on December 19, 1960, and listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000833, per the NPS National Register record), the Virginia Landmarks Register listing (VLR 029-0054) is dated September 9, 1969 by the Virginia Department of Historic Resources — after, not before, the federal listing — and both dates are printed as their records carry them.

## 2. The story
The Washington family obtained the land in 1674, according to Fairfax County's Inventory of Historic Sites, and the core of the present house was built about **1734** by George Washington's father, Augustine Washington, at what was then called Little Hunting Creek plantation. Mount Vernon's own architectural account adds the physical evidence: dendrochronology — tree-ring dating carried out for the Association — shows the trees used to frame that first section were cut in 1734, and the original house likely held four rooms and a central passage on the first floor with a garret above. George's older half-brother Lawrence later renamed the estate Mount Vernon, after Admiral Edward Vernon, under whom he had served. After Lawrence's death the estate passed under his will; his widow began leasing it to George Washington in late **1754**, and Washington took possession that year of a roughly 3,000-acre estate and a house of about 3,500 square feet.

Washington then rebuilt the house twice, over more than four decades, and the Association's account is explicit that he played the central design role himself — working from pattern books, other buildings, and the advice of artisans, in a colony with few professional architects. The first expansion began in the late 1750s: Washington directed hired and enslaved workers to raise the mansion from one-and-one-half to two-and-one-half stories, rearranged the interior, reconfigured the main staircase, and upgraded the public rooms. He was absent for most of that work, serving in the French and Indian War, and supervised by letter through a local builder; the Association's expansion account states plainly that the majority of the work was done by Washington's enslaved labor force.

The second expansion began in **1774**, planned from 1773. A two-story addition went up at the south end first, then a large two-story room at the north; the double-height piazza was carried along the whole east façade, and open quadrant colonnades linked the house to two dependencies, making a five-part Palladian composition. The cupola, the Palladian window, and the Tuscan piers of the piazza came out of English pattern books — Batty Langley's and Abraham Swan's among them — that Washington owned and used. The only known surviving elevation drawing of the mansion, a draft of the west façade and cellar plan prepared for the 1774 expansion, is in Washington's own hand. By his death the estate had grown to about 7,600 acres on the Association's expansion account (other Association pages round the plantation to 8,000 acres across five farms), and the mansion to almost 11,000 square feet. The Virginia Department of Historic Resources summarizes the arc: through alterations and remodelings completed by **1787**, Washington transformed a simple farmhouse built by his father into the mansion that stands today.

The house was a home at the center of a working plantation, and the plantation ran on enslaved labor. That is the estate's second story, and the Association's own research carries it in numbers. In **1799**, the year Washington died at Mount Vernon in December, **317 enslaved men, women, and children** lived and worked across the five farms, the gristmill, and the distillery. Of those 317, the Association's account of Washington's will breaks the total into its legal parts: **123 people were owned by George Washington himself**; **153 were dower property** of the Custis estate — the estate of Martha Washington's first husband, Daniel Parke Custis — whom neither George nor Martha could legally free; and **41 were rented** from neighboring plantations. Over the whole of Washington's residence from 1754 to 1799, the Association puts the total at at least 577 enslaved people who lived and worked at Mount Vernon. About a quarter of the 1799 community worked at Mansion House Farm itself as skilled laborers — blacksmiths, carpenters, spinners, seamstresses, cooks, and household staff — while most lived and worked on the four outlying farms.

Washington's will, written in July 1799, directed that the enslaved people he owned be emancipated after Martha Washington's death, with one exception made immediate: his valet William Lee was freed at once. The will also stipulated, as Virginia law required, that elderly enslaved people and those too sick to work be supported by his estate for life. In December 1800 Martha Washington signed a deed of manumission, recorded in the Fairfax County court records, and Washington's 123 people received their freedom early, on **January 1, 1801**. The dower community had no such provision. When Martha died in 1802, the roughly 150 Custis-estate people were divided among her four grandchildren — a division that split families the 1801 manumission had already split once. The Association presents this history as central to the estate it interprets, in its slavery exhibit and in a dedicated guided tour of the enslaved community, both carried on its current visitor pages.

After Martha's death the estate was divided among Washington heirs and declined. John Augustine Washington III, the last Washington owner, could not maintain it. The rescue came from outside the family and outside government: Ann Pamela Cunningham founded the **Mount Vernon Ladies' Association in 1853**, after her mother, passing the house by boat, saw its decay and asked why the women of the country did not keep it in repair. The Virginia Department of Historic Resources records that in **1858** some 200 acres of the original plantation were acquired by the Association; the purchase price, carried by the American Battlefield Trust's history of the estate, was $200,000 for the mansion and about 200 acres. The Association took possession and opened the house to visitors in 1860, and it owns and operates Mount Vernon still — the first national historic preservation organization in the United States, on the Association's own account. The National Historic Landmark designation followed on December 19, 1960, and the National Register listing on October 15, 1966.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a four-room farmhouse with a garret, framed from trees cut in 1734, at the center of a 3,000-acre plantation Washington began leasing in 1754. **Now:** an almost 11,000-square-foot mansion — the same core, raised and winged twice by its owner — standing at the center of the roughly 500-acre estate the Association preserves.

**Then:** five farms and some 8,000 acres worked in 1799 by 317 enslaved people, 123 of them Washington's, 153 held by the Custis estate, 41 rented. **Now:** the mansion house farm's outbuildings, quarters, gardens, gristmill, and distillery are interpreted with that community named at the center of the telling — a slavery exhibit, an enslaved-people tour, and a slave memorial and African American burial ground on the grounds, all on the Association's current pages.

**Then:** a decaying house its last Washington owner could not keep up, refused by both the federal government and Virginia as a purchase. **Now:** the same house held since 1858–1860 by the Mount Vernon Ladies' Association, restored toward its 1799 appearance — the last year of Washington's life — on the Association's stated preservation standard.

## 4. What's still there
- The Mansion itself: the 1734 core raised in the late 1750s and winged from 1774, with the east piazza, quadrant colonnades, dependencies, and cupola of Washington's second expansion (Mount Vernon Ladies' Association, architecture and expansion accounts)
- The outbuildings of Mansion House Farm — kitchen, servants' hall, workshops, stables, and enslaved people's quarters — where the Association's pages place the skilled work of the estate
- Washington's tomb, holding George and Martha Washington and, per the Association's visitor pages, 28 other family members
- The Slave Memorial and African American burial ground, marking the burial ground used by the enslaved and free African American community (Association visitor pages)
- The four gardens, the bowling green, and the Potomac-facing grounds Washington redesigned along Picturesque lines (Association architecture account)
- The reconstructed gristmill and distillery, open weekends April through October, with admission included in the grounds pass (Association visitor pages)
- The museum and education center exhibits, including the slavery exhibit drawn from the Association's *Lives Bound Together* research (Association visitor pages)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the gate, 3200 Mount Vernon Memorial Highway:** Start on the Association's terms — grounds pass as its pages price it, mansion entry by guided tour only, tour time reserved ahead if the day is tight.
2. **At the Mansion's west front:** Read the house as Washington left it drawn in his own hand — the west elevation he drafted for the 1774 expansion — then walk the colonnades to the dependencies his pattern books supplied.
3. **On the east piazza:** The double-height portico of the second expansion, facing the Potomac — the face in this entry's HABS photograph (§7) — built, like the raising before it, mostly by enslaved workers.
4. **In the outbuildings and quarters:** The working farm inside the showpiece — kitchen, workshops, and the quarters of the community the Association's enslaved-people tour follows through the day.
5. **At the tomb and the Slave Memorial:** Two burial grounds a short walk apart — Washington's tomb, and the memorial on the ground where the enslaved and free African American community buried its dead.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The estate's size in Washington's last year is carried two ways by the Association's own pages, and both are printed. The expansion account gives about **7,600 acres** by Washington's death; the Association's slavery and tour pages describe the five farms as covering **8,000 acres**. This draft does not reconcile them; the difference may be rounding, but the sources consulted do not say so.

The construction dating has a settled layer and an unsettled one, and they are kept separate here. The Association's architecture account states that dendrochronological investigation (Daniel Miles, Oxford Dendrochronology Laboratory, 2007) confirmed the dates of the three main construction phases, and that those findings contradict an earlier interpretation under which the superstructure of the 1735 house had been removed and enlarged during Lawrence Washington's tenure. The earlier interpretation is therefore printed as superseded, not as a live dispute — but a reader meeting the older Lawrence-phase account in print should know the Association's current position rests on the tree-ring work. The same account dates the first house's framing to trees cut in 1734, while the superseded interpretation spoke of a 1735 house; the year carried in §2 is 1734, on the dendrochronology as the Association reports it.

The enslaved-population figures also carry a small internal variance in secondary retellings: some accounts give 124 people owned by Washington where the Association's own will research gives **123**, and 40 rented where the Association gives **41**. The Association's figures are used here, because 123 + 153 + 41 sums to its own stated total of 317, and the secondary variants do not.

The photograph in §7 is captioned "West front of Mount Vernon" on a secondary page that reproduces it. The frame itself shows the mansion's long two-story piazza, which the Association's architecture account places on the **east** façade; the west front carries the pediment and the cour d'honneur, not the piazza. The caption in §7 follows the building in the frame, and the secondary page's caption is printed here as an error not adopted.

The NPS National Register record for Mount Vernon (66000833) carries no latitude or longitude, so the locator below prints none — coordinates are not taken from lead-list or Wikidata sources, which do carry a point for the estate.
