# Lecompton Constitution Hall — the land office and the ballot

Kansas OP04 · Draft research for Dash · November 19, 2026 Pacific

## 2. The story

### What happened downstairs

According to the state operator, Douglas County sheriff Samuel Jones commissioned the frame building in 1856. The United States land office opened on the first floor in January 1857. Applicants coming to make claims shared the room with people seeking land for investment. The operator also documents the building’s court and polling uses. The first-floor location gives a traveler one ordinary working scale for territorial government: a counter, documentary claims and a decision about who could gain title. Upstairs, a meeting room was used for a much larger claim about who would make the rules. [3,4]

### What could be written on the ballot

The convention’s official text is printed in a federal serial set. It directs the election judges to keep two poll books and prescribes the two endorsements, “constitution with slavery” and “constitution with no slavery.” Its schedule directed that a no-slavery result remove the slavery article while leaving existing property claims in enslaved people protected. December electors were offered those endorsements without a full rejection option. This is what the surviving text says, and is a reason to read a ballot instruction and its consequence together. [5]

The federal packet also includes a return giving 6,226 for the constitution with slavery and 569 for the no-slavery alternative, a total of 6,795. A separate printed report cataloged by Kansas Memory, attributed to territorial legislative leaders G. W. Deitzler and C. W. Babcock, gives a majority of 5,574 and identifies 3,012 votes from places the authors challenged. Those numbers are attributed to their reports and their different accounting. The Kansas Historical Society describes a different question in the January 1858 election, which returned a decisive rejection, and an August referendum rejecting the congressional compromise. A change of ballot, participants and reporting makes these votes different events. [3,5,6]

The present museum brings the land office and the meeting room together. Keep the story’s focus on what official forms authorized and on the people who could be held as property under the proposal. A visitor can do the complete witness reading on the first floor. [1,3,5]
