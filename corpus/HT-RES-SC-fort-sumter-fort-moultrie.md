# Fort Sumter and Fort Moultrie

**1. Trip gate — Checked November 21, 2026 Pacific. Fort Sumter Visitor Center at Liberty Square, 340 Concord Street, Charleston, is listed as free, daily 9 a.m.–4:30 p.m. Use the signed public street approach and indoor exhibits only. The city garage at 24 Calhoun Street charges separately. Check NPS alerts and holiday hours before leaving. The ferry, wharf, shoreline and both forts are outside this reflection visit. [1–4]**

## 2. Story

In December 1860, Major Robert Anderson moved the federal garrison from Fort Moultrie to Fort Sumter. U.S. engineer John G. Foster reported on December 27 that he remained briefly at Moultrie with a guard and was told to disable equipment before departing. He described going into Charleston the following day on financial business for the engineering work. At the moment secession had become an armed crisis, the records still contain ordinary obligations: wages, equipment and men to move. [5]

The NPS exhibit puts slavery and secession at the center of the conflict. The April dispatches show how that national crisis looked from inside one fort. Anderson reported burned quarters, falling provisions and a magazine inaccessible because of fire and heat. From the steamship Baltic on April 18 he wrote, “Having defended Fort Sumter for thirty four hours,” before explaining why he accepted evacuation terms. The words state the commander’s accounting of events. They do not by themselves give the whole human cost of the outbreak of war. [3, 5, 10–12]

Four years later a photograph recorded the flag ceremony inside the same fort. Read the crowd in that frame alongside the official dispatch. A military work, a place under bombardment and a place chosen to stage public memory are all visible in records connected to the same walls. [5, 6]

## 3. Then and now

The historic events unfolded at two harbor fortifications. The modern public anchor is an interpretive building on a different parcel in downtown Charleston. The park’s published Liberty Square exhibit text discusses two flags and identifies the fragile garrison flag with the visitor center at the time described. Treat its presence as a point to confirm on site. The separately displayed storm flag and the original walls are part of the wider park context. [1, 3, 7]

## 4. What survives and where a visitor may stand

- Fort Sumter Visitor Center: staffed public indoor exhibit, from the land-side public approach. [1–4]
- Fort Sumter and Fort Moultrie: documentary and reflection ground for this entry. The invitation ends indoors, before the boarding area or any shore walk.
- The 1865 photograph depicts the historic fort interior. Its view cannot be used as a modern location pin for the visitor center. [6]

## 5. Strongest visit moment

At the exhibit on the causes of the war, read the first line of Anderson’s dispatch, then spend a minute with the 1865 photograph. The strongest moment is looking at **the people gathered under the flagpole after the fighting** and realizing that the ceremony and the evacuation account remember the same ground differently. The reading fits entirely within the indoor visit. [3, 5–6]

## 6. Evidence limits and disagreements

The National Archives’ scan of the received April 18 telegram shows mounted message strips and reads “Sunday afternoon the fourteenth inst.” The 1880 Official Records transcription, printed page 12, also says Sunday. Anderson’s April 19 letter separately dates the bombardment April 12–13 and withdrawal April 14. The LOC chronology and National Archives summary place surrender on April 13. The surrender and physical evacuation were separate events. The unexamined sending-side draft, if one survives, is distinct from the received copy inspected here. The current garrison flag display has yet to verify. [5, 7–8, 10–12]

## 7. Historic photograph

Adopted for the research draft. Library of Congress item 2018671038, reproduction LC-DIG-cwpb-02478, original negative’s left half. Opened the catalog rights advisory and inspected the delivered pixels. The frame shows the ceremony crowd behind benches, the flagpole and decorated platform, with spectators above the damaged interior. It does not depict the 1861 bombardment or the modern visitor center. The 1865 Civil War photograph is public-domain material, with no known publication restrictions in the item record. Photographer uncredited in the inspected record. [6, 9]
