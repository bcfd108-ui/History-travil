## 2. The story

The useful starting contrast is a museum inside a timber family's house and a community whose work extended far beyond the people named on company signs. The Polson mansion dates to 1924; its Railroad Camp exhibit building was constructed in 2008–09 to evoke an earlier locomotive shop, not moved intact from the woods. [1] The interpretation should keep both households and machines in frame.

**Named primary voice:** in Jill Freidberg's May 2025 interview, longtime local journalist John Hughes recalled growing up among union organizers: **“I grew up in this household steeped with folk music and unionism.”** His childhood memory puts organized labor at the kitchen table, not only at a strike line. Fellow journalist Doug Barker recalled arriving during financial distress before the spotted owl became the dominant public story. These are published retrospective interview excerpts, not contemporary mill records. Text was read; embedded recordings were not played. [2]

A photograph provides a second doorway into labor. UW catalogs Clark Kinsey's circa-1920 photograph of the National Lumber and Manufacturing Company's mess-hall crew in Grays Harbor County, identifying cooks and waitresses among its subjects. The people are not individually named in the retrieved description. Feeding a camp belongs in the timber story as surely as hauling its logs. That last statement is this dossier's interpretive emphasis, not an identification of an unseen person's job. [3]

Historian Aaron Goings's UW-hosted research connects the region's lumber work with organizing by mill hands, longshore workers, sailors and camp waitresses. His account of the 1912 strike includes women, children and multilingual community gatherings. This is scholarly synthesis, not a unanimous community voice; its quoted oral-history witnesses have not been independently heard here. [4]

The strongest story is therefore not that every household agreed. It is that timber wages, risk and political conflict entered ordinary family life. A visit should ask whose work becomes visible in an engine display and whose does not.

## 3. Then / now

- **Then:** the historic Railroad Camp supported Polson's logging operation north of Hoquiam. **Now:** the museum's shop-style exhibit is a later reconstruction of an industrial setting, not the original camp or a working-mill tour. [1,5]
- **Then:** a mess-hall crew served National Lumber, not automatically Polson. UW says National operated 1920–27 and was sold to Polson in 1927. Keep the companies and photograph dates distinct. [3]
- **Then:** labor conflict filled public streets. **Now:** a credited strike-parade photograph can help explain community participation, but no matched modern street viewpoint or surviving assembly-place entrance is established. [4]

This is thematic then/now interpretation, not a verified photographic pair. No vanished camp, mill foundation or industrial property is a suggested stop.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Suggested editorial sequence only, not an activated route or timed tour:

1. **Begin at museum admission.** Establish which rooms and industrial exhibits are open and what the visit permits. If mobility needs cannot be met, do not improvise an alternate entrance.
2. **Read the house as evidence.** Consider the distinction between a surviving owner's residence and the worker households not preserved on the same scale. This is a question to investigate, not a claim about a particular domestic servant.
3. **Move to the accessible industrial display, if open.** Choose one labeled object. Ask what labor surrounded its use, without turning the stop into an operating demonstration.
4. **Bring the camp kitchen into the picture.** Read the archival mess-hall record alongside the industrial object. Do not claim the photograph shows this museum site or people employed by the same company at that moment. [3]
5. **End with Hughes's household memory.** In a permitted quiet stopping place, read the short quotation in §2. The final thought is how a wage dispute can become family culture, and how the loss of work can unsettle more than a balance sheet.

If the museum is closed, keep the archive reading and defer the visit. Do not substitute an unscheduled mill drive or private logging road.
