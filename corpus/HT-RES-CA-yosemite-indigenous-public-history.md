## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A museum can display Native history while leaving Native people to correct its narration. Listen to the person who decided she could no longer remain silent.

**Named firsthand voice:** In the NEA's published interview accompanying her 2007 National Heritage Fellowship, **Julia Parker** identifies herself as Coast Miwok and Kashia Pomo. She recalls being asked to work at Yosemite's museum, initially doubting her ability to speak publicly, then hearing a ranger say something she knew was wrong. Her response was to begin answering visitors herself. The short read-aloud is: **“And so I began to talk a little bit.”** This is Parker's recollection in a directly read institutional transcript, not an audio clip heard here. Her individual family and learning relationships do not make her a spokesperson for every Yosemite-associated Nation. [4]

**Tribal-authored account:** The **Southern Sierra Miwuk Nation's own website**, last marked June 19, 2026, describes its ancestors' good-faith treaty signing during the Gold Rush, the treaties remaining unratified, resulting landlessness, and its federal-acknowledgment petition begun in 1982. Its update describes an ongoing acknowledgment process. This is the Nation's published historical and political account, not a claim that this dossier has adjudicated recognition or checked a final federal decision. [5]

**Keep the voices distinct:** NPS's Tribal Resources page identifies seven traditionally associated communities: Bishop Paiute Tribe; Bridgeport Indian Colony; Mono Lake Kootzaduka’a Tribe of California and Nevada; North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians of California; Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians; Southern Sierra Miwuk Nation; and Tuolumne Band of Me-Wuk Indians. It reproduces Nation-specific contributions from *Voices of the People* (2019), rather than describing one interchangeable Yosemite tribe. That collaboration is a useful next reading, not permission to speak for them. [6]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Put Parker's decision to speak beside a Nation's decision to publish its own continuing history. The strongest story is not that Native culture survived as museum objects; it is that people retain the authority to challenge the frame around those objects. Loss and coercion are reflection subjects, never game mechanics.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Suggested reading-and-museum sequence, not an outdoor route or booked program:
1. **Before any journey:** check the current operator gate and conditions. If travel is uncertain, complete the reading at home instead.
2. **Begin with identity:** read the named speaker and her own affiliation before the museum interpretation. Avoid introducing her merely as a generic Native guide.
3. **Inside the open public museum only:** notice whether any publicly displayed account identifies its author, narrator or community. If it does not, record that absence without supplying an attribution yourself. No particular label is guaranteed.
4. **Pair the accounts:** read the Southern Sierra Miwuk Nation's institutional statement alongside the museum's framing. Ask what each source is positioned to know, and what neither establishes.
5. **Strongest ending:** return to Parker's decision to answer visitors. Sit with the difference between being represented and speaking. End quietly, without a stamp, score or challenge at loss ground.

Optional question for an ordinary staffed public encounter, if available: “Which published community-authored resources would you recommend alongside these exhibits?” No outreach has been made and no demonstration is requested or assumed.
