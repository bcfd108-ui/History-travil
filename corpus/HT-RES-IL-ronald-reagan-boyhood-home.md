# Ronald Reagan Boyhood Home — a rented house on Hennepin Avenue, Dixon
HT-RES-IL-ronald-reagan-boyhood-home · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Ronald Wilson Reagan was born February 6, 1911, in Tampico, Illinois, and his family moved through a succession of Illinois towns before settling in Dixon in 1920 — the place, the 2001 House report on the boyhood-home legislation states, that Reagan considered his hometown. The Reagan Library's own residences list, a list Reagan himself edited in an earlier version, gives the sequence plainly and gives this house its exact place in it: December 1920–1924, 816 S. Hennepin Ave. (now also Reagan Way), Dixon — a rented house. The family did not own it. The Library list carries the Reagans onward in Dixon after this house — 338 W. Everett Street from 1924 into 1930, then Lincoln Way and Galena Avenue addresses in the early 1930s — which matters because later retellings, including the House report itself, compress all of Reagan's Dixon youth into this one address. This dossier does not. The Hennepin house is the first Dixon home, the early-1920s home, and the only Dixon residence preserved as a museum. [2, 3]

The house itself predates the Reagans by a generation. The National Register record lists it at 816 S. Hennepin Ave., Lee County, with significant years 1920 and 1923, period of significance 1900–1924, area of significance Politics/Government, criterion PERSON, and listing date March 26, 1982 (NRIS 82002580). The nomination account carried in the wider record dates construction to 1891, names William C. and Susan Thompson as the original owners, and records a sale in 1917, before the Reagans rented it. Rent figures conflict across the accounts checked this run — $15 a month in one tour tradition, $23 a month in another, with different landlords named — and the dossier prints that conflict in Gaps rather than choosing a figure. What the sources agree on is the economic shape of the tenancy: a shoe-salesman's family renting a modest two-story frame house on a middle-class street, moving when rent and work required it. Reagan's own autobiography, excerpted by his publisher, opens its Dixon chapters on the Depression town he left in 1932 — closed cement plant, boarded shops, men clustered outside them — and on the lifeguard summers that financed his leaving. The house is where that story's Dixon years began, not where they ended. [1, 4, 5]

The Dixon years outside the house are part of this ground's record and the operator's tour takes them up. From 1926 Reagan worked summers as a lifeguard at Lowell Park on the Rock River, credited — in the House report, the Reagan Library timeline, and his own telling — with saving seventy-seven lives over seven summers, notching each rescue on a log on the shore in the congressional tribute's retelling. He graduated from Dixon High School in June 1928, where the Library timeline records basketball, drama, football and track and service as student body president, and attended Eureka College from fall 1928 to June 1932. Those are Dixon facts, but they are not all Hennepin Avenue facts: by the high-school and lifeguard years the family lived on Everett Street, on the Library's list. A careful visit holds both truths — the museum house is the documented 1920–1924 home, and the wider Dixon of the later 1920s is the town around it. [2, 3, 6]

Preservation was local before it was anything else. In August 1980 a group of Dixon residents led by Lynn Knights purchased the house — by then divided into two apartments in the tour account — and organized as the Reagan Home Preservation and Restoration Committee, later the Ronald Reagan Boyhood Home Foundation. The House report records that the foundation restored and refurnished the house in 1984 to appear as it did during the period Reagan lived there, that the complex then occupied about three-quarters of a city block with three other structures and a parking lot, drew about 20,000 visitors a year, and sat within a historic district created by the City of Dixon. Reagan himself visited in February 1984 for his 73rd birthday observance in the wider accounts. The foundation made a point, in the record, of taking no state or federal funding. [2, 5]

Federal designation was authorized and never completed — and that distinction is this dossier's most important correction to casual descriptions of the site, including the sweep list's "NPS-affiliated" shorthand. Public Law 107-137, signed February 6, 2002, authorized the Secretary of the Interior to acquire the property and establish the Ronald Reagan Boyhood Home National Historic Site under the National Park Service, with the statute specifying that the site would not be so designated until after the Department acquired the property. The Department never acquired it. The Reagan Library's residences page states that the house "became a National Historic Landmark in 2002" — no National Historic Landmark listing for this property was retrieved in the Register record checked this run, which shows a 1982 National Register listing only, and the dossier prints that conflict in full in Gaps. In 2018, on the wider record, the foundation's finances forced it to offer the home for sale to the National Park Service; instead, in December 2020 the foundation gifted the home to Young America's Foundation, which has operated it since, as it preserves Reagan's Rancho del Cielo in California. The operator today is YAF, not the National Park Service, and no NPS unit exists at this address. [1, 2, 3, 5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** 816 S. Hennepin Ave., Dixon, Lee County — a rented family house occupied by Jack, Nelle, Neil and Ronald Reagan from December 1920 into 1924 on the Reagan Library's list (the Register's significant years are 1920 and 1923). The street now also carries the Reagan Way designation. Dixon itself, on the Rock River in northwestern Illinois, is the historic ground in the wider sense: Lowell Park, Dixon High School and the later Dixon addresses are separate places in the same town, not part of this parcel. [1, 3]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The Ronald Reagan Boyhood Home complex operated by Young America's Foundation: Visitor Center and gift shop at 810 S. Hennepin Ave., Dixon, IL 61021, with the Boyhood Home at 816, a garage, and a park with a bronze statue of Reagan, on the operator and House-report parcel descriptions. Begin at the Visitor Center; house entry is by guided tour only, on the hour in season. The operator page checked this run gives phone as (872) USA-1776 and email reaganboyhoodhome@yaf.org; older local listings give (815) 288-5176 — both are printed, in Gaps, and a caller should expect the YAF number to govern. Parking is at 821 S. Galena Ave. on the House report's parcel list. Outside tour hours and season there is no lawful interior visit; the exterior is readable from the public sidewalk. [1, 2, 8]

## 5. Public progression

Start at the Visitor Center at 810 S. Hennepin, in season and on a tour day, and take the house tour on the hour. Read the house in the order the evidence gives it: first as an 1891 Thompson house, ordinary for its street and period; then as a rented home — the Reagans owned nothing here, and the modesty is the documented fact, not a staging choice; then as the first Dixon address, December 1920 into 1924, of a boy aged nine to thirteen. Hold the later Dixon story — Lowell Park, the high school, the Everett Street house — for the town walk afterward, and do not let the tour's compression move those years back into these rooms. Take the grounds, garage and statue park as the preservation era's layer: a local committee bought a two-apartment rental in 1980, restored it by 1984, refused public money on principle, and ultimately gave it to a national foundation in 2020 when its own finances failed. **Strongest story moment · Authorized, never acquired.** In 2002 the United States authorized a national historic site at this address and then never bought the house; the site a visitor tours today exists because Dixon neighbors, and then Young America's Foundation, kept it — not because the National Park Service runs it. Outside season and tour hours there is no lawful visit beyond the public sidewalk and street. [1, 2, 3, 8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Occupancy-date conflict, printed in full and not merged: the Register record gives significant years 1920 and 1923 and period 1900–1924; the Reagan Library residences list gives December 1920–1924 for 816 S. Hennepin, with the family at 338 W. Everett from 1924; the House Report (107-268) states Reagan "lived in the home located at 816 South Hennepin Road during part of his teenage years (1924-1928)" — a dating that conflicts with both the Register and the Library list and appears to compress the Everett Street years into the Hennepin house. The dossier follows the Library list and the Register (1920–1924 / 1920, 1923) and asserts neither the 1924–1928 dating nor the "Road" street suffix, which all other sources give as Avenue. [1, 2, 3]
- Designation conflict, printed in full: the Reagan Library's "Things Named" and residences pages describe the home as named/designated a National Historic Site (and, in the residences table, a National Historic Landmark) in 2002. Public Law 107-137 authorized establishment only upon federal acquisition, which never occurred; the Register record checked this run shows a National Register listing (1982) and no landmark listing. The dossier describes the site as National Register-listed (1982), authorized-but-not-established as a national historic site (2002), and operated by YAF since December 2020. No NPS page for an operating unit at this address was retrieved this run. [1, 2, 3, 5]
- Address duality: the historic house is 816 S. Hennepin Ave. (Register, Library, City of Dixon); the operator's published site address is 810 S. Hennepin Ave. (the Visitor Center parcel). Both are correct for different buildings in one complex; listings that give only 810 for "the home" conflate them. The gate gives both. [1, 2, 8]
- Hours conflict across generations of listings, printed in full: current operator page (checked this run) — April 2–December 12, Thu–Sat 10–3, tours on the hour. City of Dixon attractions page — closed Nov.–Feb., March weekends only (Sat. 10–4, Sun. 1–4), April–October Mon.–Sat. 10–4 and Sun. 1–4. Older foundation-era listings — daily April 1–October 31, Mon.–Sat. 10–4, Sun. 1–4. The gate uses only the current operator page; the older patterns are superseded and are not relied on. A May 2025 visitor account reported an interior/exterior renovation under way with modified tours; the current operator page checked this run carries no renovation notice (searched, no match), so the renovation is carried as a past visitor report, not a current closure. [5, 7, 8]
- Admission conflict: current operator page states admission is now free. Earlier listings give $5, and one winter-2025 guide gives $8 with free admission for teachers, students, military and fire/EMS. The gate uses the operator's free admission; the charged figures are printed as superseded. [5, 8]
- Phone conflict: the current operator page prints (872) USA-1776 and email reaganboyhoodhome@yaf.org; the City of Dixon and older listings print (815) 288-5176. Both stand as printed; the dossier does not merge them. [7, 8]
- Rent and landlord conflict: one tour tradition gives $15 a month; another gives $23 a month paid to Teresa and John Donovan. No lease or rent receipt was retrieved this run; both figures stand and neither is asserted. The related tradition that the family moved because the landlord raised the rent is carried as tradition, not document. [5]
- Lifeguard-span variance: the House Report gives employment as a lifeguard 1926–1933 and the Library timeline gives a first lifeguard job in summer 1926 with seventy-seven saves over seven summers (1926–1932 on that arithmetic); a Clio summary of the House Report gives 1926–1930. The seventy-seven figure and the Lowell Park ground agree across sources; the exact span does not, and the dossier prints the variance rather than a single span. The saves figure itself rests on Reagan's own account and contemporary retelling; no park payroll or rescue log was retrieved this run. [2, 3, 6]
- December 12 overlap: the operator lists December 12, 2026 both as the season's last day and as a special closure for the Annual Community Christmas Event (with a December 11 Roundtable Luncheon closure the day before). Whether regular tours run on December 12 is not explained on the page; the gate directs confirmation. [8]
- Brother's-account note: the Register nomination tradition, as summarized in the wider record, notes that Ronald Reagan associated this house with important childhood events while his brother Neil was quoted questioning whether this was the right house. The nomination text itself was not opened in full this run (the NPGallery asset is a small PDF record); the disagreement is carried here as a stated gap, not resolved.
