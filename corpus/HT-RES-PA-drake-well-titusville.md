# HT-RES-PA-drake-well-titusville — A man in a top hat beside the wooden tower that struck oil
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A former railroad conductor with no oil experience, a salt-well driller paid $2.50 a day, and a hole 69.5 feet deep beside Oil Creek — on August 27, 1859, that hole filled with oil, and the modern petroleum industry had a birthplace you can still stand on.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a museum visit: Drake Well Museum and Park, 202 Museum Lane, Titusville, PA 16354, administered by the Pennsylvania Historical and Museum Commission (PHMC). The PHMC site record describes the visit as discovering "the birthplace of the modern petroleum industry through operating oil field machinery and a replica of the original well house." The well structure on the ground today is a reconstruction on the original site — the National Register nomination states plainly that "the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania has reconstructed the Drake Oil Well on its original site" — so the visit is to the ground and to a faithful rebuilt engine house and derrick, not to surviving 1859 fabric. Admission as the PHMC's own site page states it: Adult $10; Seniors (65+) & Discount $8; Youth (3–11) $5; Children under 2 free; Active Military Duty & Immediate Family free; Friends of Drake Well members free; discounted rates for group tours by reservation. The PHMC page checked for this draft states no hours, and none are printed here. Note the ground itself sits in Cherrytree Township, Venango County, south of Titusville — the town name in this entry's title is the postal and civic anchor, not the township line.

## 2. The story
Oil was known in the Oil Creek valley long before anyone drilled for it — it was skimmed from seeps and sold as medicine. What changed was organization. The Pennsylvania Rock Oil Company sent Edwin L. Drake to inspect its property near Titusville in December 1857. His report led to a new company, the Seneca Oil Company (organized March 1858, per PHMC's Historic Pennsylvania Leaflet No. 21), with Drake as its general agent at $1,000 a year. Drake — born March 29, 1819, in Greenville, New York, a former conductor on the New York and New Haven Railroad who had retired for poor health and put his $200 savings into the company — arrived in Titusville in May 1858 and became known locally as "Colonel," a courtesy title his colleagues used in addressing his mail.

Drake decided only drilling would produce oil in quantity. Digging failed: groundwater drove his workmen out of the pit. In the spring of 1859 he hired William A. Smith, an experienced salt-well driller and blacksmith from Tarentum — "Uncle Billy" — at $2.50 a day; Smith made the drilling tools himself. By early June 1859 a derrick had been raised: twelve feet square at the base, housing a steam engine that worked a white oak battering ram. As the hole deepened, water caved the walls below the pipe. Drake's answer was to drive cast-iron pipe down to bedrock and drill inside it — the move that made the well possible.

On Saturday afternoon, August 27, 1859, the drill reached its limit at 69.5 feet, dropping into a crevice and slipping a few inches further. The crew pulled the tools and went home. On Sunday, Smith came back, peered into the pipe, and saw oil five inches from the top. He sent word to Drake, who waited until Monday morning to come and see it himself. No one gauged the well in the confusion; PHMC's leaflet puts the evidence at eight to ten barrels a day. The news set off the world's first oil boom, and the Titusville region remained the oil center of the nation for the next quarter-century.

The first structure did not last the year: the original derrick and engine house burned on October 7, 1859 (National Register nomination date), and Drake rebuilt within a month. The well itself was never the fortune its fame suggests — Drake was deposed as Seneca's president in March 1860, lost his savings in New York by 1866, and was rescued from poverty in part by Titusville acquaintances who raised $4,000 for him in 1869 and by a Commonwealth pension of $1,500 a year. The ground outlasted the man: by deed recorded December 2, 1932, the American Petroleum Institute transferred the well site — less than one acre — to the Commonwealth, and the site is now held and interpreted by PHMC as Drake Well Museum and Park.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a raw drilling rig on a flat beside Oil Creek — a twelve-foot-square derrick in a tall, steeple-like boarded tower at one end of a one-story gabled shed, a steam engine and a white oak ram, rough ill-fitting clapboard over all of it. **Now:** the Commonwealth's reconstruction of that same engine house and derrick standing on the original site, with a museum and library beside it, operating oil-field machinery on the grounds, and PHMC interpreting the site as the birthplace of the modern petroleum industry.

**Then:** on Sunday, August 28, 1859, one man looking down a pipe at oil five inches from the top, in no hurry to fetch his employer until Monday. **Now:** a National Register-listed landmark (No. 66000695) whose nomination compresses the whole event into one line — "He struck oil on August 27" — and a museum that has preserved and interpreted the site for more than eighty years.

## 4. What's still there
- The original well site itself — less than one acre, transferred from the American Petroleum Institute to the Commonwealth by the deed of December 2, 1932, as the National Register nomination records it
- The reconstructed Drake Well engine house and derrick on the original site, built to the original's form: tall boarded tower, gabled shed, steam-driven machinery inside illustrating how the well was drilled
- Drake Well Museum and Park: indoor and outdoor exhibits, operating oil-field machinery, and a replica of the original well house, per PHMC
- The museum's library and manuscript and photographic collections on the oil industry, housed with the museum (National Register nomination)
- Oil Creek itself, running past the flat where the well stood — the water the seeps rose in, and the flood risk the later dike was built against

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the museum building, 202 Museum Lane:** Start with the exhibits — oil before Drake: seeps, skimming, and patent medicine — so the drilling problem makes sense before the solution.
2. **At the reconstructed well house:** Stand where the nomination puts the work — the steam engine, the white oak ram, and the pipe driven to bedrock — and read August 27, 1859 at the depth it actually happened: 69.5 feet.
3. **On the flat beside Oil Creek:** The geography half of the story — why the well was here, on seep ground by the creek, and why the site later needed a dike against the same water.
4. **The wider Oil Creek valley, as the heritage record shapes it:** Titusville and the Oil Region National Heritage Area beyond it are their own ground, with their own records.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The drive pipe, honestly: the National Register nomination says Drake "secured 50 feet of cast iron pipe" to stop the collapse; PHMC's own Historic Pennsylvania Leaflet No. 21 says he drove the pipe "thirty-two feet down to bedrock" and began drilling at that depth. Both are Commonwealth/federal records; the figures do not agree, and both print here rather than a merged number.

The first photograph's date is a genuine two-layer record, printed as such in §7: the Library of Congress dates the item it holds to c1890 — a reproduction of a retouched photograph — and flags its own caption-card data as unverified. PHMC's leaflet captions the same famous view (Drake, right, with Peter Wilson) as a visit to the former well "circa 1866," and museum tradition attributes the underlying photograph to Titusville photographer John A. Mather. The LOC record itself names no photographer. The man beside Drake is Peter Wilson, a Titusville friend — not William Smith the driller, despite the identification that circulates with this image.

Production figures for the well's first days were never gauged at the time; PHMC's leaflet gives eight to ten barrels a day as the evidence-based figure. Higher figures in later retellings have no contemporary measurement behind them in the records checked. The nomination also dates the first derrick "early June 1857" in one passage against its own 1859 narrative elsewhere — an apparent typographical slip in the form, flagged rather than silently corrected. Claims that Drake's was the first oil well of any kind anywhere are not made here: other countries hold earlier mechanically drilled wells, and this entry claims only what the records carry — the first commercially successful well drilled specifically for oil in the United States.

## 7. Photo record
**First photograph:** *The first oil well* — Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division, item 2010649522 (reproduction number LC-USZ62-11724). What the downloaded frame actually shows: a retouched black-and-white image — two men in mid-19th-century dress stand in the foreground on grass and loose boards, the man at right in a top hat; behind them rises the tall, tapering, board-clad derrick tower attached to a low gabled engine house, with trees behind and several smaller figures by a fence at right. The retouching is visible in the frame itself, consistent with the record's description. The LOC summary identifies the man at right as Edwin L. Drake and the structure as the Drake Well; PHMC identifies the second man as Peter Wilson (see §6).
