# Mount Pisgah — The teams that came back

Iowa OP06 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

**A way station had to reach back along the road.** William Clayton’s published journal records a May 1846 arrival that almost did not happen that day. His oxen and teams were tired. Men with six yoke sent to meet the party gave the animals a rest and the travelers another chance to reach camp. “This was a great relief to us,” he wrote in the May 26 entry. The record is the digitized published edition, with text checked against the scanned passage where available. The record continues the next morning with missing animals and a search for help to retrieve loads left behind. Reaching the station was a milestone. It did not end the problem of feeding teams or moving bedding and belongings. [4]

**Households stayed while wagons moved on.** The National Park Service dates the selection of the site to May 16 and says the advance company departed in groups June 1–2, leaving at least 200 people. Cabins, dugouts and cultivation kept a place available for those arriving later. The agency's auto-tour guide summarizes a population of about 700 after arrivals over the summer. The county's figure of 3,000 refers explicitly to a peak it dates to 1852. Iowa DNR’s more than 2,000 people pitching tents in 1846 is a different claim with no same-day count stated. Do not combine these figures. [1–3, 5]

**Names stand in for many missing markers.** Illness and death were part of the station’s life. The NPS guide transmits Lorenzo Snow’s recollection: “Scarcely a family escaped sickness”. The guide is the source of the wording used here, with his original manuscript unverified in this review. NPS and the cemetery operator use approximately or up to 150 burials. Iowa DNR reports an estimated 300–800 vanished wooden grave markers scattered on the hillside. The estimates have uncertain boundaries and count different described things. The monument is a memorial rather than a census or a map of individual burial sites. [2, 3, 5, 6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic place.** The settlement and cemetery occupied a wider area near the Grand River than today's county park. The surrounding private parcels remain outside this itinerary. The county describes a limestone obelisk, while NPS calls the memorial marble. Material has not been independently tested. [1–3]

**Lawful public anchor.** Union County's Mount Pisgah Park, off the signed east-of-Creston approach from US 34 to US 169 and the local unpaved approach. Use the public signed arrival and waysides only. NPS warns that GPS can suggest unsuitable dirt roads. If the posted approach is closed or unsuitable for the vehicle and conditions, omit the stop. The church cemetery and private historic ground are separate. [1–3, 5, 6]

## 5. A short visit

Begin at the county wayside. Read Clayton's brief sentence about the returning ox teams, then the reports about people remaining to care for themselves and later arrivals. Finish with **The people who could not go on**, looking quietly from the designated area and leaving burial ground undisturbed. [1–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

The population and burial estimates describe different periods, boundaries or objects and remain unreconciled. The monument material differs by agency description. Clayton’s entry is in a published journal scan and the autograph was not read. Snow’s words were read through NPS transmission. No complete contemporaneous burial register or documented positions of individual graves were found. The park’s year-round listing does not establish plowing, present road surface or every winter gate condition. [1–6]

## 7. Historical photo records

**No adopted historical frame.** The NPS trail guide includes an actual-site landscape view labeled Mount Pisgah Historic Site. Exact exposure date, photographer and distinct rights/asset ID were not established in this review. No historical-to-present image pairing or Photo Point. [5]
