## 2. The story
**Start before the newcomers arrived.** The land was not an empty stage. For Indigenous relationships, use Tribal-published material rather than making a settler interview speak for Tribes: the **Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation’s final 2022 Climate Adaptation Plan**, section on Hanford restoration and decision-making, states the continuation of Treaty Rights and sovereign engagement in restoration. Its discussion connects Hanford with cultural concerns and First Foods. This is CTUIR’s institutional position, not a claim that one Nation represents all Indigenous peoples connected to the area or a 2026 legal-status update. [3]

**Named displaced resident:** **Annette Heriford**, interviewed by **S. L. Sanger on September 12, 1986**, remembered orchards, neighbors and attachment to her town. The published full transcript includes: **“I was the kind that loved my school and my town, my home.”** She recalled the shock of the 1943 removal notice and loss that patriotism did not erase. Her evidence is retrospective lived memory, not an independently checked appraisal or eviction file. The page separately prints a condensed 1995 book version; the quotation here comes from the full transcript. Audio was not heard. [4]

**Named home-front worker:** **Willie Daniels**, interviewed by Sanger on **September 12, 1986**, described leaving low-paid work in the South for wartime employment in Washington. The edited book version records his arrival with his brother in late summer 1943 and beginning work on Labor Day. His account concerns wages, manual construction labor, life away from home and social relationships. It conveys why a job could offer meaningful opportunity without turning one worker’s memory into a claim of universal equality or satisfaction. Exact wage figures are not treated here as verified payroll. [5]

These histories meet but must not cancel each other. Heriford was not simply a prelude to Daniels; Daniels was not merely a symbol of a project. The home-front story involves people pursuing security while others lost familiar places.

Original interpretive voice: *For one family, wartime change arrived as a notice to leave. For another, it arrived as news of a paying job. A responsible account keeps both doors in view, and remembers that Indigenous relationships to the land did not begin or end with either household.* This is our interpretation, not a historical quotation.

**Memory became community work.** The Hanford History Project’s **Harry and Juanita Anderson Collection**, created by the Hanford-White Bluffs Pioneer Association, catalogs correspondence, reunion material and land appraisals relating to displaced residents. The collection description was read, not the underlying correspondence. Its existence supports a further research path into how people maintained connections after removal; it does not authorize quotation from unread letters. [6]

## 3. Then / now
- **Displacement:** residents lost homes and community ties in 1943. **Now:** a public museum and published voices can support remembrance without visiting former properties. [4,7]
- **Employment:** wartime recruitment drew workers seeking better livelihoods. **Now:** labor history should include individual motives and limits of recollection, not just aggregate achievements. [5]
- **Continuing Indigenous relationships:** CTUIR’s published plan frames Hanford through ongoing rights and responsibilities. **Now:** “former inhabitants” alone would misleadingly erase continuity. [3]
- **Reunions and correspondence:** displaced residents worked to preserve connections. **Now:** an archive can remain meaningful when a familiar community no longer exists in its old form. [6]

This entry does not assess technical production, contamination pathways, weapons, current cleanup operations or federal access arrangements.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**One indoor museum visit.** The operator estimates a complete museum visit at 45–60 minutes; allow additional reflection time as a visitor preference, not a booked program. [1]

1. **Before entry:** read the short Tribal-published context. Establish that land relationships continue, rather than treating the regional story as starting with the 1943 project.
2. **Begin with community:** use available public galleries to place towns, families and everyday work in view. Do not promise particular objects or labels.
3. **Read Heriford’s named recollection:** let a home and school register as lived attachments, not merely parcels on a planning map.
4. **Read Daniels’s employment account:** hold the opportunity of wages beside displacement, without resolving the tension through a simplistic verdict or claiming the narrators met.
5. **End with the strongest story moment:** the community archive shows that moving people did not dissolve their relationships. The question becomes what people carried, rebuilt and refused to forget. Conclude inside the public museum, not with a search for physical remnants.

**Current/winter mode:** indoor museum under its own hours and holiday calendar. **Open-season mode:** exactly the same independent visit; no federal tour or outdoor route is automatically added. If the museum closes, use the published sources remotely. No Route Group is created.
