# HT-RES-VA-monticello — The little mountain one man designed, and the enslaved people who built and ran it
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A brick house under a low dome on a levelled Virginia mountaintop — designed, demolished in part, and redesigned by Thomas Jefferson across forty years, and raised, cooked for, cleaned, farmed, and made workable the whole time by the enslaved families of Mulberry Row, whose street ran along the slope just below the portico.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a house and its mountaintop: Monticello, Thomas Jefferson's home, in Albemarle County, Virginia, just outside Charlottesville, operated by the Thomas Jefferson Foundation, which has owned it since 1923. The Foundation's own pages state the terms, and they are printed here as those pages carry them: the Highlights Tour — the Foundation's most popular tour, 45 minutes, covering Jefferson's life, the house's architecture, and slavery on the plantation, including first-floor rooms and outdoor spaces — is listed at $42 for adults, $13 for ages 12–18, $4 for ages 5–11, and free for children under 5. A Grounds Ticket, which provides access to the historic mountaintop, exhibits, and daily programs but does not include house entry, is listed at $22 for adults, $8 for ages 12–18, $2 for ages 5–11, and free under 5. Slavery at Monticello tours and Gardens and Grounds tours are described by the Foundation as included with admission. The Foundation's address, as its own pages carry it, is 1050 Monticello Loop, Charlottesville, VA 22902. Monticello was designated a National Historic Landmark on December 19, 1960, and added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000826, per the NPS's National Register record and the Virginia Department of Historic Resources); with the University of Virginia, it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1987.

## 2. The story
Thomas Jefferson inherited the land from his father, Peter Jefferson, in 1757, and chose for his house the summit of a small mountain in Albemarle County — Monticello, "little mountain" in Italian. In May 1768 he contracted to have the top of the mountain cleared and levelled. Building began in 1769, to a design drawn largely from Andrea Palladio's *Four Books of Architecture*: a two-storey Palladian villa with doubled porticoes. The first part finished was not the house but the South Pavilion, a small brick outbuilding; when fire destroyed Jefferson's childhood home at Shadwell in 1770, he moved into the pavilion, and his wife Martha Wayles Skelton joined him there in 1772 while the main house rose slowly nearby. A French visitor, the Marquis de Chastellux, described Jefferson in 1782 as "the architect, and often one of the workmen." By the time Jefferson left for Paris in 1784 as American minister, the outside of this first house — Monticello I — was largely complete and the inside was not.

None of it was built by Jefferson's hands alone, and the honest version of the story has to say whose hands they were. Monticello was the home farm of a plantation of about 5,000 acres, and its building, farming, cooking, and craft work were done overwhelmingly by enslaved people. The bricks were made on the mountain. Enslaved joiners, carpenters, blacksmiths, nailers, cooks, and field hands raised the house and kept it; the Foundation's own roster of people enslaved at Monticello names, among many others, John Hemmings, the master joiner whose woodwork is in the house's finest rooms; James Hemings, trained as a French chef in Paris and freed in 1796 after teaching his brother Peter his skills; and Edith Hern Fossett, head cook from 1809 to 1826. Along the slope southeast of the house ran Mulberry Row, which the Foundation describes as the industrial hub of the plantation — a street of more than twenty cabins, workshops, and storehouses that was the center of work and domestic life for dozens of free and enslaved workers, among them the Hemings family, the largest enslaved family at Monticello.

Five years in France changed Jefferson's idea of what a house should be. He studied the new domestic architecture of Paris at first hand, and after leaving the post of Secretary of State in 1794 he came home intending to rebuild. Work began in 1796. He took off the upper storey of the first house, more than doubled the building's depth, and turned an eight-room villa into a house of twenty-one rooms whose fronts read as a single low storey, the upper bedrooms hidden behind the entablature. The octagonal dome — the first dome on a house in America, modelled on the Temple of Vesta in Rome — was added in 1800 over the west front, in place of the second-storey portico. The working rooms of the house — kitchen, dairy, smokehouse, stables — went into L-shaped service wings sunk into the hillside, their roofs forming the terraces that run out from both sides of the house, so that the labor of the house passed under the visitor's feet and largely out of sight. Building continued through Jefferson's vice-presidency and presidency; the house was finished in 1809, the year he left office and retired to it for good.

Jefferson died at Monticello on July 4, 1826, deeply in debt. The estate's contents — and its enslaved people — were sold; the dispersal sales of 1827 broke up families who had lived on the mountain for generations, a fact the Foundation's research documents name by name. The house itself passed out of the family: sold in 1831 to James Turner Barclay, a Charlottesville druggist who tore out trees and gardens for a failed silkworm venture, and then, in 1834, to Uriah Phillips Levy, a U.S. Navy commodore who admired Jefferson and chose preservation over remodeling, spending his own money to maintain the house as Jefferson had made it. Levy's heirs contested his will for years; in 1879 his nephew Jefferson Monroe Levy took control and spent, by the NPS's account, around a million dollars across 44 years restoring the deteriorating house. In 1923 the newly formed Thomas Jefferson Memorial Foundation — today's Thomas Jefferson Foundation — purchased Monticello and 650 acres for $500,000, taking ownership on December 1, 1923, and it has kept the house as a museum and research center since.

The second rebuilding of Monticello is interpretive, and it is still going on. For much of the twentieth century the enslaved community was largely absent from the house's public story. The Foundation's own research program reversed that: landscape archaeology from 1979 onward recovered Mulberry Row's building sites; the Getting Word oral-history project recorded descendants of the enslaved families; and in 2018 the Foundation opened *The Life of Sally Hemings* exhibit in the house's south wing, in a room identified as Hemings's own. Sally Hemings (1773–1835), an enslaved domestic servant, was Martha Jefferson's half-sister and the mother of six children recorded in Jefferson's own farm book. Her son Madison Hemings's 1873 published account named Jefferson as his father. A 1998 DNA study published in *Nature* found that a male-line descendant of her youngest son, Eston Hemings, carried the Jefferson family Y-chromosome, and ruled out the Carr nephews long named by Jefferson's grandchildren. The Foundation's research committee reported in January 2000 that the preponderance of evidence indicated Jefferson was the father of Eston's — and, by the pattern of the evidence, of Hemings's other children. In a statement of June 6, 2018, the Foundation went further and stated its view plainly: the issue is, in its words, "a settled historical matter," and Jefferson fathered Sally Hemings's children. The four who survived to adulthood — Beverly, Harriet, Madison, and Eston Hemings — were all allowed to leave or were freed, the only enslaved family at Monticello of whom that is true.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a levelled summit with a two-storey Palladian villa rising slowly through the 1770s, its owner living in a one-room pavilion while enslaved brickmakers, joiners, and carpenters built the main house around him. **Now:** the rebuilt house of 1809 — twenty-one rooms reading as one storey under the dome — kept by the Foundation, with the South Pavilion still standing at the end of the south terrace.

**Then:** Mulberry Row, a working street of cabins, workshops, and storehouses below the house, home to the Hemings, Fossett, Gillette, Granger, and Hern families and the industrial center of a 5,000-acre plantation. **Now:** the Row is largely archaeology — building sites marked and interpreted along the lane, some structures reconstructed, with guided Slavery at Monticello tours starting at the recreated Hemings cabin and a contemplative site honoring the enslaved community.

**Then:** a house whose service work was deliberately hidden — dumbwaiters, a revolving serving door, and service wings sunk under terraces so enslaved workers stayed out of the public rooms' sight. **Now:** the Foundation interprets those same devices as evidence, and the south wing holds an exhibit on Sally Hemings drawn from her son Madison's account.

## 4. What's still there
- The house itself, substantially of its 1796–1809 fabric: entrance hall, parlor, dining room and tea room, Jefferson's bedroom and library suite, the dome room above, and the terrace wings with the dependencies beneath them (Foundation and Encyclopedia Virginia accounts)
- Mulberry Row: the lane, its excavated and marked building sites, reconstructed structures including the Hemings cabin, and the Foundation's Slavery at Monticello tours (Foundation pages)
- The Sally Hemings exhibit in the south wing, drawn from Madison Hemings's 1873 account (Foundation pages)
- The gardens: the restored flower gardens around the house and the thousand-foot terraced vegetable garden on the southeast slope, first cut by enslaved laborers beginning in 1807 (Foundation and World Heritage listing summary)
- The Monticello Graveyard on the slope below the house, where Jefferson is buried under the obelisk he designed, still owned by his descendants (Foundation pages)
- The David M. Rubenstein Visitor Center at the mountain's base, with the Foundation's exhibits and the shuttle to the mountaintop (Foundation pages)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the visitor center:** Start at the base of the mountain, where the Foundation's exhibits frame both halves of the story — the architect and the enslaved community — before the shuttle up.
2. **In the house, on the Highlights Tour:** The first floor as the Foundation presents it — Jefferson's life, the architecture, and slavery on the plantation in one 45-minute pass, from the entrance hall to the bedroom-library suite.
3. **Under the terraces:** The south wing and its dependencies — the kitchen and service rooms sunk into the hillside, and the Sally Hemings exhibit in the room identified as hers.
4. **Along Mulberry Row:** The working street below the house — the Hemings cabin, the workshop and dwelling sites, taken at walking pace or with the Slavery at Monticello tour, which the Foundation includes with admission.
5. **In the gardens and at the graveyard:** The vegetable garden terrace on the southeast slope, cut by enslaved laborers from 1807, and the walk down to the family graveyard and Jefferson's obelisk.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Monticello's construction chronology is carried with slightly different start dates across the primary records, and the differences print rather than resolve. The National Historic Landmark nomination (Virginia Department of Historic Resources file 002-0050) states that "Jefferson began building Monticello from his own design in 1770." The Foundation's own published timeline, as quoted in secondary accounts, puts the first bricks and the start of construction in 1769, after the summit was levelled in 1768. The NPS National Register record adds a third marker: its significant years for the property are 1772 and 1826. This draft prints the sequence as the sources divide it — summit levelled 1768, building under way by 1769–1770, South Pavilion occupied 1770 — and does not settle on a single "begun" year.

Room counts differ by what is being counted, and both figures in circulation come from reputable summaries: the World Heritage listing summary describes the rebuilt house as 21 rooms, while other architectural summaries count 33 rooms in the main house and 43 across the whole complex including pavilions and dependencies. The counts are printed here as a scope difference, not reconciled into one number.

On Jefferson's paternity of Sally Hemings's children, the Foundation's own publications carry two positions seventeen years apart, and both print. Its January 2000 research committee report concluded from the preponderance of evidence that Jefferson fathered the children, while expressly noting the scientific possibility, on the DNA evidence alone, of another Jefferson male. Its June 6, 2018 statement supersedes that caution in the Foundation's own voice, calling the question "a settled historical matter." A minority scholarly dissent (the Thomas Jefferson Heritage Society's 2001 scholars' commission) exists in the literature and is noted here as existing; this draft follows the operator's current stated position while printing that its own 2000 report was more qualified.

The HABS photograph in §7 carries no verified capture year: its record gives only documentation compiled after 1933, and the survey dates of 1937 and 1940 carried in the National Register nomination's survey line cannot be tied to this specific frame. No year is printed for it. The NPS National Register record consulted for this entry (NRIS 66000826) carries no latitude or longitude, so the locator below prints none — coordinates are not taken from lead-list sources.
