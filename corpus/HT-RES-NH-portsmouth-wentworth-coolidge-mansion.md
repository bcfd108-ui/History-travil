# HT-RES-NH-portsmouth-wentworth-coolidge-mansion — Portsmouth: the governor’s house at Little Harbor
Division NH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

A saltbox from about 1695, an ell from about 1730, and a royal governor’s formal rooms from about 1750, grown together into one house at Little Harbor. Benning Wentworth, New Hampshire’s first royal governor, ran a province from this household, and his council chamber survives. A century and a half later the artist John Templeman Coolidge made the estate a summer gathering place, and Francis Parkman wrote in an upstairs room.

## 1. Trip gate

Wentworth–Coolidge Mansion is a New Hampshire state historic site at Little Harbor. The mansion interior is seasonal and is currently closed for the season. New Hampshire State Parks states that the grounds and the Little Harbor Loop Trail remain open dawn to dusk throughout the year. The state page lists free parking and trail access at the site. Interior tours, admission, staffing, and event access must be checked against the operator’s current notice before travel.

The lawful public anchor is the official state-site parking area, opened grounds, and, when staffed and open, the mansion’s public tour route. The 1.5-mile Little Harbor Loop Trail connects the mansion grounds with Creek Farm and Portsmouth conservation land. No access is implied to a closed mansion, staff rooms, preservation work, shoreline structures, neighboring residences, or unmaintained edges. Off-season users assume the ordinary risks of an unstaffed park and should not treat a closed building as an invitation to test doors or boundaries.

## 2. Story

The house is not a single construction campaign. The National Register description identifies three principal sections whose sequence explains its unusual form. A small saltbox structure dating to about 1695 formed the western leg. An eastern ell followed about 1730, although some records place that enlargement around 1725. After Benning Wentworth inherited the property, a more formal eastern section was built about 1750 for the public and private life of New Hampshire’s royal governor.

Wentworth had become the first royal governor of the separately administered Province of New Hampshire in 1741 and remained in office until 1767. Little Harbor was therefore more than a country residence. The 1750 rooms included a formal entry, parlor, and spaces associated with official business. The surviving council chamber is physical evidence that government could operate from a household as well as from a purpose-built public building. The house’s modest early fabric, skilled shipwright-style framing, and formal governor’s rooms stand beside one another rather than forming a tidy period tableau.

Wentworth’s governorship was tied to the granting of land west of the Connecticut River. Those grants contributed to the long jurisdictional struggle between New Hampshire and New York and to the settlement pattern that became Vermont. A visit should not reduce that process to a governor’s signature. Land grants altered Indigenous homelands, created competing colonial claims, and enriched officials and speculators. This dossier retains the mansion as the ground of administration without pretending the consequences stopped at Little Harbor.

Wentworth married Martha Hilton in 1760 at the mansion. Popular retellings often center the difference in their ages and her earlier position in his household; the documented house matters more than the embellished romance. He left office in 1767 and died in 1770. The property passed through later owners and gradually lost the concentrated political role it had carried under the governor.

Artist and antiquarian John Templeman Coolidge acquired the property in 1883. He repaired the neglected mansion and used it as a summer home. In 1916 he added a western wing with bedrooms and baths; the state nomination notes that later restoration converted that section to support uses. Coolidge’s family and guests made the estate an artistic and literary gathering place. His first wife, Katharine, was historian Francis Parkman’s daughter, and Parkman used an upstairs room as a summer writing retreat.

Coolidge’s widow, Mary Abigail Parsons Coolidge, donated the property to New Hampshire in 1954. The Division of Parks undertook a major restoration in 1966: structural members were strengthened, chimneys substantially rebuilt, modern fixtures removed from the main historic sections, concealed electrical service added, and researched paint colors applied. The state thereby preserved an unusually complex colonial house, but it also made choices about which periods to show. The building visitors see is historic fabric interpreted through twentieth-century preservation work.

## 3. Then and now

The circa-1900 adopted photograph records the mansion during the Coolidge era, after the colonial property had become a summer home but before the 1916 wing and the state’s 1966 restoration. The image shows the layered massing rather than a symmetrical governor’s palace: connected clapboard sections step across the frame, with different roof lines, windows, entries, and chimneys. Garden plantings partly obscure the lowest section. That irregularity is evidence of adaptation over time.

Today the site interprets a roughly forty-room mansion and its remaining grounds. New Hampshire State Parks describes five architectural periods and points visitors to the hand-carved mantelpiece, council chamber, and so-called spy closet. The National Register record is more precise about three major construction sections and later alterations. Those accounts are compatible only if “periods” includes smaller campaigns, finishes, or later work beyond the three principal blocks.

The mansion remains lightly furnished compared with fully reconstructed house museums. That restraint is useful. It keeps the building’s frame, floor levels, joinery, room sequence, and restoration history visible rather than claiming that every object occupies an original location.

## 5. Public progression

Begin on the lawn where the connected sections can be read together. Compare the building’s present massing with the circa-1900 photograph before entering. The most important visual fact is accumulation: a late-seventeenth-century domestic structure, an early-eighteenth-century expansion, a governor’s formal rooms, and later family and state work occupy one connected house.

When the interior is open, move with the operator’s sequence rather than seeking a single “oldest room.” Read the early keeping spaces and framing first, then the 1730 ell, then the formal 1750 rooms. In the council chamber, consider the mansion as government ground. In the parlor and entry, distinguish surviving fabric from restored color and later furnishing. End with the Coolidge and state-preservation chapters so the visit does not freeze the property in 1750.

On the grounds, the harbor relationship explains why this was a working estate and political retreat rather than an isolated inland mansion. The trail may extend the visit, but it does not authorize shoreline scrambling or entry onto adjacent private parcels.

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

- The National Register form dates the oldest section to about 1695, the middle ell to 1730 while noting a 1725 date in some sources, and the governor’s section to 1750. The exact year of every alteration is not settled.
- State interpretation describes five architectural periods; the National Register nomination describes three major sections plus later additions and restoration. This dossier prints both scales instead of forcing a false one-to-one match.
- The state page calls Wentworth New Hampshire’s first royal governor. That refers to the separately governed royal province beginning in 1741, not to the first European colonial authority on this ground.
- The individual identities and labor of many craftspeople, servants, farm workers, and household workers are not established by the free records used here. Their work should not be absorbed into Wentworth’s biography.
- The 1966 project was a substantial restoration. “Surviving” does not mean every structural member, finish, chimney, fixture, furnishing, or landscape feature is untouched.
- The LOC record dates the adopted photograph only circa 1900. A narrower year is not asserted.
- The State Parks page contains a contradictory repeated line describing the grounds as open “dusk to dawn.” Its main hours statement, the site association, and ordinary park use identify the intended period as dawn to dusk; visitors should follow current posted signs.
- Coordinates remain blank until two independent authoritative sources agree within the project tolerance.
