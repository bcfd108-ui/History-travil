## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** labor history did not stop at the mine entrance. It continued in the households that lost housing, cared for children and carried the memory into later generations.

In Shanna Lewis's **April 18, 2014 KRCC report**, **Mary Elaine Petrucci**, granddaughter of survivor Mary Petrucci, recalls her family's loss of six-year-old Bernard during the strike winter. Her brief published words are **“He died at Ludlow.”** The report places Thomas and Mary Petrucci and their children among families displaced from company housing into union tent colonies. [2]

This is a named descendant's published recollection, not an interview with a person who witnessed 1914. The same report describes Mary Petrucci later traveling with other strikers' wives to tell the public what had happened. Her public advocacy matters alongside bereavement; the family should not appear only as a list of victims. [2]

A complementary household lead is **Mary Thomas**, whose 1974 oral history is excerpted in the publisher's transcript of *Criminal*, episode 264, dated April 12, 2024. Its domestic passages concern making a tent habitable and sustaining companionship. The episode distinguishes archival voice from narration. [4] The original interview was not retrieved, and no audio was heard; this is a mediated excerpt, not a full oral-history review.

**Original interpretation:** home is not a small subject beside a labor dispute. A household is where a dispute changes meals, rest, care and the possibility of planning tomorrow. The strongest interpretive question here is not how to reconstruct an event but how descendants choose to remember people whose lives were larger than the event that killed them.

## 3. Then / now

The strike began in 1913; the April 20, 1914 massacre and its losses belong to a longer household history, not an isolated anniversary. [2] This draft deliberately avoids an operational chronology or reproducing violent details.

The present museum is a place for later interpretation. Its advertised paintings are works of remembrance, not contemporaneous photographs or evidence that every depicted scene happened exactly as painted. [1] A visitor should be able to distinguish an artist's choices, a relative's recollection and a document created at the time.

The CPR companion photo page identifies **Mary Elaine and her father Frank Petrucci at a September 2013 event** and separately describes older family photographs. [3] The repeated names across generations require care. A later Frank must not be silently equated with a child named Frank in a 1913 image.

## 4. What's still there

- **Public museum interpretation:** winter hours are published, but actual admission on a selected day and the installation condition remain unverified. [1]
- **Published descendant testimony:** the KRCC text can be read remotely, without entering any historical property. [2]
- **Photographic evidence lead:** CPR publishes captions identifying family members across generations. The individual images were not visually inspected in this research pass. [3]
- **Recorded worker-household voice lead:** the Mary Thomas excerpt requires original-recording verification before quotation or more detailed treatment. [4]
- **Outside visitor scope:** former industrial areas, private homes, camp ground features and places of death. No apparent surviving feature is offered as a public entrance or a discovery target.

The accessible historical record is sufficient for reflection without promising that an original household object is currently on display.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Proposed public-museum experience only, subject to the day's actual opening:

1. **Arrive during the short winter opening window.** Check the entrance notice before treating the visit as available. Do not assume adjoining premises belong to the museum.
2. **Begin with a household question.** Ask what the interpretation shows about everyday care, housing and community, not just employers and union leaders.
3. **Read an identified work as interpretation.** Note its creator and date if a label supplies them. Do not supply missing attribution from this draft.
4. **Compare kinds of evidence.** A descendant remembers a family; an artist composes a scene; a contemporary document records a particular encounter. None automatically replaces the others.
5. **End quietly with the named family.** Reflect on Bernard as a child in a household rather than a number in a total, and on the later work of relatives preserving memory. No physical pilgrimage to a death location is required.

This is the strongest story moment: the visitor leaves with a clearer idea of who is speaking, not a completed challenge. No timing, walking distance, seating or accessible entrance geometry is invented. If the museum is closed, the sequence becomes a private reading exercise rather than an alternate drive.
