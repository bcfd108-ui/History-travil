## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** The most important thing a museum visit can teach here is that public history does not make an entire community public property.

**Named Pueblo-published voice:** Taos Pueblo's own *The Return of Blue Lake* attributes a response to **Cacique Romero**, identified there as a religious leader of the late 1960s and 1970s who testified before Congress. After the 1970 return, he said: **“Anew day begins not only for the American Indian, but for all Americans in this Country.”** The joined spelling “Anew” is retained from the webpage. This is a quotation republished by the Pueblo, not an original recording heard or a verbatim congressional transcript independently recovered in this pass. The page supplies no fuller personal name, so none is invented. [4]

The same account dates the signing of Public Law 91-550 to December 15, 1970, and presents restoration as a foundation for continuing culture and self-determination. Its invitation attributed to Governor James A. Lujan belongs to that undated commemorative account; it is not a current invitation or evidence of a 2026 officeholder. [4]

**Separate sovereign institutional account:** Taos Pueblo's Central Management System describes the return in terms of trust status, exclusive traditional use and continuing protection. It explicitly states that the Blue Lake Wilderness Area is closed to the public. [5] The account is read as published policy/context, not converted into a destination or a request for access.

**Everyday continuity:** The Pueblo's history page describes recurrent family maintenance of adobe homes and their continuing connection to ancestors. [6] That emphasis changes the reading of a historic image: apparent architectural permanence rests on living responsibilities. A picture cannot substitute for permission to approach or enter a home.

**Authority distinction:** Taos Pueblo's government website links the Pueblo tourism site used for Romero's account. [5] The museum separately describes collaboration; it is not the Pueblo government. Taos Pueblo is not interchangeable with Acoma, Zuni or any other Pueblo, and a regional museum's Southwest collection does not erase those distinctions.

## 3. Then / now
The 1970 restoration is a documented turning point in the Pueblo's own public narrative. [4,5] Today's institutional boundary makes the lesson concrete: acknowledgement of a historic injustice does not require visitors to reach the affected ground.

The museum supplies another kind of comparison. Juan Mirabal's *North Side, Taos Pueblo* is dated 1960 and identified as a painting on board. [3] It is an artist's work, not a survey, an archival photograph or a camera-location instruction. No street-by-street reconstruction or present-day matching shot is proposed.

Interpretive synthesis: place the public historical account beside a credited artist's representation, while keeping both separate from the contemporary people's authority over their lives. The strongest “now” is not an unchanged skyline; it is ongoing agency.
