# HT-RES-PA-gettysburg-national-military-park — The rocky hill above the grass, photographed in the year it was fought over
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Two ridges, a wheatfield, a peach orchard, and a rocky hill — ordinary Pennsylvania farmland that two armies converged on in July 1863 and left as the most studied battlefield ground in America, with a president's two-minute speech still attached to it.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a battlefield, not a single building: Gettysburg National Military Park, the ground of the Battle of Gettysburg, fought July 1–3, 1863, around the town of Gettysburg in Adams County, Pennsylvania. The operator is the National Park Service. The NPS's own fees page states the terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: the Gettysburg battlefield and visitor center are free to visit, no entrance pass is required, and the battlefield is open daily (weather permitting) from 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset. Tickets are required only for the film "A New Birth of Freedom," the Cyclorama painting, and the Gettysburg Museum of the Civil War inside the Museum and Visitor Center — the NPS fees page lists $20.75 for adults, $19.75 for seniors (65+) and veterans, $15.75 for youth (6–12), with active military and children under 6 free. The NPS directs visitors to begin at the National Park Service Museum and Visitor Center at 1195 Baltimore Pike. The park was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000642, per the NPS's own National Register record).

## 2. The story
By May 1863 the Civil War had run two years. In the east, Gen. Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia had just beaten the Federal Army of the Potomac at Chancellorsville, Virginia, while in the west Maj. Gen. Ulysses S. Grant had Vicksburg, Mississippi, under siege. The NPS overview states Lee's purpose plainly: after Chancellorsville he decided on an invasion of the North to revive Confederate morale and relieve pressure on Vicksburg. Lee began his movement north on **June 3, 1863**, moving west from Fredericksburg into the Shenandoah Valley, using the mountains to screen the march, while the Army of the Potomac followed east of the mountains under standing orders to stay between Lee and Washington, D.C. On **June 28, 1863**, Maj. Gen. George Gordon Meade was named the Army of the Potomac's new commander, with his army around Frederick, Maryland, about 35 miles south of Gettysburg. By June 30 a division of Lee's army under Maj. Gen. Henry Heth was nine miles west of Gettysburg on the Chambersburg Pike, and Brig. Gen. John Buford's Federal cavalry division was approaching from the south. In the NPS's words, the time and place were set for the largest battle of the American Civil War.

The main battle opened on the morning of **July 1, 1863**, with Confederates attacking Federal troops on McPherson Ridge west of town. Outnumbered, the Federals held until afternoon, were overpowered, and were driven back to Cemetery Hill south of town; during the night the main body of the Federal army arrived and took up positions.

On **July 2** the lines stood in two sweeping arcs about a mile apart on parallel ridges — Federal forces on Cemetery Ridge, Confederates on Seminary Ridge to the west. Lee ordered attacks against both Federal flanks. Lt. Gen. James Longstreet's thrust on the Federal left turned the base of Little Round Top into a shambles, left the Wheatfield strewn with dead and wounded, and overran the Peach Orchard. Farther north, Lt. Gen. Richard S. Ewell's evening attack on the Federal right at East Cemetery Hill and Culp's Hill was momentarily successful but could not be exploited.

On **July 3** Lee's artillery opened a two-hour bombardment of the Federal lines on Cemetery Ridge and Cemetery Hill. Then some 12,000 Confederates advanced across open fields toward the Federal center in the attack known as "Pickett's Charge." It failed, and cost Lee over 5,000 soldiers in one hour. The Battle of Gettysburg was over; on July 4 Lee began his retreat to Virginia.

The park's second story is a burial ground. The NPS records that a committee headed by Gettysburg attorney David Wills, the Pennsylvania state agent, chose a 17-acre site for a common burial ground for the Union dead, and that reburial began October 27, 1863. For the dedication the committee chose Massachusetts statesman Edward Everett to deliver the main speech and asked President Abraham Lincoln to deliver "a few appropriate remarks." At the **November 19, 1863** ceremony, before a crowd the NPS puts at some 15,000, Everett spoke for two hours; Lincoln then spoke for about two minutes. His brief speech — the Gettysburg Address — gave meaning, in the NPS overview's words, to the sacrifices of the men who had struggled here, and stated his belief that the war would lead to a "new birth of freedom" for the nation. The war itself ran another 18 months, ending in the east with Lee's surrender to Grant on April 9, 1865, at Appomattox Court House, Virginia.

The ground was then kept. The NPS Historical Handbook for the park (Tilberg, Handbook No. 9) records that the act approved February 12, 1895, established Gettysburg National Military Park, including a provision for a memorial to Lincoln's address. The National Register followed on October 15, 1966.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** parallel ridges a mile apart — Cemetery Ridge held by the Federal army, Seminary Ridge by the Confederates — with farm fields, a wheatfield, and a peach orchard between and below them, and the rocky base of Little Round Top at the Federal left. **Now:** the same ridges and fields, kept as a National Military Park, with the NPS reporting a multi-year rehabilitation project that removed non-historic trees, replanted historic woodlots and orchards, and rebuilt fences to return major battle areas to their 1863 appearance.

**Then:** Union dead in shallow battlefield graves, reburied from October 27, 1863, into a new 17-acre soldiers' cemetery on Cemetery Hill. **Now:** Gettysburg National Cemetery, where the Soldiers' National Monument stands — its cornerstone laid July 4, 1865, the monument dedicated July 1, 1869, per the NPS handbook — and where Lincoln's two minutes are the site's second, permanent battle.

**Then:** a glass wet-collodion negative made in 1863 showing Little Round Top as a bare, rock-strewn, tree-dotted hill above grass (see §7). **Now:** the hill is a named stop on the battlefield the visitor drives and walks, its 1863 face recoverable only because photographers reached the field within days.

## 4. What's still there
- The battlefield ground itself: McPherson Ridge, Cemetery Ridge, Seminary Ridge, Cemetery Hill, Culp's Hill, East Cemetery Hill, Little Round Top, the Wheatfield, and the Peach Orchard, all named in the NPS overview as the battle's own ground
- Gettysburg National Cemetery on Cemetery Hill, with the Soldiers' National Monument (cornerstone laid July 4, 1865; dedicated July 1, 1869, per the NPS handbook)
- The Museum and Visitor Center at 1195 Baltimore Pike, holding the film "A New Birth of Freedom," the Cyclorama painting, and the Gettysburg Museum of the Civil War (NPS fees and visitor-center pages)
- A collection the NPS press kit puts at more than 300,000 objects and artifacts and 700,000 archival materials
- The rehabilitated 1863 landscape: replanted historic orchards and woodlots and rebuilt fences in the major battle action areas (NPS press kit)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Museum and Visitor Center, 1195 Baltimore Pike:** Start where the NPS directs visitors to start — the film, the Cyclorama, and the museum, ticketed as the NPS fees page states, inside a building that is itself free to enter.
2. **On the first day's ground:** McPherson Ridge west of town, where the battle opened on the morning of July 1, 1863, and Cemetery Hill south of town, where the driven-back Federals rallied.
3. **On the second day's ground:** The parallel ridges — Cemetery Ridge against Seminary Ridge — and the Federal left: Little Round Top's base, the Wheatfield, and the Peach Orchard; then the Federal right at Culp's Hill and East Cemetery Hill.
4. **On the third day's ground:** The open fields of Pickett's Charge, crossed by some 12,000 Confederates toward the Federal center on Cemetery Ridge on July 3.
5. **At the cemetery:** Gettysburg National Cemetery, where the reburials began October 27, 1863, and where Everett's two hours and Lincoln's two minutes were delivered on November 19, 1863.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The army totals do not agree across the NPS's own publications, and the disagreement is printed rather than resolved. The NPS Historical Handbook (Tilberg, 1954, reprint 1961) gives 75,000 Confederates and 88,000 Union troops — about 163,000 men — and 51,000 casualties. The NPS press kit gives more than 165,000 soldiers fought and more than 51,000 casualties (killed, wounded, captured, or missing). Both NPS figures are carried here as their sources state them; this draft does not reconcile them.

The NPS National Register record for the park (66000642) carries no latitude or longitude, so the locator below prints none — coordinates are not taken from lead-list sources. The same record lists significant years 1863, 1864, and 1893, while the NPS handbook dates the park's establishing act to February 12, 1895; the two are printed as their records carry them, and the difference between the 1893 significant year and the 1895 act is not explained in the sources consulted.

The first photo's record (§7) names no photographer and gives no month or day — only the year 1863 — so no photographer and no finer date are printed for it.

## 7. Photo record

No dead, no wreckage, and no monument appear in the frame; it is the hill as terrain. Inspected from the downloaded file before captioning.
