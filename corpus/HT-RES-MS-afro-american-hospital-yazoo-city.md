# Afro-American Sons and Daughters Hospital, Yazoo City — supplement update: 1928 Black fraternal hospital demolished in 2024

**Research draft · Labeled THEME SWEEP (Family 3 — Hospitals · Updated to NO-ANCHOR / demolished-site street-edge reading) · Yazoo City, Yazoo County, Mississippi · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate

**CRITICAL STATUS UPDATE / SUPPLEMENT CORRECTION:** The institutional sweep supplement listed the **Afro-American Hospital** (`8th Street and Webster Avenue, Yazoo City, MS 39194`) as a standing historic hospital LEAD. Local press and preservation verification confirm the building is **no longer standing**: an **October 11, 2022 fire** gutted roughly half of the vacant hospital, the Mississippi Department of Archives and History (**MDAH**) subsequently determined the damaged shell was **unsalvageable**, and the city/owners **demolished the remaining structure in 2024** (*Yazoo Herald*, Oct. 16, 2022; Oct. 14, 2023; Mar. 23, 2024; July 14, 2025). [1, 2, 3, 4]

As of July 2025 reporting, the corner lot at 8th Street and Webster Avenue is a **"now-empty site"** while community stakeholders discuss a commemorative historic marker and mural (erection unconfirmed as of November 2026). No historic hospital building remains to enter or tour. [4]

## 2. The story

During the Jim Crow era, Black Mississippians faced systematic exclusion or segregated, substandard basement wards at white-run hospitals. In **1924**, Yazoo City businessman and fraternal leader **Thomas J. Huddleston Sr.** founded the **Afro-American Sons and Daughters**, a Black fraternal benefit society that grew to tens of thousands of members across Mississippi and provided burial and medical benefits for modest monthly dues. [5]

In **1928**, the organization commissioned a dedicated hospital on the corner of **8th Street and Webster Avenue** in Yazoo City, constructed by Black contractor **Austin Broomfield** for **$32,147**. Opened on **December 27, 1928**, under first medical director **Dr. Lloyd Tevis Miller**, the single-story dark-brick building initially held **25 beds** alongside an operating room, X-ray equipment, laboratory, and an attached **nurses' training school** and dormitory. [5]

Demand quickly outpaced the original wing. In **1935**, a major expansion converted the footprint into an **E-shaped plan** housing roughly **50 beds**, making it a vital medical center for African American patients across the Delta and central Mississippi hills for more than four decades—serving in total over **100,000 patients** before closing its inpatient hospital doors in **1972** (operating briefly thereafter as a clinic/nursing center before long vacancy). [5]

The hospital was listed on the **National Register of Historic Places on January 24, 2006** (`NRHP 05001558`), recognized as one of the premier surviving Black fraternal hospitals in Mississippi. After years of deterioration, fire tore through the vacant complex on **October 11, 2022**. Following MDAH structural review in 2023–24, the building was razed in **2024**, leaving only the historic corner lot and archival memory. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

### Haunted history / lore

No haunted-place folklore is claimed or carried here. Its historical significance rests entirely on Jim Crow healthcare self-determination, fraternal mutual aid, and Black medical training. [5]
