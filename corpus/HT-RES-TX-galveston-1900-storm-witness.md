# Galveston — the message sent while there was still a wire
Research draft · Texas OP01 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

Weather Bureau forecaster Isaac Cline’s written report describes the September 8, 1900 storm from the office and his home. In his account observer J. L. Cline took readings while Isaac went to the bay and Gulf. The report records the 3:30 p.m. observation: “Gulf rising, water covers streets of about half of city.” It also describes the failure of telegraph wires and the use of a remaining telephone connection to Houston. These are the Weather Bureau’s recorded observations through a digital reproduction of the historic printed report. [3–4]

Cline later writes of a sharp rise at his doorway, people sheltering at his residence and the loss of his wife when the building fell. His report is an eyewitness’s account and also an official employee’s effort to set down a timeline. It is not a count for every neighborhood. Rosenberg Library’s archive preserves a separate letter from a survivor identified as “Charlie” to his wife, dated September 12. The source’s catalog and initial scan page are available. The letter was not fully transcribed in this run. [3–6]

The History Center notes that no exact death total exists, reports the frequently accepted estimate of more than 6,000, records accounts as high as 12,000 and favors 7,000–8,000. It also prints a difference between the federal census count and directory population estimate. The museum offers a way to stay with the human record while the exact totals remain open. [1,6]

## 3 · Then / now

Historic locations were distributed across Galveston. The public physical anchor is the Rosenberg Library Museum’s fourth-floor Great Storm exhibit, drive-up to legal public parking or a short sidewalk approach followed by ordinary visitor access. The present gallery is interpretation. The initial observation and homes are not self-guided tour destinations. [1–2,4,6]

## 4 · What’s still there

Public interior: ordinary fourth-floor exhibit and its film where operating. Research reading rooms have separate hours and no role as visit anchors. Historic home footprints and individual refuge sites are unverified as ordinary public stops. [1–2,4–6]
