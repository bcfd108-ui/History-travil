## 2. The story
**Hook:** The bridge report preserves names. It does not preserve everyone's voice.

**Actually read contemporary primary record:** George S. Morison's *The Bismarck Bridge*, published 1884, includes Appendix M, printed page **44**, a committee report dated **October 21, 1882**, addressed to Morison as chief engineer. The signatories printed there are **L. L. Buck, J. B. Clough, Fredk. W. Gilbert, F. N. Finney, A. J. Merritt and Wm. Watson**. They identify themselves as witnesses and characterize the workmanship as **“eminently satisfactory”**. The ETH/e-rara page's OCR text was retrieved directly and read after browser fetch failed. This is a contemporaneous engineering voice, not an interview with construction laborers. Technical testing instructions are deliberately not reproduced. [5]

**Independent historical framework:** the 2023–24 Historic American Engineering Record documentation names Morison for the original project and Ralph Modjeski for the later rebuilding. It also records contractors, illustrating how construction involved more people and organizations than the lead engineer alone. Its administrative authorship is not a rank-and-file worker account. [6]

**Original interpretation:** A signed report is an act of professional responsibility. It also makes a hierarchy visible: some people's names stand beneath a conclusion while much of the physical labor remains behind collective nouns. Reading the document as labor history means noticing both what it certifies and whom it leaves unnamed. Do not invent a crew member's exhaustion, pride, wages or family merely because the finished structure invites an emotional story.

## 3. Then / now
The 1882 committee document and the 1884 publication date are different events. A later historical report's overall 1880–83 construction span is not necessarily a contradiction of the dated committee record. Completion, testing, approaches and publication need separate labels. [5,6]

BNSF reports that the new bridge entered service on **September 16, 2026**. Its October update describes the old bridge's removal, so the historic record must not be presented as an unchanged current visitor attraction. [3]

The archival reading room offers a lawful place to investigate this change without approaching railway property. An archive preserves evidence; it does not imply preservation in place or public access to the structure depicted.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Immediate mode:** read the historical page remotely, then the short current-status notice. Editorial pacing: 20–30 minutes, not an operator program.
1. Begin with the dated committee's salutation and signatures, not specifications.
2. Notice the distinction between a named engineer, named witnesses and the broader workforce not individually represented.
3. Compare that documentary survival with the present replacement/removal status.
4. **Strongest landing:** the record outlives the structure, but it does not automatically preserve the people who built it. End with that honest gap rather than a made-up worker story.

**Optional archive visit:** after confirming an actual relevant item and retrieval arrangements, use only the public reading room within its opening window. Allow an editorial 45–60-minute research block, subject to staff retrieval. No appointment, reproduction order or staff contact has been made. Closed-day fallback stays remote; do not redirect to the riverfront.
