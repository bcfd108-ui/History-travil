# Bishop Hill State Historic Site — the Swedish Pietist colony of 1846, its church, hotel and Krans paintings
HT-RES-IL-bishop-hill-state-historic-site · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Bishop Hill began as an act of religious defiance in Sweden. Erik Jansson — the name is also spelled Eric Janson and Erik Jansson across the record, and this dossier does not merge the spellings — was a Pietist preacher formed in the Swedish *läsare* (Reader) movement who taught that the Bible was the only true book, that holiness was attainable on earth, and, in the National Register nomination's summary, that he was a prophet raised up to continue the work of Jesus. His open disregard for the ordinances of the state Lutheran Church, his charismatic preaching and his book-burnings brought repeated arrests and imprisonments, and finally the decision to emigrate and build a New Jerusalem in America. An advance party went ahead; Olof Olsson is named in the wider record as the emissary sent to find the ground. [2, 7, 9]

In 1846 four hundred Swedes, primarily from the rural district of Hälsingland, pooled their resources and followed Jansson to Henry County, Illinois. The advance party had purchased eighty acres and a log cabin on a wooded tract known as Red Oak Grove; by the end of the year 696 additional acres had been acquired, including the town site the colonists named Bishop Hill for Jansson's home parish, Biskopskulla, in Uppland. The first settlers reached the prairie in early fall of 1846 after walking, on the state brochure's account, 160 miles from Chicago. Over the next few years some 1,200 Janssonists in total departed for Bishop Hill on the nomination's count — other tellings give 1,400 emigrants, and the figures are printed as a conflict below, not averaged. The colony would come to own some 11,000 acres on the nomination's figure (12,000 in its own continuation sheets) and reach a population of 800 to 1,000, though many who arrived died in the early years or left. A fifth party of more than four hundred arriving in 1847 pushed the population toward 700 before a severe winter brought food shortages and illness, and about 200 colonists left to join a nearby Methodist community. [2, 8, 9]

The colony was communistic in nature, as dictated by Jansson, who supervised all activities and was looked upon by his followers, in the state brochure's plain words, as a second Christ. What the colonists built in fifteen years is the reason the ground survives: between 1846 and 1861 the colony erected nineteen large structures and some thirty smaller residences, barns, wash houses and outbuildings around a public square, housing its people not in log cabins but in apartment buildings that the brochure calls modern and comfortable by the standards of the 1840s. Permanent building began in 1848 with the Colony Church, whose basement and first floor each held ten single-room apartments and whose second floor held the sanctuary, complete with original pews that remain. The Steeple Building (1854) served as the colony's administrative center; the three-story stuccoed-brick Colony Hotel (1852–ca. 1860) served commercial travelers and was the colony's link to the outside world; and between 1848 and 1861 Bishop Hill became, on the brochure's account, a major center of commerce between Rock Island and Peoria. Inside that communal world, a boy who arrived in 1850 at age twelve, Olof Krans (1838–1916), taught himself to paint and left the primitivist canvases that remain, in the brochure's judgment, one of the best chronicles of daily life and personalities at Bishop Hill — a unique visual record of communal life on the Illinois prairie in the 1850s, now held in the site's Museum. [1, 2, 6, 8]

The colony's religious unity broke on violence. In the fall of 1849 Jansson consented to the marriage of his cousin Charlotta Lovisa "Lotta" Jansson to John Root, an outsider. After cholera reached the colony with a new immigrant party, Root wanted to leave with his wife; Lotta did not want to go. There followed a series of kidnappings — Root and others seized Lotta and her newborn son on March 2, 1850 and were pursued and forced to return her; Root seized her again at the Henry County courthouse in Cambridge, where she had come as a witness — and then lawsuits against Jansson. On May 13, 1850, at the Cambridge courthouse, while Jansson spoke with the county clerk, Root shot and killed him. Some followers expected Jansson to rise on the third day; he did not. Root was convicted of manslaughter and served about a year. Management passed to a board of seven trustees, headed in the wider record by Jonas Olson; the colony was incorporated under an Illinois charter dated January 17, 1853. In 1850 Jansson had also sent nine followers to California to prospect in the Gold Rush for the colony's support. [2, 5, 7, 9]

Dissolution came by economics and dissension, not by a single day. The colony prospered for several years under the trustees, then suffered in the financial crisis of 1857; colonists learned that Olson had secretly speculated in now-worthless railroad and bank stock, and the community split into factions. The wider record gives the men of the community voting to dissolve in 1858, a slow dissolution running 1858–1862 in the university account, and the formal dissolution in 1861, when property was divided among the members — all three datings stand in this dossier as printed variance. Many residents left; those who remained became members of other churches, and Bishop Hill continued as an incorporated village on the colony's plan. [5, 7, 9]

The ground's second life is preservation. Widespread interest began as early as 1946, when Bishop Hill became an Illinois State Memorial; the historic district was added to the National Register of Historic Places on April 17, 1970 (NRIS 70000244) and designated a National Historic Landmark District on April 27, 1984. The 1984 landmark nomination — Kathleen Lidfors, National Park Service, February 1, 1984 — counts twenty-one colony structures (1846–1861) in a district of roughly 200 acres (the form's geographical data gives approximately 150 acres; both figures stand), thirteen of them major buildings retaining a high degree of integrity, and frames the significance exactly: outstanding value for interpreting immigration and ethnic heritage and nineteenth-century communitarian societies, with an extensive material record of records, colony-manufactured objects and the Krans paintings. The state today owns four of the buildings and the village park; the Bishop Hill Heritage Association holds the Steeple Building Museum and other colony buildings, and the village remains a living town inside its own landmark district — descendants, shops and congregations among the colony fabric, not a fenced park. Many consider the Jansonist emigration the beginning of Swedish America, and the nomination calls Bishop Hill the landmark Swedish settlement in western Illinois, leading to the large Swedish-American communities in Galesburg, Rock Island and Chicago. [1, 2, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The village of Bishop Hill, Henry County, Illinois, on a hill surrounded by rolling prairie and bounded by the Edwards River (South Edwards River) on the north, about two miles north of U.S. Route 34. The colony town was laid out around a public square on which the major colony buildings faced; the landmark district comprises about fifty percent of the village as platted in 1866 and nearly eighty percent of the developed town. [1, 2]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Bishop Hill State Historic Site (IDNR), 304 S. Bishop Hill St., Bishop Hill, IL 61419 — the state-owned Colony Church, Colony Hotel, Boys Dormitory, Colony barn, village park and Museum, visited self-guided (Church and Museum) or by appointment (Hotel) per the state page checked this run, with hours in conflict across listings and the Museum flagged "(Once Reopened)" on that page — confirm before driving. The district's public streets and the state-owned village park are lawful ground regardless of building hours. The Bishop Hill Heritage Association's Steeple Building Museum, 103 N. Bishop Hill St., is a separate, complementary anchor with its own seasonal hours (see table). Bishop Hill is a lived-in village: private homes and yards inside the district are not part of any visit. [1, 4, 5, 6]

## 5. Public progression

Start at the state Museum if it is open — it is the visitor center, and its ten-minute orientation video is also the site's own answer for visitors who cannot climb to the Church sanctuary or the Hotel's upper rooms. Take the Krans paintings slowly: they are not decoration but the colony's own visual archive, painted by a man who arrived as a twelve-year-old colonist in 1850 and stayed to the dissolution. Then cross to the Colony Church and read it as housing first and sanctuary second — ten single-room apartments below, ten more on the first floor, worship above — because that stacking is the commune's theology in floor plan: no family houses, no private hearths, one body. Make the Hotel appointment if staff can take you; it was the colony's deliberate opening to the outside world, the place where commercial travelers slept inside a commune that otherwise held all things in common. Read the Boys Dormitory and the relocated barn from the street, honestly labeled — one small, one moved — and finish in the village park among the settler and Civil War memorials, then walk the square's facades, where the south and west lines stand virtually unchanged from the colony period on the nomination's assessment. The Steeple Building, under the Heritage Association, is the natural second stop on a longer day. **Strongest story moment · The prophet's empty chair.** On May 13, 1850 the colony's prophet — a man his followers held to be a second Christ — was shot dead in a county courthouse over one family's marriage, and the commune he had ruled absolutely had to learn, within days, to govern itself by a board of trustees. Everything standing at Bishop Hill was built either by his certainty or by the fifteen years of ordinary people carrying on without it. [1, 2, 5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Hours conflict, printed in full and not merged: the IDNR site page checked this run prints NO opening hours at all. The site's Google listing gives Wednesday–Sunday 9 a.m.–4 p.m. The village's historic-locations page gives the Colony Church March–October Wednesday–Sunday 10 a.m.–5 p.m. and November–February Wednesday–Sunday 10 a.m.–4 p.m. The gate carries all three statements and routes the visit through a confirm-by-phone step; no single pattern is asserted. [1, 6]
- Museum status unresolved: the IDNR page states visitors can make self-guided tours of "the Colony Church and the Museum (Once Reopened)." Whether the Museum building was closed for a season, a repair, or a longer term is not stated on the page, and no reopening date is published there. The dossier prints the wording verbatim and does not promise the Krans collection is viewable on any given day. [1]
- Steeple Building winter-hours conflict across the operator's own postings: one BHHA winter post states the Steeple Building is open weekdays 10 a.m.–4 p.m. and closed on weekends, reopening weekends in April 2026; a separate BHHA posting for the winter starting November 15 gives the Steeple closing at 4 p.m. on weekdays with weekend hours noon–4 p.m. both Saturday and Sunday (Julmarknad and Lucia Nights excepted). The village page gives Monday–Friday 10–5, Saturday 11–4, Sunday 12–4. All three stand; confirm at 309-927-3899. [4, 5, 6]
- Hotel name variance: the IDNR narrative calls the 1852–ca. 1860 building the Colony Hotel; the same page's accessibility section calls it the Bjorklund Hotel (also spelled Björklund in wider listings). The dossier uses both names for one building and does not treat them as two hotels. [1]
- Founder-name spellings: Eric Janson (IDNR), Erik Jansson (university and Augustana records), Eric Jansson (Britannica and the IHLC account) all appear for the same man (1808–1850). The dossier's Story uses Erik Jansson and prints the variance here; no spelling is asserted as the sole correct form. [1, 5, 7, 9]
- Emigrant and population figures vary: the nomination gives 400 in the first party, some 1,200 Janssonists departing in total, eventual landholding of 11,000 acres in its significance text and 12,000 acres in its continuation sheets, and population 800–1,000; the IHLC account gives 1,200 followers immigrating; another telling in this run gives 1,400 colonists emigrating. All figures stand as printed; none is averaged. [2, 5, 9]
- District acreage variance: the nomination's introduction gives approximately 200 acres; its geographical-data section gives approximately 150 acres. Both stand. [2]
- Dissolution dating variance: formal dissolution and property division in 1861 (nomination period 1846–1861; Britannica); a community vote to dissolve in 1858 (wider Jansson record); dissolution running slowly between 1858 and 1862 (university account). The dossier dates the colony 1846–1861 as the formal span and prints the 1858 vote and the 1858–1862 wind-down alongside. [2, 5, 9]
- State-site address framing: listings give the site address as 304 S. Bishop Hill St. and as 304 S. Bishop Hill Rd., PO Box 104; the state page checked this run prints no street address. The dossier uses 304 S. Bishop Hill St. from the current listing data and flags the variant. Phone 309-927-3345 is carried on the state brochure and listings; the Heritage Association's separate number is 309-927-3899. [1, 6, 8]
- No National Register asset-detail page for NRIS 70000244 was separately opened this run; the Register facts here rest on the nomination PDF itself (text and photos assets), which is the stronger source in any case. [2, 3]
