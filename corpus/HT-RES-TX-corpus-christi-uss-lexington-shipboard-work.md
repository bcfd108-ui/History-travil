# USS Lexington CV-16 — the crew that made flying possible
Research draft · Texas OP04 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

### Two ships shared the name

The National Archives has distinct log entries for Lexington (CV-2) and Lexington (CV-16). CV-2’s series ends May 8, 1942. For CV-16 the published wartime list gives Feb. 17, 1943 through June 30, 1945. These are the coverage dates in this list and do not give the full span of service. The museum identifies the Corpus Christi ship as the later carrier, commissioned Feb. 17, 1943, and describes the original intended name Cabot. Its later training and museum use explain why a wartime site can move to a new home [3–4].

### A pilot remembered the rest of the ship working

Torpedo Squadron 16 pilot Warren McLellan remembered training on the new Lexington and flight operations in the Pacific. In his 2007 interview he recalled a landing emergency: “There were crashes on the flight decks that held us up.” Read this as a named veteran’s later account, checked in the museum’s PDF transcript. His account then puts an airplane, the forward elevator and other crew in the same narrow space. Pilot, signal officer, deck handlers and elevator crew shared a workplace, and a single delay or mishap could affect the whole deck. Read the losses in silence [5].

### One photograph gives the pause a human scale

In a November 1943 view, Navy photographer Edward Steichen looked down at enlisted deck crew around a small vehicle. In the inspected frame, men stand or sit appreciably close to each other; people and gear occupy the deck at a human scale. The NARA attribution carried by the historical image index identifies them as the gasoline crew during a lull. Unlike an after-action tally, the photograph preserves what the men did while they waited [6–7].

## 3 · Then / now

**Historic place.** The CV-16 hull and shipboard work areas moved with the ship from wartime service to Corpus Christi Bay [3–4].

**Lawful physical anchor.** USS Lexington Museum public entry and signed ship tour, 2914 N. Shoreline Blvd. Drive up to the ordinary visitor approach and take the signed walk/ramp. The operator identifies the flight deck as wheelchair accessible via the LEX LIFT. Ask staff for the open route when conditions change. Closed compartments, ship edges, ladders or levels excluded by staff and operational or maintenance areas are outside the itinerary [1–2].

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. At entry confirm whether the flight deck and lift are open. Start inside at the admitted interpretation if the deck is held [1–2].
2. From the visitor deck path or an open interior exhibit, look at the Navy photo before reading the oral history. In that frame the pause is as important as the machinery [2, 5–7].
3. **Strongest story moment — The pause before the next landing.** From the ordinary public visitor path, let the color photo’s stillness sit beside McLellan’s short recollection. If discussing the reported fatalities, use a quiet pause on the designated public path or in an open exhibit [2, 5–7].

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

McLellan’s retrospective transcript places launch around June 1943 while recalling when his squadron went aboard; the ship had a documented commissioning date of Feb. 17, 1943 in the museum and Archives. The two dates cannot both describe the original launching sequence. Retain June as his recollection of the training/boarding period rather than a proven launch date [3–5]. The named-vessel list was retrieved and read; log images and the specific elevator-accident log entry were not retrieved. NHHC history and image direct opens timed out or returned 503 during this pass; the dated operator history, catalog and pixel-inspected copy were used [3–8].

## 7 · Historic photo record

**Selected for draft review, original-site view.** National Archives and Records Administration, official U.S. Navy photograph 80-G-K-15296, Commander Edward Steichen, USNR, November 1943. Public-domain basis: U.S. Navy official-duty photography. The inspected mirror pixels show deck boards, track/tie-down lines, men in red or pink working tops, and a military vehicle marked U.S.N. 46534. The crew-role attribution comes from the archival caption carried on NavSource. Printed credit: “National Archives and Records Administration, 80-G-K-15296, Commander Edward Steichen, USNR, November 1943.” Elevated camera angle and generic deck lines alone do not establish a lawful repeat position.
