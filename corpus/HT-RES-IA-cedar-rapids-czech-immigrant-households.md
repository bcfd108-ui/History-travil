# Cedar Rapids — The language the family kept

Iowa OP04 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

**A carpenter made a house fit his family.** Josef and Helena Sleger moved into the little house in the 1890s according to the visitor-services interview preserved in a 2017 student project. Fricke identified the parlor addition, connecting hall, closet and added entrance as work Josef undertook after purchasing the home from an earlier family. A photograph of the couple and their three children was the one original family item Fricke identified in the furnishings then on view. Two children were born in Bohemia, the youngest in Cedar Rapids. Furniture interpreted how the home might have been used. The project distinguishes furnishings from family provenance and reports that a layer of historic paint revealed after the 2008 flood informed repainting. The kitchen ceiling was replaced after flood damage. [3, 4]

**A schoolbook outlived its lessons.** Martha Kralik remembered studying in Czech between 1898 and 1905. A Library of Congress fieldwork report recovers her recollection of strict teachers, reciting and acting alongside lacemaking, knitting and embroidery. Her family kept the 1881 Czech grammar she used. Martha married the immigrant Joseph Kralik and sent daughters to the school she had attended. The report follows Czech instruction into the great-grandsons' generation. It preserves the fieldworker’s account of the interview. These are the Kraliks' experiences, independent of the Slegers' house. [5]

**A later arrival tells it in her own words.** The museum's published Vera Plesek interview excerpt shifts the story to the twentieth century. She left Czechoslovakia in 1969, took work in Cedar Rapids, received a sentence in absentia after staying in the United States, and worked for decades in hospital radiology. In recalling a visit to a Czech-speaking social circle she said, “I want to be Czech again”. She also recalled her husband sitting through the evening without the promised translation. Read the interview as her own memory of what a language included and what it could shut someone out of. [6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic place.** The Sleger house is workmen's housing from Cedar Rapids, now interpreted at the museum. The precise earlier parcel and move chronology were not verified independently. The earlier household, the Kralik school experience and Plesek's later home and work occupy different places and years. [3–6]

**Lawful public anchor.** National Czech & Slovak Museum & Library, 1400 Inspiration Place SW, Cedar Rapids. Drive up and use the signed public museum entrance in regular hours. Use only posted open visitor paths and public exhibit rooms. Skip the house interior unless opened by the museum during an ordinary visit. The separately scheduled library is a source, with no reading-room stop. [1, 2]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

Direct reads of the operator's visit page, homepage and Czech-collection finding aid returned HTTP 403. Indexed excerpts from the operator carried the gallery and holiday schedule. The interview and catalog pages and the fieldwork report were readable. The 2017 student project places the Sleger move in the early 1890s, and its homepage describes migration more broadly as during the 1890s. The exact arrival, transfer and house-move years need deeds or a museum object record. Full fieldwork interview tape and Sleger original family image accession were not retrieved. [1, 3–6]
