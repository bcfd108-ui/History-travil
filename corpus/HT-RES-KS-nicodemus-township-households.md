# Nicodemus — a household becomes a town

Kansas OP01 · Draft research for Dash · November 19, 2026

## 2. The story

### A claim had to be proved on paper

The National Archives traces Zachary Fletcher through separate sets of records. The 1880 census records him in Nicodemus Township with Jenny Smith Fletcher and their sons Thomas and Joseph. The land case file records a dugout, an unfinished stone house and what the Archives describes as 50 acres of braking. The pension file records something the census columns could not explain. In his autobiographical pension letter, Fletcher wrote, “I being raised a slave, I have no record of my age”. The Archives publishes the letter in reproduction and transcription. The sentence is his account of a missing record, written for an office that required him to establish an identity and age. [3]

### Work at a house, a shop and a school

NPS records Jenny and Zachary’s arrival on July 30, 1877 in the first group of settlers. Jenny is also called Francis in the agency’s biographical account. The first arrivals and the larger later migration are distinct events. Zachary and his brother Thomas built the St. Francis Hotel in 1881. NPS describes a restaurant in the hotel and a general store and post office on the same lot. The family’s dugout and hotel also hosted school classes before a school building opened. Fletcher’s letters and land paperwork give one household’s experience out of several. Jenny’s own testimony was not retrieved and the labor of the household cannot be assigned from the names on a federal form alone. [3,4]

### The return of a descendant

In Teresa Switzer’s May 30, 2011 oral history, Priscilla Bates speaks about the long connection her family kept with Nicodemus after living elsewhere. Asked about the meaning of the town she answered, “Nicodemus is home.” NPS publishes the interview transcript and notes that some interviews are edited for time. Her recollection belongs beside the atlas. The 1906 atlas shows township land ownership at one scale and town lots at another, while the interview describes the continuing place of a home a person can return to. [5,6]

## 3. Then / now

The Township Hall is a documented public interpretation venue. The historic town and township include more buildings and land than that visitor space. The Library of Congress atlas and published HABS documentation place settlement evidence on the page. They do not establish public access to a listed building, lot or claim. [1,2,6–8]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- An NPS legacy feature describes the visitor center as open daily 9 a.m.–4:30 p.m. except major holidays. The current operating schedule states that it is closed Tuesdays and Wednesdays and posts the November 26 holiday closure. Use the current schedule. [1,2,9]
- NARA cites Fletcher’s reported August 1845 birth information, and the pension letter says he lacked a record of his age. Keep attribution and uncertainty attached. The separate census and affidavit materials do not identify where every member of the household worked. [3,4]
- The Archives’ published account and letter reproduction were retrieved. The full original homestead packet and final patent image for Fletcher were not retrieved. The Kansas Memory item 526 direct text returned a three-line page with no item description. The underlying historic image could not be viewed there. [3,10]

## 7. Historical photo record

The Library of Congress publication provides a historical ownership atlas and exhibit imagery including the Fletcher-Switzer House, with related HABS material. The atlas is dated 1906 and is a map. The direct Kansas Memory image retrieval did not provide a usable frame. No public standing point for a matched historical photograph was established. No Photo Point. [6,7,10]
