# Fort Raleigh and the Roanoke Island Freedmen’s Colony

**1. Trip gate — Checked November 21, 2026 Pacific. Use the Lindsay Warren Visitor Center at 1500 Fort Raleigh Road, Manteo. NPS lists the indoor visitor center open daily, 9 a.m.–5 p.m., closed Christmas Day. Use its paved public lot and signed entrance, and check current park alerts before travel. This reflection visit ends indoors. Battlefield, reconstructed earthwork, shore, cemetery and tribal ground are outside the visit. [1–4]**

## 2. Story

After Union forces occupied Roanoke Island in 1862, people escaping slavery came to the island. The Freedmen’s Colony was established in 1863. Superintendent Horace James’s official report did two things at once. It celebrated the school and refuge, then acknowledged that the community could not support itself under the conditions it faced. He attributed the dependence on rations to limited nearby jobs, uncleared land, a poor fishing season and the enlistment of many working men. His report describes an administrator’s version of a place where families had their own judgments and demands. [5–6]

Richard Etheridge and William Benson were among the men serving in the 36th U.S. Colored Infantry. Writing to Union General Oliver O. Howard in a letter dated approximately May or June 1865 by the transcriber, they began from service they had already given and promises about their families. Their words were direct: “our family’s are suffering at Roanoke Island, N.C.” Their petition challenged how the colony was administered. James’s report remains evidence for his claims. The soldiers’ letter is evidence that at least these petitioners disputed the conditions his account praised. [5, 7]

In December 1866, Joseph Tillett and other signers appealed to the Freedmen’s Bureau on behalf of longtime island residents. They described a living made by “fishing, fowling and ‘progging’” and asked to remain, offering to rent or purchase land. They explained why removal to mainland farm labor would take them away from skills they had practiced since youth. The petition links a legal dispute over land to work the signers themselves chose to describe. It gives those residents their names and words, while leaving the disposition of each family’s case for further research. [8–9]

## 3. Then and now

Fort Raleigh preserves and interprets several histories on northern Roanoke Island. The sixteenth-century Indigenous–English encounters and the nineteenth-century Freedmen’s Colony are separate in time, place and evidence. The park’s mission and archaeological limits do not locate any particular signer’s cabin. The present visitor center was constructed in 1965 and dedicated in 1966. NPS’s landscape plan distinguishes the building and plaza from the earthwork, monuments, cemetery and shore. Its indoor exhibits interpret the colony alongside the park’s other histories. [3–4, 6]

## 4. What survives and where a visitor may stand

- Lindsay Warren Visitor Center: operator-identified paved lot, marked approach and indoor exhibits. It is the only visitor anchor in this entry. [1–4]
- Original colony ground and individual dwelling or burial locations: documentary context. No visitor direction is inferred from the land petition or the location of the wider park. [4, 8–9]
- Original letters and petition: archival or digitized evidence for reading. No archive or reading room is a trip stop.

## 5. Strongest visit moment

Go from the visitor lot straight into the indoor interpretation. Read the Etheridge–Benson protest first and the later land petition second. **Tillett and the other signers explaining how they made their living on the island** is the strongest moment. It turns an administrative statement about relocation into a choice that residents understood in terms of work, land and home. [7–9]

## 6. Evidence limits and disagreements

The 1865 letter’s month is supplied approximately by the transcription. The 1866 petition was read in a freely available transcription pointing to the Freedmen’s Bureau records; the federal resource study reproduces the petition as an additional trace to the original. A full original-ink comparison and a subsequent ruling on the petition were not completed. An administrator’s 1864 report and the soldiers’ 1865 protest record differing perspectives and conditions at different times. The difference cannot establish one uniform experience for everyone. NPS’s 2025 count of 266,775 applies to the unit, with no separate indoor door count established. [5, 7–10]
