## 2. The story
**Stewardship includes the authority to say how a homeland and its people are named.** That is this dossier's interpretation, grounded in a public statement issued by the Washoe Tribe itself, not a land manager's recreation description.

On **September 13, 2021**, the **Washoe Tribe of Nevada and California** published *Washoe Tribe Commends Resort in Olympic Valley on Rename*. The release supports the change to Palisades Tahoe after years of effort to remove a derogatory term from the former name. It quotes **Serrell Smokey, identified in that dated statement as Tribal Chairman**: **“we have great reverence for our ancestors, history and lands.”** It also identifies Tribal Historic Preservation Officer **Darrel Cruz** and past and present Tribal leaders as participants in the relationship with resort management. [3] This is a named voice directly published by the Tribe, rather than an unattributed tourism paraphrase. It describes a 2021 event in California; it is contextual reading, not a California stop added to Nevada Division work.

The release frames Tahoe and the surrounding area as Washoe ancestral homelands. The importance is not simply that a resort changed its marketing. It records a sovereign community publicly asserting the relationship between place, dignity and historical memory. A name change is not treated here as land return, resolution of every grievance or permission for visitors to enter cultural places.

The Tribe's own history page emphasizes continuing presence and responsibility to care for the homeland. The page's substantive text was available through the official search index; direct opening returned a JavaScript shell. [4] Its framing is retained as an attributed Tribal account, not converted into a universal account for neighboring Nations. The Washoe Tribe is distinct from the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe and other Paiute, Shoshone and Goshute governments.

The museum's basketry exhibition offers a separate, tangible public encounter with named creative work. Its description identifies **Louisa Keyser, also known as Datsolalee**, as a Washoe artist. [1] Do not make a basket into a generic illustration of a vanished people or attach an invented meaning to its design. The visitor can pay attention to the artist's name, the museum's source credit and how a public interpretation is voiced. The displayed object and the Tribe's 2021 statement are different kinds of evidence, not interchangeable accounts.

The strongest story moment is recognizing that a collection in a public museum and a contemporary Tribal statement belong in the same inquiry. History is not over when an object enters a case. Who interprets it, names the place and determines what should remain protected still matters.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
A single public-gallery reflection sequence, not a lakeside tour:

1. **Before arrival:** verify the museum's actual date-specific opening and the gallery's availability. If unresolved, use the source-reading version instead.
2. **Start with authorship:** read the Tribe's 2021 statement and identify whose voice is speaking, its date and what the statement does and does not claim.
3. **At the gallery, if open:** notice one named artist and one interpretive credit. Follow posted display rules; touch only expressly designated interactive features, never collection objects.
4. **Compare kinds of evidence:** an artist's work, a museum caption and a Tribal statement do different jobs. Do not make any one of them stand for all Washoe people or all periods.
5. **End with the strongest moment:** care for a homeland includes ongoing authority over representation, not merely admiration of a view. The experience ends without seeking access to protected places.

Optional onsite question, not sent: **How were Washoe voices involved in this gallery, and how can a visitor distinguish the Tribe's interpretation from the museum's explanatory text?** No staff interview or special access is promised.
