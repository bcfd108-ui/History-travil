# Scotts Bluff and Mitchell Pass — the road the wagons chose

DRAFTED · Nebraska OP02 · Pending independent Dash QC

## 2. The story

### A bluff a traveler had to get around

Coming west on the North Platte, a traveler could see the bluffs before being able to read a route through them. In a June 9, 1849 entry reproduced in the NPS handbook, Alonzo Delano described “many fantastic shapes” as his party approached. The handbook places the main wagon movement of these years farther south through Robidoux Pass. It quotes Bennett Clark's 1850 description of bluffs that “sweep around rain-bow like.” These are published reproductions of period voices, with the day and route arguments coming from the surrounding handbook. [4,5]

### A shorter passage made practical

The Park Service dates construction of a wagon road through Devil's Gap, later called Mitchell Pass, to 1850. It quotes William C. Lobestine describing the pass as made usable in 1851 and saving eight miles. The Nebraska State Historical Society's journal article reproduces his account from a printed diary edition and compares it with the older route. Construction, first successful use, and wider adoption are distinct claims. A reader should retain both years. [4,6]

Elias Smith's August 13, 1851 journal provides a different kind of evidence. “Road first best,” reads the Church History Library's published transcription. His party went about 17 miles that day. One group lagged after stopping to set wagon tires in the morning. The dated entry names Scotts Bluff, and the precise cut he used is unconfirmed in that passage. A good road and a working wagon were separate problems, and the 17 miles describe a day's travel, not the distance between today's visitor centers. [7]

### The shape of the decision

The present visitor area makes the choice legible without using the summit. From the open section of designated Oregon Trail path near the visitor center, or from the public base viewpoint, compare the opening between the bluffs with the country an earlier party had to go around. The later motor road and its tunnels are a different chapter in crossing the bluff. Period observers can speak for their own journeys. A place-specific Tribe-published account is an open research item. No site of Indigenous activity is a stop here. [2–4,8]

## 3. Then / now

Wagon parties shifted from an older detour to the usable gap in the early 1850s. The monument preserves traces of the trail and interprets the pass from designated ground. The modern county road and the separate 1930s Summit Road also mark the landscape, with different purposes and access rules. [2–4,8,9]

1. Start at the visitor center base area. Compare the wagon route and county road on the map. [1,2,4]
2. Read Delano’s 1849 approach and Lobestine’s account of the later shortcut from an open wayside or inside. [4–6]
3. **Strongest story moment — Read the gap.** Face Mitchell Pass from a designated public base viewpoint. Read Smith's tire-delay entry, then consider what eight fewer miles meant to a party with equipment to repair. If snow or wind prevents the exterior stop, use the landscape image and map indoors or remotely. [1,4,6–8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- NPS dates road work to 1850. Lobestine's published diary recollection dates practical passage to 1851. NPS spells the diarist’s surname Lobenstine, while the Nebraska History article’s citation prints Lobestine. No source retrieved here fixes the exact day of opening or every party's route. [4,6,7]
- Delano and Clark were read in the handbook reproduction. Smith was read in the archive's transcription. No manuscript inspection for these entries. [5,7]
- A repeat photograph from a safe visitor position remains unverified. Conditions and signs control any outdoor stop, and the NPS Summit Road status needs a separate check. [1–3,8]
