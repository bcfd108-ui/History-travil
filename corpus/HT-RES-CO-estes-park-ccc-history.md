## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A visitor sees a finished landscape. A worker remembers being cold, waiting for bedding, and finally having a job. That difference is the story.

**Named participant voice:** Lloyd K. Musselman's NPS administrative history quotes **Battell Loomis**, identified as an unemployed magazine writer and early CCC enrollee in Rocky Mountain National Park. In the reproduced passage, Loomis describes arrivals without adequate warm clothing and delayed supplies. One short sentence conveys the mismatch between an organized program and lived conditions: **“They were in their own worn clothes, many without coats or sweaters.”** [3]

The chapter's notes trace the passage to Loomis's **“With the Green Guard,” Liberty, April 29, 1934, pages 52–53**. That bibliographic date is reproduced as printed in the administrative history; the original magazine issue has not been independently inspected. The evidence is a participant's published account quoted within an official history, accessed through an independent mirror, not a newly retrieved diary or an audio recording heard here. [4]

**Institutional context:** The park's own CCC overview describes a program that began there in 1933 and continued for nine years, with work spanning visitor facilities, communications, maintenance and interpretation. That account emphasizes restored confidence as well as physical improvements. Its quoted unnamed participant is not substituted for the named Loomis account. [5]

**Interpretive synthesis:** Relief work had both a public result and a household meaning. Rather than make the park look as though it simply arrived ready for visitors, this stop asks whose work made a visitor experience possible. It also resists a flawless success story: material hardship and administrative delay belong beside opportunity.

This is specifically an Estes Park/Rocky Mountain account. No experience from Longmire or Silver Falls has been transferred here. Nor does a federal labor narrative establish that the landscape lacked earlier human histories. No unsourced Indigenous speaker or generic Tribal account is inserted to fill that gap.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the reproduced participant account makes everyday needs visible within an ambitious relief program. [3,4]

**Now:** the Town provides an open local-history museum, while NPS publishes a CCC history and a period photograph online. [1,5,6] These resources allow a visitor to place a human account alongside institutional memory without approaching a former camp.

The museum is the public interpretation anchor, not the original location of Loomis's account. No surviving bunk, work tool or building there has been verified as his. There is no present photograph aligned to the historical scene and no claim that a current view is unchanged.
