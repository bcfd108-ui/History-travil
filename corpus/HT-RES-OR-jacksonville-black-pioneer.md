## 2. The story

A blacksmith's surname can disappear even while the mountain associated with him remains on the map. Historian **Zachary Stocks** identifies **Ben Johnson** in Jackson County's 1868–69 assessment records at **Uniontown**, near today's Ruch. His work belonged to the Applegate mining economy, not a documented downtown Jacksonville household. Stocks connects his later life to **Amanda Gardner Johnson**, whom he married in Albany in 1870. The couple's home together must not be relocated to Jacksonville for a convenient tour. [2]

Amanda's published recollection supplies a human voice: **“I was afraid to accept my liberty, so I came to Oregon with my owners.”** Oregon Black Pioneers republishes her interview with Fred Lockley. She explains fear of kidnapping or false accusations in Missouri, recalls cooking on the overland journey, paid work in Oregon and her marriage. This is remembered testimony mediated by an interviewer and a later transcription, not an audio recording heard here. Her apparent choice was constrained by enslavement and insecurity. [3]

For a household actually associated with Jacksonville, **Harriet Johnston** is the stronger local lead. Jackson County Library Services' published description of SOHS archivist **Jan Wright's** 2025 lecture identifies her arrival in the early 1860s with a family to raise. The description was read; the full lecture was not watched or transcribed. No children’s names, invented dialogue, exact home or relationship to Ben and Amanda is asserted. Johnston and Johnson are not interchangeable surnames. [4]

The interpretive connection is an original synthesis: a visitor can see a preserved town without assuming that its best-preserved buildings represent all the people who made a life there. Labor, marriage and care belong in the story alongside mining fortunes. This draft offers a source-backed reading at a public history anchor, **not a claim that the center currently displays these biographies**.

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** Ben sharpened miners' tools and worked as a blacksmith in the Applegate area. Historic Jacksonville's community history credits archivist Jan Wright with recovering his identity. [5]

**Now:** Oregon Black Pioneers publishes Ben and Amanda's story and portrait together, placing their married life in Albany. Their relationship gives the regional story a personal scale without making a Jacksonville residence out of an Albany one. [6]

Stocks records the federal approval of **Ben Johnson Mountain** in 2020, replacing an earlier racialized name. The change restores a person's identity; it does not by itself establish a legal or safe November mountain visit. [2]

The public center is an orientation setting, not a surviving Johnson home, shop or memorial dedicated to Harriet. No historical/modern photograph pair verified.
