# Joplin — The air they worked in
HT-RES-MO-joplin-tri-state-mining-workers · Draft for independent Dash review · Quiet witness

## 2. Story

On May 25, 1916, a man named Samuel Hubbard had to be raised out of a mine in a bucket. The Joplin Public Library recounts that foul air overcame him. The summary preserves the worker’s name and the rescue, while his own words and the underlying accident report are still missing. Imagine the bucket as the means of return to the surface, where a co-worker could finally see what had happened. [3]

Another danger was carried out on miners’ clothes and into the body a little at a time. The U.S. Public Health Service called out “Silicious rock dust in the underground workings,” in the indexed text of its report. Investigators looked at health and working conditions together. Joplin Public Library describes the investigators’ local work from late 1914 to the middle of July 1915. The federal report puts the investigation’s formal end in December 1915. The two windows describe different portions of the work. A sudden loss of air in an entry and prolonged dust exposure belong to two different stories about working underground. [3–5]

A generation later, Gerald Lynde Clinton still remembered the district. Missouri Southern State University’s catalog identifies his interview with Robert Markman in 1987 and indexes mining and local life. The accessible catalog gives a path back to a person whose account can eventually stand alongside the inspectors’. The linked download returned HTML during this review. Direct quotation awaits the recording or transcript. [6]

At street level the city also made room for people passing through on the road. Joplin’s municipal Route 66 history identifies a former tourist campground in front of today’s museum building. The same public entrance gives a way to read the distinction between a night’s rest on a journey and the effort that kept a local mining household going. [1, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The incidents and medical investigation concern the Tri-State lead and zinc district around Joplin. The precise shaft for Hubbard’s rescue and the postcard location are open evidence questions. The campground site is a distinct historical feature identified by the city. [3–5, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Joplin History & Mineral Museum, 504 S. Schifferdecker Ave., is a drive-up stop with a short visitor walk from designated parking. Keep to the signed museum entrance and open, developed public park approach. The current building and grounds interpret the district. [1, 2]
