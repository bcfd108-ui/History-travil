# HT-RES-NJ-barnegat-lighthouse — Old Barney Against the Sea

Division NJ seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Barnegat Lighthouse — "Old Barney" — stands at the north end of Long Beach Island, on the south side of Barnegat Inlet in Ocean County, New Jersey. The New Jersey State Park Service, which operates Barnegat Lighthouse State Park, describes the station's place in the coastwise system plainly: the site "was regarded as one of the most crucial 'change of course' points for coastal vessels" [1]. The present tower was built in 1857–58 to Lt. George G. Meade's design and first lit on January 1, 1859, and its whole history is a fight with the water it guards: it was built 900 feet from the shore, and today it stands, in the park's words, "at the waters edge, less than 50 feet from the Atlantic Ocean due to beach erosion and the never-ceasing currents flowing around the inlet" [1]. The tower, its first-order Fresnel lens, and the shoreline that has nearly vanished from under both are the record this entry keeps.

## 1. Why this place

Barnegat Inlet was dangerous water long before it had a light. Dutch explorers named the inlet "Barendegat" — Breakers Inlet — for the shoals and breaking seas at its mouth [2]. Vessels sailing for New York from Europe, and coasting vessels turning at the Jersey shore, made their course change here, and the wrecks piled up on the shoals. The park's history carries the count that forced the federal government's hand: in 1854 the wrecks of the Powhattan and the New Era took over 350 lives between them [1]. A light at Barnegat was part of the federal system of seacoast lights meant to let mariners find their way up and down the New Jersey shore [1], and the second tower — a first-class light, among the most powerful on the coast — was built because the first one failed at the job and then fell into the sea. The lighthouse is also a survivor of a second kind: decommissioned in 1927, dark through the Second World War, it was relit on January 1, 2009, exactly 150 years after its first lighting, and is a working aid to navigation again [1].

## 2. What happened here

Congress appropriated $6,000 for the first lighthouse in 1834. The park brochure records that it was completed and placed in service in July 1835 [2]. It did not do the work. The Historic American Buildings Survey's written record describes it as a 50-foot whitewashed brick tower with a fixed white light — the fourth light on the New Jersey coast — refitted in 1855 and lost the following year: "In 1856 the whole structure toppled into the water because of the encroachment of the sea," and a temporary wooden structure was raised with the salvaged light [3]. (The brochure gives the first tower's height as 40 feet; the conflict is printed in §6.) Wrecks continued in the meantime — the Powhattan and the New Era, 1854, over 350 lives [1].

In 1855, Lt. George G. Meade of the Army engineers inspected the station and reported on the old light in terms the park still quotes: it was, he wrote, "without exception, the most indifferent and unreliable light from the Capes of Virginia to Boston" [1]. On Meade's recommendation, Congress appropriated $60,000 in 1856 for a first-class seacoast light [2]. Meade designed the new tower and Lt. W. S. Raynolds supervised its construction [2]. The HABS record dates the work: begun in 1857, finished in 1858, at a total cost of $60,000 — $45,000 for the tower and $15,000 for the light [3].

The tower the engineers built is a masonry argument against the sea. The HABS description: "A circular tower about 160 feet high of brick, walls 10 feet thick at the base and 18 inches at the top," with a heavy iron pipe anchored vertically at its center and the spiral stair — cast-iron treads — threaded on the pipe, windows at different levels for light and lookout, and a fenced platform ringing the lantern [3]. The State Park Service gives the tower's height as 163 feet [1]. Its 217 steps climb to a lantern that first held a first-order Fresnel lens made by Henri LePaute of Paris in 1856, its glass from St. Gobain: six feet in diameter, ten feet high, about five tons, built of — by the park's count — 1,027 prisms in 24 bull's-eye panels, turning once every four minutes to throw a flash every ten seconds, visible 19 nautical miles [1][2]. The tower still wears its daymark, red over white, so the station can be identified by day [1].

The station was a worked place, not a monument. The keeper's residence housed the keeper and two assistants, who stood four-hour watches around the clock [1]. The HABS photograph carried with this dossier shows that station as it stood in the survey's day: the tower and the big porch-fronted dwelling together at the water's edge, the eroded bluff already cut to a cliff beside them [3].

The sea kept its side of the argument. The tower had been built 900 feet from the shore; by the time of the HABS survey its condition was recorded in four words: "In danger of being washed away" [3]. Around the turn of the century the Lighthouse Board considered abandoning the tower for a lightship; local opposition and the light's popularity held it, temporary measures followed, and shore residents raised $2,000 toward the permanent jetties that finally armored the point [2]. In 1926 the lighthouse and its reservation were transferred from the federal government to the State of New Jersey, to be maintained for historical purposes and for the preservation of the tower [2]. (The HABS record dates the gift to 1925; see §6.) In 1927 the Barnegat Lightship took over the station's work offshore. The original lens was removed that year and sent to the lighthouse depot at Tompkinsville, on Staten Island [3]; the tower kept lesser lights for a time — a gas light, then, when electricity reached the island, a 250-watt bulb visible about eight miles [3] — and was extinguished during the Second World War [1]. Barnegat Lighthouse State Park opened to the public in 1957; a bust of General Meade was unveiled at the dedication [2].

The last chapter so far is a return. The Friends of Barnegat Lighthouse State Park raised the funds to buy a new U.S. Coast Guard–approved lens, and on January 1, 2009 — 150 years to the day after the first lighting — the tower was lit again, its beam visible up to 22 nautical miles, a formal aid to navigation once more [1]. The original LePaute lens survived its travels and can be visited today at the Barnegat Light Historical Society's Schoolhouse Museum in the borough [1].

## 3. What's gone

The first lighthouse is gone entirely — toppled into the inlet in 1856, its light salvaged into a temporary wooden tower while the present one rose [3]. The shoreline the station was built behind is gone: of the 900 feet of ground between the tower and the sea in 1859, fewer than 50 remain, and the park reads the loss to beach erosion and the inlet's currents [1]. The lightship era is gone; the Barnegat Lightship that replaced the tower in 1927 no longer rides offshore [2][3]. The keeper's dwelling that stands beside the tower in the HABS photograph is not among the structures the park describes today; no source checked for this dossier states when it left the site, and the gap is printed in §6 rather than filled. The original lens's working life is over — the lens itself survives, but in a museum in town, not in the lantern [1].

## 4. What's still there

The tower itself, 163 feet of brick on its armored point, wearing its red-over-white daymark, and climbable — 217 steps to the lantern, with the view over Barnegat Bay, Island Beach, and Long Beach Island at the top [1]. The light, relit in 2009 and working [1]. The Interpretive Center, whose "Story of Barnegat Lighthouse" exhibit runs from shipwrecks to first-class seacoast light, and which carries live views from four cameras at the top for visitors who do not climb [1]. The Maritime Forest Trail, a 0.2-mile self-guided loop through one of the last maritime forests on Long Beach Island — black cherry, sassafras, eastern red cedar, and American holly [1]. The 1,033-foot concrete walkway on the south jetty along the inlet, built for fishing and for standing over the water the light was raised against [2]. The bust of Meade at the park, unveiled at the 1957 dedication [2]. And in the borough, the original 1856 LePaute lens at the Schoolhouse Museum [1].

## 5. Standing there

Visitor order, conditional on season and weather; the lighthouse is open daily 9:00 a.m. to 3:00 p.m., weather permitting, and seasonal schedules vary — the park page is the check before a trip [1].

1. **Interpretive Center.** The "Story of Barnegat Lighthouse" first — wrecks, the failed first light, Meade, and the lens — then the tower [1].
2. **The climb.** 217 steps to the lantern; children must be accompanied by an adult. The live camera views in the Interpretive Center carry the same view for anyone who stays down [1].
3. **The tower's base and the waterline.** Stand where the HABS photograph was taken in spirit: tower, point, and the <50 feet of shore where 900 used to be [1][3].
4. **The south jetty walkway.** 1,033 feet of concrete along the inlet — the breakers' side of Barendegat [2].
5. **Maritime Forest Trail.** The 0.2-mile loop through what the barrier island was before the borough [1].
6. **The Schoolhouse Museum, in town.** The original lens, under a separate operator; call ahead for its hours [1][2].

## 6. Unresolved / gaps

- **The first tower's height.** The park brochure gives 40 feet [2]; the HABS written record gives a 50-foot tower, built 1834 [3]. The brochure dates completion and first service to July 1835 [2]. The figures print side by side, unreconciled.
- **The present tower's date.** HABS: begun 1857, finished 1858, "Date of Erection: 1858" [3]. The park names the construction year as 1859 in its telling [1] and the Register record carries 1857 as a significant year [4]. What every source agrees on is the first lighting: January 1, 1859 [1][2]. This dossier dates the build 1857–58 and the light 1859.
- **Heights of tower and light.** Tower: 163 feet (State Park Service [1]) against "about 160 feet" (HABS [3]). The light's focal height: the brochure gives 175 feet above sea level; the HABS record puts its kerosene light "one hundred and sixty-three feet above sea level" [3]. Both spreads print.
- **The lens's prism count.** The park history page counts 1,027 prisms [1]; the brochure says "over 1,000" prisms and twenty-four bull's-eye lenses [2]; a state news account of the relighting says 1,000. No single count is adopted.
- **The transfer year.** The brochure: transferred to the State in 1926 [2]. The HABS record: the lighthouse "belongs to the State of New Jersey having been presented to it by the federal authorities in 1925" [3]. The park history page says only that the lens was removed in 1927, "shortly after the lighthouse was turned over to the State" [1]. Unreconciled.
- **The keeper's dwelling.** It stands in the HABS photograph and in no current park description. No source checked states when it was removed. Blank, not guessed.
- **No verified point.** No primary source checked for this dossier carries a surveyed point for the station; the Register record locates it by description only ("N end of Long Beach Island, off Broadway Ave.") [4]. §10 leaves the coordinates blank.
