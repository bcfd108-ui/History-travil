## 2. Story

Arlington’s earliest documents leave its construction open to question. The federal nomination reproduces two words from an 1818 deed to Jane Surget White: “extensive improvements.” It records Jonathan Thompson as the previous owner. In the same nomination, the authors identify a tradition that John Hampton White designed the house for Jane in the years around 1816–20. An improvement on a deed can be a house, work undertaken on it, or some other substantial investment. The surviving phrase provides evidence of development by 1818 without identifying the crew or settling exactly what stood there. The deed itself was not retrieved. [1]

The architectural survey documented a tall portico, marble details at the entrances and delicate carved panels over interior doorways. A two-story brick building housed a kitchen and separate quarters, and a service porch belonged to the working side of the house. The nomination’s room names tell where some of the work took place. They leave the people who cooked, carried, cleaned and maintained the grounds largely invisible in this record. The individual workers and their legal status remain an open archival question. [1–2]

On September 15, 2002, fire damaged Arlington. Betty Uzman, one of the Mississippi Department of Archives and History staff sent to help, reported that the second floor and roof were devastated and that the library had largely escaped the flames. Its books were still wet and smoke-damaged. She described volunteers and staff preparing “an estimated 3,000 volumes for freezing or storage” over three days. Historic Natchez Foundation staff Ron and Mimi Miller directed the effort with help from National Park Service curator Kathleen Jenkins at Melrose and Rosalie curator Chel’yl Munyer Branyan. Freezing or dry storage was immediate triage. Uzman’s estimate describes the rescue operation. The destination of every volume is a separate question. [3]

## 3. Then and now

The nominated site combined a main house and garden with work buildings. Federal survey plans preserve the relationship of rooms, doors and service spaces before the fire. The surviving books and salvage account tell a second history of the place, one that starts when the roof was lost. The postfire condition of individual rooms, outbuildings and grounds could not be established from current operator evidence. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The HABS record names “James Hampton White” and gives 1816. The national listing gives John Hampton White and the nomination explicitly calls the attribution a tradition. The nomination’s date range and the deed phrase leave the first building campaign unresolved. The LOC sheet page returned HTTP 403 in the web reader. The federal survey’s written data was accessible. [1–2,5]

Uzman dates the outbreak to September 15, 2002. A later preservation account gives September 17. Use the responder’s chronology in attribution and leave the exact date open to the primary city fire report. Later descriptions report deterioration and missing material. The most recent parcel-specific structural assessment and public access evidence remain open. [3–4,6]
