## 2. The story
**Named, actually read first-person household testimony:** historian **Kimberly K. Porter** writes about her own displacement in *1997 Grand Forks Flood: When History Became Personal*, published in *North Dakota History*, volume **82.1**, pp.18 onward. In the opening personal account, she recalls sleeping on a Minneapolis host's floor and trying to reconnect with family and friends. Her account joins household disruption to her decision to preserve other people's memories. This is a later authored recollection, not a contemporaneous diary or an interview recording heard here. [5]

**Original interpretive voice:** Flood history has two clocks. One marks the dramatic event; the other measures the much less visible time needed to find ordinary life again. An archive can make room for that second clock without pretending every household experienced it alike. Reading one person's words is an invitation to listen, not a license to locate their home or score the severity of their loss.

**Contemporaneous public witness:** a September 18, 1997 MPR report by **Hope Deutscher** includes **Eliot Glassheim's** description of donated flood-damaged belongings at the North Dakota Museum of Art. The article's transcript was read, not its audio. It records why ordinary things carried significance for families, without establishing their current display location. [6]

These sources differ: Porter is both a person displaced and a historian reflecting on preservation; Glassheim speaks as a museum staff member observing donated material. Neither voice represents every household in Grand Forks or East Grand Forks.

## 3. Then / now
The 1997 broadcast is close to the event. Porter's retrospective essay openly explores the difficulty of studying an experience in which the historian participated. That difference in time and perspective is useful evidence, not a flaw to conceal. [5,6]

Today UND maintains public historical research services. That does not imply that every recording is unrestricted, that a particular object is on display, or that present residents invite visitors to places described in a flood account. [2]

Keep **Grand Forks, North Dakota**, distinct from **East Grand Forks, Minnesota**. Regional accounts may discuss both; the public anchor selected here is in North Dakota. Do not transplant an East Grand Forks household into this city to fill an evidence gap.
