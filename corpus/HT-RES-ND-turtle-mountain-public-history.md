## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A history book can begin with the experience of leaving home and realizing how much of home still needs explaining.

**Named primary voice:** In the signed foreword to *Who I Am: A Guide To Your Turtle Mountain Home*, **Susan Davis** recalls leaving Turtle Mountain at seventeen for college at UND and discovering gaps in her understanding of her own community's history. She describes language names used within her family rather than presenting identity as a single uniform label. Her succinct framing is: **“There are no right or wrong answers about who you are.”** [4, printed pp. 3–4]

The cover credits **Les LaFountain, Orie Richard and Scott Belgarde**, **Project Peacemaker**, **Turtle Mountain Community College**. Davis identifies her Indian Law program directorship in the foreword; that is her role **at the time of writing**, not a verified present appointment. The hosting filename contains **20Aug07**; a separately printed publication date was not established. [4]

**Provenance boundary:** This is a signed first-person contribution in a Tribal-college publication, not a Tribal Council resolution or an interview recording. College publication and sovereign governmental endorsement are different claims. No audio was heard and no biographical details beyond the source are inferred.

**Interpretive synthesis:** The strongest story is not a visitor discovering somebody else's supposedly hidden identity. It is a community author deciding that young people deserve a usable reference to their own home. Read the work as an offered account with named makers, not as raw material for a test of cultural authenticity.

For contemporary institutional distinction, the Tribe's own website identifies the Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa and publishes governmental notices and records. [5] Its current presence should not be collapsed into an old handbook, a state tourism description or a generic account of all Chippewa/Ojibwe peoples.
