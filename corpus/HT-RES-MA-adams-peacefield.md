# HT-RES-MA-adams-peacefield — Four Generations Under One Gambrel Roof

Division MA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Peacefield is the house the Adams family chose, rather than the houses they happened to be born in. Built in 1731, bought by John Adams in 1787 from London, and occupied by four generations of Adamses from 1788 to 1927, it grew wing by wing — an east wing raised in 1800, a stone library built in 1870 — until the family handed the house, its outbuildings, and its furnishings to the United States in 1946. It stands today at 135 Adams Street in Quincy as the Old House at Peace field, in Adams National Historical Park — and it is seen by guided tour only.

## 1. Trip gate

Everything at Peacefield runs through the park's Visitor Center, and the National Park Service is explicit: "Visitors should begin their visit at the Visitor Center." The Visitor Center is at 1250 Hancock St., Quincy, MA 02169, open Wednesdays through Sundays, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.

Access to the house itself is tour-only. The park's FAQ states it plainly: "You must be on a guided tour to see the Old House at Peace field and the Stone Library," and the 2026 tour-season page adds that the Old House and Stone Library "are not available for self-guided tours." Reservations are required, made on the day of the visit, in person at the Visitor Center, first-come first-served; an entrance pass must be purchased in addition. Two tours cover Peacefield. *Presidents at Peace field* (30 minutes) covers the Old House and Stone Library through John and Abigail Adams and John Quincy and Louisa Catherine Adams, Wednesdays through Sundays at 9:45 a.m., 11:15 a.m., 1 p.m., 2:30 p.m., and 4 p.m. *Legacies of Leadership* (45 minutes) covers the same buildings across four generations, at 10:30 a.m., 12 p.m., 1:45 p.m., and 3:15 p.m. All times are "as staffing and weather permit"; the park advises calling the Visitor Center at 617-770-1175 to confirm.

The entrance fee, as the fees page states it, is a Standard Pass at $15.00 per person; the park Annual Pass is $45.00, and America the Beautiful passes are accepted. Passes are sold at the Visitor Center or online at Recreation.gov. The park does not accept cash. Passes are not required to visit the grounds — the fee is for entering the historic buildings. Groups of 11 or more should contact the Visitor Center at least two weeks in advance.

The house rules are enforced: bags larger than 9" x 11" x 5" are not permitted in the historic homes, and the park has no lockers; food, drinks including water, and chewing gum are not permitted inside; the only public restrooms are at the Visitor Center; and numbers inside are limited because, as the park puts it, "the houses are old and were built as family homes, not tourist attractions." Motorized chairs are not permitted in the houses; the park loans non-motorized wheelchairs, and the second floor is reached by stairs only.

One timing note, printed as the park states it: the 2026 tour season was extended through Friday, November 13, 2026. This dossier was checked on November 26, 2026 — after that date. Per the park's FAQ, in the closed season the homes are completely closed; the grounds remain open sunrise to sunset. The terms above are the park's published 2026 terms; confirm the next season's schedule before planning on the interior.

## 2. The story

The oldest part of Peacefield was built in 1731. The National Register nomination describes the original house as a Georgian, two-story-plus-attic frame structure of post-and-beam construction, clapboarded on a low stone foundation, symmetrical across a seven-bay front, with a gambrel roof and brick end chimneys — only four main rooms and an attic. The Historic American Buildings Survey record dates its first building campaign to about 1731 and names Maj. Leonard Vassall among those associated with it; the Founders Online editors call it the Vassall-Borland house, after the Loyalist John Borland, whose widow Anna (Vassall) Borland recovered the property after the war.

John Adams bought the house and its farm in 1787 without seeing it first — he was still in London, envoy to the Court of St. James. The Founders Online annotations record the purchase from Mrs. Borland's son, Leonard Vassall Borland, and fix it in September 1787. The nomination agrees: "The Adamses' association with the site in Quincy began in 1787 when John Adams bought it while still envoy to the Court of St. James. In 1788, on their return to the United States, he and his family moved into the small house on the property."

Adams intended, the nomination says, "to settle down in Quincy to a life of farming after years of political and literary service." He returned as often as he could and retired to the house in 1801. In his absence Abigail ran both the farm and the building campaign: the nomination credits her with overseeing additions that doubled the house's capacity while she also managed the farm. The largest addition, the L-shaped east wing of 1800, rose while Adams was president, adding a second entrance and staircase and the large reception room the family called the Long Room.

In his diary for September 8, 1796, Adams explained the name himself: "I think to christen my Place by the Name of Peace field, in commemoration of the Peace which I assisted in making in 1783, of the thirteen Years Peace and Neutrality which I have contributed to preserve, and of the constant Peace and Tranquility which I have enjoyed in this Residence." Within the family, though, the house soon became simply the "Old House," old already when the Adamses arrived.

John Quincy Adams used the Old House as his summer home through postings abroad, the State Department, the presidency, and seventeen years in the House of Representatives. His mark on the fabric is practical: kitchen renovations in 1829, and in 1836 the two-story north passage linking the kitchen ell to the east wing. In his will of January 18, 1847, he bequeathed his library, his manuscripts, and his father's papers to his son Charles Francis Adams, recommending that he "cause a building to be erected, made fire-proof, in which to keep the said library, books, documents, and manuscripts safe." 

Charles Francis Adams — congressman, Civil War minister to Great Britain, and the family's great editor — carried out the will and remade the estate. In 1870 he built the Stone Library beside the house: a one-room, fireproof building of granite and brick, its walls lined to ceiling height with oak shelves and a gallery. There he edited the ten-volume *Diary of John Adams* and the twelve-volume *Memoirs of John Quincy Adams*; his sons Henry and Brooks both wrote there. Charles Francis also remodelled the kitchen wing, added a three-story servants' wing after 1868, re-roofed the whole house in slate in 1870, and commissioned the stone Carriage House, completed in 1873. Under him the working farm became a Victorian country gentleman's seat.

Brooks Adams, Charles Francis's youngest son, was the last of the family to live at Peacefield. With his death in 1927 the family tenure ended — 139 years, four generations, from 1788. In 1946, the Founders Online annotation records, the house, outbuildings, and furnishings were presented by the family to the United States, administered by the National Park Service since. The nomination's judgment stands: the Old House "remains now as it was when they departed in 1927," displaying "intact the continuity of the Adamses life there," each generation contributing something of itself rather than the house representing any single period.

The park itself draws the contrast that organizes a visit. A mile and a half south stand the two birthplace houses at Penn's Hill — small saltboxes seventy-five feet apart, where John Adams was born in 1735 and John Quincy Adams in 1767, and where John Adams kept his law office. Those are houses of beginnings, and the park's tours keep them separate: no Peacefield tour includes the birthplaces, and no birthplace tour includes Peacefield. Peacefield is the other half — what four generations built, enlarged, gardened, and finally gave away intact.

## 3. Then / now

The house a visitor tours is not the house John Adams bought. The 1731 original was four rooms and an attic behind a seven-bay front. The 1800 east wing nearly doubled it. Charles Francis Adams remodelled rooms, added the servants' wing, and in 1870 stripped the eighteenth-century railing from the roof when the whole house was re-roofed in slate — the original wood shingles and the 1731 gambrel profile survive legibly only on the west end. What stands now is the accumulated house.

The grounds have changed register as completely. The nomination describes a working farm turned country estate: an orchard north of the house, a duck pond John Adams first excavated about 1821, and a formal garden west of the house laid out in 1731, three box-edged beds on gravel paths. When John and Abigail bought the property the garden held many kinds of fruit trees; the planting a visitor sees now follows the flower scheme laid down in the mid-nineteenth century by the wife of Charles Francis Adams, with a fourth bed added beside the Library. The rural setting is gone: the nomination, writing in 1977, already described surroundings changed "to a suburban, almost urban, one," and a wall of Quincy granite now stands where fences once stood.

The park's own early image hangs inside the house: Godfrey Frankenstein's *Portrait of the Old House in 1849*, painted the year after John Quincy Adams's death. Set against the present view from Adams Street, the 1849 canvas is the then; the tour is the now; and the distance between them is mostly Charles Francis Adams.

## 4. What's still there

Of the many structures that stood on the estate during the family's 139 years, the nomination counts five that remain, and all five are still on the ground. The Old House fronts closely on Adams Street. The Stone Library stands just northwest of it at the edge of the formal garden. The Carriage House and Woodshed lie along the eastern boundary, and the Greenhouse stands near the northern boundary. The Beale House of 1792, built by the Adamses' neighbour Captain Benjamin Beale to the west and now part of the park, also survives, its Federal exterior substantially unchanged.

Inside the Old House, what survives is the family's own accumulation. The park notes that "the vast collection of original artifacts inside the Old House greatly assists the park's interpretive staff to relate the Adams family's legacy of service," and the nomination adds that every room and its furnishings are associated with one or more members of the family, much of the contents reflecting the diplomatic careers of John, John Quincy, and Charles Francis Adams. The Santo Domingo mahogany panelling of the 1731 section is still in place.

The Stone Library's collection is still there and still working. The nomination counted some 14,000 volumes and noted the room was "still in use for research and reference purposes"; the park describes the shelves as holding the books of four generations, about ten percent the second president's own and the greater part of the remainder John Quincy Adams's.

Outside, the maintained landscape is itself a survival: a historic orchard and an eighteenth-century-style formal garden holding thousands of annual and perennial flowers, in the beds the nomination maps. John Adams's duck pond remains a named feature in the park's structure records. The Woodshed: built by John Adams in 1799 or 1800 and moved to its present site in 1873, it is the one farm building that survived Charles Francis Adams's reorganization of the farm.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Start at the Visitor Center, 1250 Hancock St.** Everything begins here — passes, reservations, restrooms, and the trolley. Arrive early: reservations are made in person, on the day, first-come first-served, and the entrance pass is a separate purchase on top.

**Take the trolley, or walk, to Peace field, 135 Adams St.** The free trolley loops Visitor Center → Adams Farm at Penn's Hill → Old House at Peace field. Allow travel time — a tour reservation is for a fixed time.

**Walk the grounds before or after the tour.** No pass or reservation is needed for the grounds. The formal garden lies west of the house, the orchard to the north, and the Stone Library stands at the garden's edge northwest of the house. This is the exterior half of the entry: the 1731 west end, the 1800 east wing, and the vine-grown front the family knew.

**Take the guided tour — it is the only way in.** Choose *Presidents at Peace field* (30 minutes) for the two presidential generations, or *Legacies of Leadership* (45 minutes) for all four. Both cover the Old House and the Stone Library: the small panelled 1731 core, the Long Room of 1800, then out to the fireproof Stone Library of 1870, where the family's books line the walls.

**Close on the grounds.** The Carriage House of 1873 and the Woodshed of 1799–1800 stand along the eastern rise, and the garden in season is the right last stop. If the birthplaces are on the same day, treat them as a separate trolley stop; in the 2026 season they were closed for a landscape project while Peacefield remained open — check their status separately.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**The purchase year is printed two ways inside the nomination itself.** Its significance and garden texts date the purchase to 1787, and the Founders Online annotations support 1787, one fixing it in September 1787. But the nomination's architectural description dates the kitchen ell to "some time prior to the Adams' purchase of the house in 1789." No source consulted reconciles the two; this entry follows 1787 and prints the conflict rather than silently choosing.

**The Stone Library carries two dates in the park's own materials.** The nomination is consistent — the Library was "Built in 1870 by Charles Francis Adams" — and the park's Places page says the same in its detailed paragraph. But the same Places page, two paragraphs earlier, calls it "the Stone Library, built in 1873." 1873 is the completion year the nomination gives the Carriage House, which suggests a transposition, but the disagreement is printed unresolved.

**The library's size is given two ways.** The nomination counts "some 14,000 volumes"; the park's Places page says "more than 12,000 books." Both are National Park Service figures; neither is treated as settled.

**The fees page leaves one age ambiguous.** It states in one block that "Children age 15 and under are free" and in another that "All visitors over the age of 16 need an entrance pass… Children under the age of 16 don't need an entrance pass." A sixteen-year-old falls between the statements as printed; the $15 price is consistent, the age boundary is not.

**The builder of the 1731 house is not named by the nomination.** The attribution to Maj. Leonard Vassall rests on the HABS record's related-names field and the Founders Online editors' identification of the property as the Vassall-Borland house — good evidence, but a thinner thread than the rest of this entry's facts.

**The nomination's coordinates were not usable.** Its UTM references are not legible in the digitized form as consulted. The coordinates in §10 come instead from the NPS HABS asset record for the Adams Mansion, scoped to that record and to the Old House; no coordinate was taken from any aggregator.
