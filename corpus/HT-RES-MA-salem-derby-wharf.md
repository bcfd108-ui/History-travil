# HT-RES-MA-salem-derby-wharf — Made Land in the Harbor: The Derby Family's Wharf at Salem

Division MA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Derby Wharf is a strip of made land in Salem Harbor, begun in 1762 by the merchant Richard Derby and his son Elias Hasket Derby and lengthened with the family's trade until, in 1806, it reached the length it holds today — 2,045 feet out into the harbor. It is the oldest surviving wharf in Salem, the working front of the Derby trading empire, and today the central walk of Salem Maritime National Historical Park. Its story carries the whole weight of Salem's Golden Age of Sail: global trade, privateering, the East India commerce that made Elias Hasket Derby famous — and the enslaved people whose labor and whose sale were woven into the Derby fortune from the start.

## 1. Trip gate

The trip gate is the operator's own — the National Park Service, Salem Maritime National Historical Park — from the park's current visitor pages as checked for this draft.

The wharf itself is open around the clock. The park's Places To Go page lists "Derby Wharf and Park Grounds Around Historic Structures" as "Open 24/7," and the park's wharves page states the same terms in words: "The wharves are open 24 hours. Please exercise caution when visiting the wharves after dark or during inclement weather." There is no gate, ticket, or fee for the wharf or the grounds. For the buildings, the same page states: "All buildings and programs are free," with all buildings closed on Thanksgiving, Christmas Day, and New Year's Day.

Building hours run on the park's seasonal table. In the September 21 – November 14 season, the Salem Armory Regional Visitor Center and the Waite and Peirce Park Store are open daily, 9:30 AM – 4:30 PM; in the November 15 – April 30 season both are open Wednesday – Sunday, 9:30 AM – 4:30 PM. The Custom House is open Wednesday – Sunday, 9:30 AM – 4:30 PM in the first season and "Closed for the Season" in the second. The two visitor centers stand less than a ten-minute walk apart: the Armory Visitor Center downtown and the Waite & Peirce Park Store on Derby Street. The park's listed address is 160 Derby Street, Salem, Massachusetts.

The practical shape of a visit is simple. The wharf is a walk, not an appointment: you can stand on it at dawn or after dark, in any season, without paying or booking anything. What the season changes is the buildings around it — whether the visitor centers are staffed that day and whether the Custom House is open. Check the park's current hours page before building a trip around any building; build the wharf walk itself whenever you like, keeping the park's caution about darkness and weather in mind.

## 2. The story

Salem's harbor once held "50 plus wharves," in the Park Service's count, and it calls the four that survive at Salem Maritime "perhaps the best remnants of Salem's Golden Age of Sail." Derby Wharf is the longest and oldest of them.

Richard Derby and his son Elias Hasket Derby began construction of the wharf in 1762. A 1992 archeological dig, reported by the Park Service, showed how the 1762 section was made: timbers laid on the mud flats at low tide, filled between with dirt and stones. Later in the nineteenth century the wharf was encased with large granite blocks, the hard edge a visitor sees today. As the family's trade expanded, they extended the wharf again and again, until in 1806 it reached its current length — 2,045 feet into Salem Harbor, in the Park Service's measured figure, "its current half-mile length" in its narrative one. The two figures do not agree, and §6 prints that disagreement rather than smoothing it over.

At its working height, this was not an empty pier: nearly twenty structures stood on it, some of the warehouses two to three stories high, and among them Elias Hasket Derby's counting house — "the headquarters of his international trading empire."

The family's rise ran through war first. During the Revolutionary War, Elias Hasket Derby — "Hasket," in the family's usage — converted many of the family's cargo vessels to privateers, and the Park Service's Derby House page states the sequence plainly: "The wealth that the Derbys amassed from privateering was the foundation of the great East India trade that Hasket and others pioneered after the Revolution." That trade made him Salem's most famous merchant; Nathaniel Hawthorne wrote of "the days of old King Derby," and "King Derby" is the name that stuck. Across Derby Street stands the brick Derby House, built in 1762 as Richard Derby's wedding gift to Elias Hasket and Elizabeth Crowninshield Derby.

None of that wealth stands apart from slavery, and this entry does not treat it as a side note. The Park Service's own history page for Richard Derby opens with the fact: "We know he enslaved at least six people while living on Derby Street." The Derby papers let the Park Service name some of them. Jane, a Salem woman enslaved by Captain Richard Derby, married Isaac, a man enslaved by Samuel Gardner, in 1754; the town's vital records entered both under the euphemism "servants." Richard Derby's last will and testament, written in 1783, disposes of three more people as property in a single document: "my Negro child Peggy," left to his wife Sarah; "my Negro Girl named Cate with her apparel," left to his daughter Mary Crowninshield; and "my Negro Man Caesar with his apparel," left to his daughter Martha Prince.

The paper trail runs earlier and colder than the will. A 1755 record of taxes owed by Richard Derby to the Province of Massachusetts Bay lists his imported goods — rum, sugar, tierces, wine — and, in the same accounting, an enslaved child, entered as "a Negro boy." A Salem Custom House record of the same year shows that the child, originally from Africa or of African descent, was forcibly transported on Derby's own schooner *Eagle*, from Barbados to Salem; Derby paid £33 and 16 shillings in impost duties on May 17, 1755, on a cargo listed as 49 hogsheads of rum, 40 barrels of sugar, and "1 Negro boy." The Park Service notes that some colonists invested in enslaved children, who cost less, and trained them into "a valuable, unpaid labor force." Derby also owned a rum distillery, and Derby Family Papers documents show him drawing on the labor of a man enslaved by Samuel Fisk — labor "rented" from the man's enslaver across 1762–1763, the very years the wharf was rising out of the mud flats. At the Derby House, Elias Hasket and Elizabeth Derby lived with their seven children and, the Park Service records, at least two enslaved people of African descent.

The wharf's cargoes belonged to the same economy. The Park Service's article on Salem's maritime economy of slavery puts it without flinching: "the transformation of Salem's waterfront into rows of long, busy wharves and fine homes for wealthy merchant families could not have been possible without slavery and the slave trade." Salem merchants provisioned the West Indies' sugar plantations with fish, livestock, and lumber, and Salem ships came home with sugar, molasses, and coffee produced by enslaved people's forced labor. The article illustrates the trade from Derby's own fleet: the 1764 account of sales of his schooner *Pembroke*, cargo out in wood products, horses, rice, and fish, and home from Dominica in sugar, coffee, and molasses. Enslaved people in Salem worked alongside free people in farming, fishing, shipbuilding, the maritime trades, and rum distilling — usually unpaid, the Park Service notes, and often given the most dangerous work. Slavery technically ended in Massachusetts in 1783, the year of Derby's will, but Salem merchants went on trading in the economy of slavery afterward. The Park Service's judgment on the families involved, the Derbys named among them: their involvement "caused great harm to countless Black people."

The wharf outlasted the trade that built it. In 1937 the Derby House was transferred to the newly formed Salem Maritime National Historic Site, and on March 17, 1938, Salem Maritime was established as the first National Historic Site in the United States — the Park Service's management page states both the date and the distinction. (Its own publications do not all tell the 1937–1938 sequence the same way; §6 prints the disagreement.) At the far end of the wharf stands its one original surviving structure, the Derby Wharf Light Station, first lit in 1871 (§4). The vessel moored along the wharf, the *Friendship of Salem*, is a replica — the Park Service calls it "the replica tall ship" — built to the appearance of the 1797 Salem East Indiaman *Friendship*, whose fifteen voyages ended with her capture by HMS *Rosamond* on September 5, 1812 (§4). The replica is a stand-in of real presence, and this entry names it as one.

## 3. Then / now

Then, Derby Wharf was one working strip among more than fifty, built over with work: nearly twenty structures, warehouses two to three stories high among them, and the counting house running the family's trade from the middle of it.

Now, four historic wharves survive at Salem Maritime — Derby, Hatch's, Central, and Tucker — and Derby is the long, open walk of the park. The warehouses and the counting house are gone; what survives is the made land itself, timber-and-fill construction of 1762 inside its later granite casing. Its only original surviving structure is the small brick light station at its far end, still an aid to navigation. Alongside rides the replica *Friendship of Salem* at her dock. The ground reads today as open space — grass, path, water on both sides. The Park Service counts the whole park at ten historic buildings, nine archeological sites, four historic wharves, and a historic light station, dating between 1675 and 1944; the wharf is the piece of that inheritance you experience by walking it, at any hour.

## 4. What's still there

The wharf itself is the artifact, and it is substantially there. The made land begun in 1762 and completed to its present length in 1806 still extends into Salem Harbor, and the later nineteenth-century granite-block casing still armors the edges a visitor walks beside.

At the end of the wharf stands the Derby Wharf Light Station, the wharf's only original surviving structure: a small square brick lighthouse, approximately twelve feet square and about twenty feet high to the cupola, first lit in 1871 as one of three lights marking the main channel into Salem's anchorage. It still works — solar-driven, 300 candlepower, flashing red every six seconds — and for many years it carried one of only seventeen sixth-order Fresnel lenses in the United States. It was listed on the National Register on June 15, 1987, under the Lighthouses of Massachusetts thematic listing.

Moored at the wharf is the *Friendship of Salem* — a replica, stated plainly. She was built to present the appearance of an original 1797 Salem-built vessel, based on a model of the original held by the Peabody Essex Museum, and built with modern technology and materials to meet current safety and accessibility requirements. She gives the wharf back its scale — a two-decked, three-masted, square-rigged ship of 342 tons in the original's measure — but she is a twentieth-century vessel, keel laid in 1996, and nothing about her is 1797 fabric.

At the head of the wharf, the Derby Street ground holds the rest of the park's cluster: the Derby House of 1762, the Custom House, and the park's other historic buildings, with the Waite & Peirce Park Store at 193 Derby Street — the frame the wharf walk starts from, under the opening terms printed in §1.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Start at either visitor center — the Salem Armory Regional Visitor Center downtown or the Waite & Peirce Park Store on Derby Street — for orientation and the day's building schedule; the two are less than a ten-minute walk apart. Take Derby Street to the head of the wharf, where the Derby House stands across from the wharf it served: the house Richard Derby built in 1762 as a wedding gift for the son whose counting house stood out on the wharf, and the home where the family lived with at least two enslaved people.

Then walk the wharf. The progression is the length itself: the granite-cased edges, the harbor opening out on both sides, the replica *Friendship of Salem* at her dock along the way. Finish at the far end, at the Derby Wharf Light Station, still flashing red over the channel it was built to mark in 1871. The walk back is the same ground in reverse. Because the wharf and grounds are open 24/7 and free, the progression works at any hour; because the buildings keep the seasonal hours in §1, the staffed parts are a daytime, in-season proposition.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The sources for this entry disagree with each other in print, and the disagreements are carried here unresolved.

**The wharf's dates.** The Park Service's current pages state that construction began in 1762 and that the wharf reached its current length in 1806. The Historic American Buildings Survey record for the light station (HABS MA-1350) states instead that the family built the wharf "beginning in 1764," expanding it "to its current length of over 2,000' by 1809." 1762 or 1764 for the start; 1806 or 1809 for the completion. This entry follows the park pages in its narrative and prints the HABS dates here, unreconciled.

**The wharf's length.** The Park Service's Derby Wharf page measures the wharf at 2,045 feet. Its wharves page, describing the same 1806 completion, calls that "its current half-mile length." A half mile is 2,640 feet; 2,045 feet is not a half mile. The two figures cannot both be right, and no source checked resolves which the 1806 extension produced. This entry prints both, attributed, rather than choosing.

**The park's founding year.** The Park Service's management page states that Salem Maritime was "established March 17th, 1938" as the first National Historic Site established in the United States. A Park Service news release of March 16, 2010, states instead that the site "was designated in 1937," recording that in 1937 Derby, Hatch's, and Central wharves were handed over to form the site's nucleus; the Derby House page likewise dates the house's transfer to "the newly formed" site to 1937. Both years stand in the Park Service's own record. This entry prints 1938 on the management page's authority and records the 1937 statements here, unresolved.

**The light station's significant year.** The National Register record for the Derby Wharf Light Station (NRIS 87001466) carries "Significant Years: 1877." The Park Service's light station page and the HABS record both place construction in 1870–1871 and first lighting in 1871. The Register's 1877 stands against both, unexplained in the record; it is printed as it appears, unresolved.

**The wharf's own Register record.** A Register record number for the wharves as such was not verified from a primary record in this pass, and none is printed; the light station's verified listing is cited in §10.
