# 1850 House — a Baroness's Paris row house, its enslaved and immigrant workers, and a middle-class interior that belongs to no single family
HT-RES-LA-1850-house · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The ground came first, and the woman who built on it inherited it. Don Andrés Almonester y Roxas, the Spanish colonial builder and philanthropist, financed the St. Louis Cathedral, the Cabildo and the Presbytère that frame Jackson Square, and at his death, when his daughter Micaela was three years old, his property around the square passed to her. Micaela Leonarda Almonester de Pontalba was born in New Orleans on November 6, 1795, married at fifteen in the cathedral her father had built to her cousin Célestin Delfau de Pontalba, and sailed to France, where she lived for most of her life. Her father-in-law, the eighty-year-old Baron Joseph Xavier de Pontalba, tried to seize her inheritance, forbade her to see her mother unless she gave him half her Place d'Armes property, and, on the morning after a three-hour meeting between Micaela and Célestin in 1834, trapped her in an upstairs bedroom and fired at her four times at point blank range, shooting away her left breast and two fingers of the hand she raised to the muzzle, then killing himself when he found he was out of ammunition. She survived, remained a semi-invalid for years, and after two harrowing trials a French tribunal awarded her control of her property. She built the Hôtel Pontalba in Paris in 1840 — now the United States embassy residence — supervising every detail, and in 1849 she returned to New Orleans bringing plans and specifications for the buildings that carry her name. [2, 5]

What she proposed in 1846 was a Paris square transplanted to a muddy parade ground. Her announced intention was to give the Place d'Armes, now Jackson Square, the character of the Place des Vosges in Paris: two matching rows of red-brick row houses facing each other across the square, sixteen houses to a row, with shops on the ground floor and living quarters above, sharing party walls, with a service wing and small courtyard to the rear of each house. The National Register nomination records that she intended an arcaded walk recessed in the building mass, as at the Cabildo and Presbytère; as completed, the buildings have projecting cast-iron galleries sheltering the walk instead, and the nomination notes the difference became a pretext for the city to rescind a twenty-year tax exemption agreed in recognition of the work. Construction began in 1849. The Upper Pontalba Building, on St. Peter Street, was completed in the fall of 1850; the Lower Pontalba Building, on St. Ann Street, was finished in 1851. The cost is given in the sources inspected as over $300,000. [3, 4, 5]

Who designed the buildings is still a matter of debate, and this dossier does not settle it. The Baroness first engaged the noted local architect James Gallier Sr., dismissed him before construction began, and employed Samuel Stewart as builder. The nomination states the buildings are based on drawings and specifications of Gallier, with whom she failed to enter a contract, and drawings of Henry Howard, to whom she would pay only a $120 drafting fee for developing her own designs — and that Stewart ran into immediate difficulties when Gallier's specifications, Howard's drawings, the Baroness's opinions and actual site conditions were incompatible. The Society of Architectural Historians' entry records that she replaced Gallier with Howard in 1849, that Samuel Wilson Jr. believed Gallier was responsible for the plans and that the extant Gallier plans correspond with the as-built conditions, but that Gallier never claimed credit for the elevations and Howard did in his short autobiography. A later 64 Parishes account, drawing on Christina Vella's biography, adds that Howard testified in the 1851 court case *Stewart v. Pontalba* that the Baroness claimed she was a good architect herself and said she built a great deal in France, that she had brought plans with her from New York, and that Vella concludes Howard's authorship claim "is not borne out by any document concerning the construction of the Pontalbas." The builder who made the working drawings and adjusted the buildings to the site's thirteen-inch downward slope from Decatur to Chartres Streets was Samuel Stewart, with his draftsman Waldemar Appolonius Talen. The cast-iron work — fabricated in New York, possibly the first in New Orleans to serve as an integral part of a design rather than a later attachment — carries the intertwined "AP" monogram for Almonester-Pontalba through its lacy railings. The 64 Parishes biography credits the monogram design to Micaela's son Gaston, a gifted artist; the later 64 Parishes account credits Talen with designing the cast-iron elements that incorporate the AP monogram. Both attributions are printed in §6. [2, 3, 4, 7]

The buildings also remade the square around them. The Baroness personally paid for extensive landscaping improvements to Jackson Square, convinced authorities to renovate the square, the Cabildo and the Presbytère, and church authorities to enlarge the cathedral — the same campaign of improvements that gave the square its 1851 form, covered in the Jackson Square dossier. Her son Gaston, who was in New Orleans with her during the three years of construction, captured the architecture and activity in vivid sketches and watercolors, including views of his mother's buildings as they were being constructed; the drawings, recently discovered in France, were loaned by the Pontalba family for the Historic New Orleans Collection's 2019 exhibition *The New Orleans Drawings of Gaston de Pontalba, 1848–1851*. The corner of the Lower Pontalba at Chartres and St. Ann Streets stands, according to the Orleans Parish Landmarks Commission marker there, on the site of the French colonial Government House, residence of Governor Étienne de Périer from 1727 until 1731 and of Governor Jean Baptiste LeMoyne, Sieur de Bienville, from 1731 until the house was abandoned in 1738; the site was later acquired by Don Andrés Almonester, and various buildings occupied it until his daughter built the present building. Micaela left New Orleans in March 1851, never to return, and died in Paris in 1874 at seventy-nine, having cared for the incapacitated Célestin for twenty-three years. [2, 4, 5, 6]

The houses were built as row houses, not as apartments, and their first occupants were the city's merchants and professionals — mostly newcomers — and their servants. The 64 Parishes biography names among the first tenants the soprano Jenny Lind, who stayed in one of the houses when she performed in New Orleans. Each house had a flagged passageway from the street to the principal stairway, a courtyard and service area at the rear of the ground floor; the principal floor had a salon at the front, a connecting dining room, and kitchen and service rooms in the rear; bedrooms occupied the next floor; and the attic, with horizontal windows just below the narrow cornice, provided servants' rooms and storage. The Society of Architectural Historians states the attic space accommodated the residents' enslaved workers. The Louisiana State Museum's own page for the 1850 House says visitors encounter the stories of the Baroness and of the people who lived here in the 1850s, including enslaved workers and Irish immigrant servants. No names of enslaved or servant residents of this particular house were found in the sources inspected, and none are supplied here; the house's own interpretation is that it does not represent any single family. [1, 2, 3, 4]

After the Civil War the Vieux Carré declined, desirable tenants moved out, and by 1900 poor tenants were crowded in the buildings in slum conditions. The Pontalba family retained ownership until the 1920s and took little interest in the townhouses. In 1921 the family sold the Lower Pontalba Building to philanthropist William Ratcliffe Irby — the spelling Ratcliff appears in some secondary summaries, printed as a conflict in §6 — who bequeathed it to the Louisiana State Museum in 1927. The Upper Pontalba was acquired by local civic leaders and sold in 1930 to the Pontalba Building Museum Association, which turned it over to the City of New Orleans, which has owned it since the 1930s. Both buildings were remodeled into apartments in 1935–1936 with Works Progress Administration funds, and a restoration in the 1950s by Koch and Wilson returned them to residential use. Over the years the buildings have housed significant authors, including Sherwood Anderson, William Faulkner and Katherine Anne Porter. The Louisiana State Museum took possession of the Lower Pontalba in 1927 and opened the 1850 House to the public in 1948 — the opening year as given in secondary summaries; the operator page inspected this run does not state it, and it is flagged in §6. The museum has re-created what one of the residences would have looked like during the antebellum era, furnished with domestic goods, decorative arts and art of the period, including a set of John Slidell's china, Old Paris porcelain, New Orleans silver, and a kitchen with a cast-iron range. It depicts middle-class family life during the most prosperous period in New Orleans' history — a composite interior, deliberately, not a family's house. The Pontalba Buildings were declared a National Historic Landmark in 1974. [1, 3, 4, 5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Lower Pontalba Building, 523 St. Ann Street, between Chartres and Decatur Streets, on the downriver (St. Ann) side of Jackson Square, French Quarter, New Orleans, Orleans Parish. The Upper Pontalba Building faces it across the square on St. Peter Street. The pair, with the square, cathedral, Cabildo and Presbytère, form one National Historic Landmark ensemble, NRHP reference 74000934, designated May 30, 1974. [1, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** 1850 House, Louisiana State Museum, 523 St. Ann St., New Orleans, LA 70116, 504-524-9118 — staffed museum entry at the published daily hours. The Lower Pontalba's ground-floor shops, the Jackson Square sidewalk and the building's cast-iron galleries are public street-level reading ground; the Upper Pontalba's apartments and the 1850 House's upper floors are not open ground — the house interior is ticketed museum ground only, and the rest of the Lower Pontalba's residences are private. [1, 3]

## 5. Public progression

Enter the 1850 House from St. Ann Street within museum hours and take the house in the order it was lived in: ground-floor passage and courtyard first, then the principal floor — salon at the front, dining room behind it, kitchen and service rooms at the rear — then bedrooms above and the attic servants' rooms under the cornice, reading the whole section as one household's division of labor. **Strongest story moment · The attic windows.** The narrow horizontal openings just below the cornice are the smallest feature on the facade and the one that names who kept the house running: enslaved workers and immigrant servants slept and stored their things up there, above the family bedrooms, and the museum's own framing — that this is no single family's house — is the honest way to read them, as a composite of many households' arrangements rather than one family's romance. Then step back out to the square and read the pair as the Baroness intended them: two Parisian rows closing a public square she also landscaped, with her father's cathedral, Cabildo and Presbytère completing the front — and the AP monogram in the ironwork as her signature woven into a public place. Finish at the Chartres Street corner marker, where the Government House of Périer and Bienville stood before any of it. [1–4, 6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Who designed the buildings.** The nomination says the buildings are based on Gallier's drawings and specifications and Howard's drawings, with the Baroness herself as designer in substance. SAH Archipedia records the debate: Wilson believed Gallier was responsible for the plans, the extant Gallier plans correspond with as-built conditions, Gallier never claimed the elevations, Howard did in his autobiography. The 64 Parishes account drawing on Vella concludes Howard's authorship claim "is not borne out by any document concerning the construction of the Pontalbas," and records Howard's 1851 testimony that the Baroness claimed she was a good architect herself. No single attribution is adopted here. [3, 4, 7]
- **The AP monogram.** The 64 Parishes biography of the Baroness credits the AP monogram design to her son Gaston, a gifted artist. The later 64 Parishes account credits Waldemar Appolonius Talen — Samuel Stewart's draftsman, who signed a recently discovered rendering with Stewart — with designing the cast-iron elements that incorporate the AP monogram. Both are printed; the ironwork itself is undisputed. [2, 7]
- **Brick origin.** SAH Archipedia says the houses are constructed of pressed red brick from Baltimore. The 1974 nomination describes red Philadelphia pressed brick. The two origins are not reconciled in the sources inspected. [3, 4]
- **Completion dates.** The nomination gives the Upper Pontalba completed in the fall of 1850 and the Lower in 1851; SAH and the Baroness biography give the range 1849–1851, finished 1851. The shorthand "built in 1850" in secondary summaries collapses the two completions and should not be read as the Lower building's date. [2, 3, 4]
- **Irby's name.** The operator page spells the philanthropist William Ratcliffe Irby. Secondary summaries spell him William Ratcliff Irby. The operator spelling is followed in the story; the variant is noted so a search under either spelling finds the same bequest. [1]
- **1850 House opening year.** Secondary summaries give the museum's opening to the public as 1948. The operator page inspected this run states the museum took possession in 1927 and describes the furnished interior, but does not state an opening year. The 1948 date is carried here as a secondary-summary date, not verified against the operator page. [1]
- **Hours have changed across listings.** An older Louisiana State Museum "French Quarter Five" leaflet listed all five French Quarter properties open Tuesdays–Sundays, 10 a.m.–4:30 p.m. The operator's current page for the 1850 House lists Open Daily 9:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m., last ticket 3:30 p.m. The current operator page governs the trip gate; the older pattern is recorded so a stale listing is not mistaken for a closure error. [1]
- **The enslaved and servant record is a composite, not a household record.** The museum states the house reflects mid-nineteenth-century life including enslaved workers and Irish immigrant servants, and SAH places enslaved workers in the attic rooms. No names, numbers or tenures for this house's enslaved or servant residents were found in the sources inspected. The interior is a furnished composite; do not present any room as a named person's room. [1, 3]
