# HT-RES-MI-fort-wayne-detroit — A star fort that never fired a shot, on ground a thousand years older
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Detroit's third fort stands on the Springwells bluff above the Detroit River: a star-shaped earthwork fort begun in the 1840s, fronted by an 1848 limestone barracks, on ground the National Park Service calls arguably one of the most historic sites in Michigan — a Woodland-period burial ground, the place where the Treaty of Springwells closed the War of 1812, and then, for more than a century, the place where Michigan's soldiers entered the Army. The fort never fired a shot in combat. It is now a City of Detroit park and heritage center, mid-way through a city strategic plan to stabilize its vacant buildings and lease them back to life.

## 1. Trip gate
This is a city-run site with a street address and stated hours, and this gate carries access only as the operator states it. Historic Fort Wayne is owned and operated by the City of Detroit; the fort's own site gives the address as 6325 West Jefferson Avenue, Detroit, MI 48209, the phone as (313) 348-7721, and the contact email as historicfortwayne@detroitmi.gov, and presents the grounds as a historic riverfront park and heritage center open daily from 6 AM to 8 PM (operator's pages, checked November 26, 2026; the opening hour prints two ways inside the operator's own channels — see §6). The National Register record carries the same street address, 6325 West Jefferson Avenue.

Grounds and buildings are not the same visit. The Detroit Historical Society's Encyclopedia of Detroit records that visitors can enter the original 1848 limestone barracks — partially restored — principally on weekends during the summer, alongside the star fort, the restored Commanding Officer's house, the Spanish-American War guard house, and the Tuskegee Airmen Museum housed on the site; the operator also offers the grounds and buildings for events and leases under the City's strategic plan. No standing admission fee or tour price is printed, because no current operator page checked in this pass states one; dated event listings from earlier years carried their own terms and are not carried forward (see §6). One part of the reservation is not the city's and not a visitor stop: a remaining federal parcel on the river is operated by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers as a boatyard (see §6). The burial mound on the grounds is a Native American burial site: something to stand before, on the paths — nothing in this entry directs a visitor onto it.

## 2. The story
The fort is the newest layer on this ground, and the National Park Service's Springwells place page carries the older ones. Springwells takes its name from the freshwater springs behind the sandy bluff along the Detroit River. The NPS records that the site "has deep cultural significance going back more than a millennium to the area's first native inhabitants," that the springs "attracted long-term indigenous settlements and served as a major burial site dating from the Woodland period (400 CE–1200 CE) up through the early 1800s," and that researchers suggest the earthworks along the bluff "ranged from six to as many as nineteen mounds." The NPS's Native American history of Detroit adds that the fort site held precolonial burial mounds and later a Potawatomi village. In 1815 the ground hosted diplomacy as well: the Treaty of Springwells, signed here at the close of the War of 1812, ended hostilities between the United States and the assembled Native nations — the NPS names the Wyandot, Delaware, Seneca, Shawnee, Miami, Ojibwe, Odawa, and Potawatomi — restoring their prewar rights and possessions.

The fort came out of a later border scare. The NPS Springwells page records: "By 1843, with continuing concerns over future disputes with Canada and fears of another British invasion like the one that crippled the United States at the River Raisin in 1813, construction of a massive fort at Springwells finally began. Christened Fort Wayne after General 'Mad' Anthony Wayne, the star-shaped fort was built on the strategic flat sandy bluff overlooking the north bank of the river adjacent to the springs, largely displacing the remaining Woodland burial mounds." The fort's own site dates the star fort's building to 1845, and the National Register record frames the district's period of significance as 1845–1965, with 1849 as the significant year and Montgomery C. Meigs named as architect (see §6 for the full date spread). The Detroit Historical Society gives the fort's completion as by 1850 and names it Detroit's third fort, after the French and British posts downtown. Its centerpiece, per the Historical Society, is the original 1848 limestone barracks — a three-and-a-half-story building the Register-era descriptions lay out in five sections — and the star fort itself was renovated in 1861, its works refaced as the Civil War approached.

The war the fort was built for never came, and the Historical Society's account of what happened instead is the fort's real biography: "the fort has served the city of Detroit through many eras, yet has never seen a shot fired in combat. The peaceful location became a primary induction center for Michigan troops entering battle in every U.S. conflict from the Civil War to Vietnam." In the Civil War it was a mustering post for Michigan's volunteer regiments. In the First World War it housed troops, including construction battalions and an aero-squadron. In the Second World War it became the Motor Supply Depot, "a primary procurement location for the vehicles and weapons manufactured in Detroit during the war," and it also housed Italian prisoners of war as a temporary internment center. In the 1950s and 1960s, the Historical Society records, it was "one of the largest induction centers in the Midwest during the Korean and Vietnam wars." The Register record's period of significance, running to 1965, matches that long arc. The post's duties were not all military in the narrow sense: after Detroit's 1967 uprising, the Historical Society records, the fort sheltered families whose homes had burned, many of them living there until 1971.

The transfer to the city came in pieces. The Historical Society records that beginning in 1948 the fort was given to the City of Detroit in parcels; the Detroit Historical Museum oversaw its operation until 2006, when responsibility passed to the city's Recreation Department. MotorCities National Heritage Area records the designations that frame the site: a Michigan State Historic Site in 1958 and National Register listing in 1971 (reference 71000425). The fort's present chapter is a plan: the operator's site records that the final Historic Fort Wayne Strategic Plan — produced by the City of Detroit General Services Department with support from the National Park Foundation and the Kresge Foundation — "will guide Historic Fort Wayne on its path toward sustainable operation," recommending stabilization of vacant buildings, partner support, and new compatible uses, with the city rezoning the property to allow rehabilitation and leasing. The star fort that never fought is being kept, deliberately, as a working piece of the city.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a river bluff of springs and burial mounds, used and ceremonially held by Native nations for over a thousand years (NPS). **Now:** the same bluff inside a city park, with one mound surviving on the fort grounds and the Treaty of Springwells among the site's interpreted stories (NPS; operator).

**Then:** a new star fort on the Canadian border, built in the 1840s against a British attack that diplomacy had already made unlikely, its limestone barracks raised in 1848 (NPS; Detroit Historical Society). **Now:** the earthworks and the barracks still stand, the barracks partially restored and open to visitors principally on summer weekends (Detroit Historical Society).

**Then:** Michigan's front door to the Army — mustering, induction, motor supply, and prisoner housing from the Civil War through Vietnam, the Register's period of significance running 1845–1965 (Detroit Historical Society; NRIS record). **Now:** a City of Detroit park and heritage center since the parcel transfers that began in 1948, with a strategic plan to stabilize and re-lease its vacant buildings (Detroit Historical Society; operator).

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its source attached:
- The star fort itself — the 1840s earthwork fortification on the river bluff, renovated in 1861 (Detroit Historical Society; operator)
- The original 1848 limestone barracks, partially restored — the fort's centerpiece building (Detroit Historical Society)
- The restored Commanding Officer's house, the Spanish-American War guard house, and the Tuskegee Airmen Museum housed on the site (Detroit Historical Society)
- The parade ground and the rows of later post buildings — officers' row, non-commissioned officers' housing, headquarters and guard buildings are all named among the site's on-ground markers (HMdb marker listings for the grounds, checked November 26, 2026)
- One surviving Native American burial mound on the grounds, marked on site as the Fort Wayne or Springwells Mound, among the oldest burial sites in southeast Michigan (site marker, transcribed at HMdb; NPS Springwells page)
- The riverfront setting itself — the sandy bluff and springs ground above the Detroit River, at the foot of Livernois Avenue (NPS; NRIS address record)
- The federal remainder: a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers boatyard parcel on the reservation's river edge, not part of the public site (MotorCities; Historic Fort Wayne Coalition — acreage in conflict, see §6)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, within the operator's stated hours and the boundary in §1 — the grounds are the visit; building interiors are program- and season-dependent:

1. **At the gate, 6325 West Jefferson Avenue:** Start with the address the Register record and the operator agree on. The fort is a district, not a single building — read it as a post, in plan, the way the Army laid it out.
2. **On the star fort's works:** The earthwork star on the bluff is the 1840s argument in dirt — built against Canada, across the narrow river. The National Park Service's sentence for this spot is the one to carry: the fort rose "largely displacing the remaining Woodland burial mounds." The military layer and the burial layer are the same ground.
3. **At the 1848 limestone barracks:** The building in this entry's first photograph as the Historic American Buildings Survey found it in 1934 — three and a half stories of limestone, dormers, and tall chimneys, housing the garrison of the next hundred years.
4. **Across the parade ground to the later post:** Officers' row, the guard house, the headquarters ground — the working post of the induction era, read building by building against the on-site markers.
5. **At the mound, from the path:** End at the oldest thing here. The marker calls the Fort Wayne or Springwells Mound one of the oldest Indian burial sites in southeast Michigan. Stand on the path, read it, and leave it exactly as it is.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The fort's construction dates print in several forms, and this entry does not reconcile them. The NPS Springwells page says construction "finally began" by 1843. The fort's own site says the star fort "was built in 1845." The Historic Fort Wayne Coalition's compiled history puts Army surveyors on the ground in 1840 and the post's 125 years of use from the 1840s. The National Register record opens the period of significance in 1845 and names 1849 the significant year. The Detroit Historical Society says the fort was "completed by 1850." Secondary accounts widen the spread further (begun 1842, substantially complete 1851); those forms are noted here as secondary only. Section 2 prints each date with its source rather than choosing.

The star fort's renovation prints two ways: the Detroit Historical Society and the Coalition give 1861 for the renovation (the Historical Society's phrasing is "renovated in 1861"), while widely repeated secondary accounts describe the walls' rebuilding with brick facing as beginning in 1863 under Capt. Thomas J. Cram. The 1861 date is used in §2 with its source named; the 1863 form was not verified from a primary source in this pass.

The federal remainder's size conflicts between the two stewardship-adjacent sources: MotorCities National Heritage Area gives the reservation as 96 acres, 83 belonging to the city and the remaining 13 operated by the Army Corps of Engineers as a boatyard; the Historic Fort Wayne Coalition's history says the city eventually owned the entire property "with the exception of nine acres still occupied by the Army Corps of Engineers." Both forms print here; neither is chosen.

The operator's hours print two ways inside the operator's own channels: the fort's website states the grounds are open daily from 6 AM to 8 PM, while a visitor's exchange on the fort's own Instagram account refers to "the website's daily 8 AM–8 PM hours," and a 2025 seasonal graphic announced Saturday–Sunday 10 AM–4 PM opening. The website form is used in §1 as checked November 26, 2026; an early or off-season visit should confirm current hours first. Departmental naming also varies by city page — the Historical Society records the 2006 transfer to the Recreation Department, while the General Services Department produced the strategic plan — so this entry carries "City of Detroit" as the operator and names each department only where its own document does.

The National Register nomination itself could not be read in this pass: the NPGallery record for reference 71000425 describes a nomination PDF asset, but the served file is the Park Service's placeholder stating the record "has not yet been digitized." The Register facts here therefore rest on the NRIS asset record's own fields, and the nomination's narrative — the authoritative federal account of the fort's buildings and Army years — remains unread; the Army-life narrative in §2 is carried instead by the Detroit Historical Society's Encyclopedia of Detroit and attributed to it throughout. Standing tour terms were likewise not verified: earlier City event listings (a 2021 holiday event at $10 per person) are dated event terms, and no current operator page checked states a standing tour price or schedule, so none prints. Finally, one secondary listing gives the address as 6053 West Jefferson Avenue; the Register record and the operator agree on 6325, and 6325 is what prints.

Lucas, Library of Congress HABS MI-27-4-B, via its Wikimedia Commons file record "Fort Wayne Barrack 1934.jpg." What the downloaded frame actually shows: the long limestone barracks seen from its gable end and long side in snow — three and a half stories of coursed stone with quoins, rows of tall windows, dormers breaking the roofline, and tall brick chimneys, snow sitting on the roof edges and the ground, narrow vertical loophole slits in the end wall's upper stories, and the survey's negative number "27-4" lettered at the lower right. No people. It is the same 1848 barracks named in §2 and §4, photographed by the federal survey eighty-six years after it was raised.
