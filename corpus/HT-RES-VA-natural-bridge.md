# HT-RES-VA-natural-bridge — The limestone arch Jefferson bought for twenty shillings and would not let anyone deface
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A single block of limestone left standing over Cedar Creek when a cave roof fell in everywhere else — a natural arch in Rockbridge County that ranked with Niagara Falls as one of the young nation's two great natural wonders, that Thomas Jefferson owned, described, and tried to protect in writing, and that Virginia finally took into public hands in 2016.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a state park built around one natural feature: Natural Bridge State Park, at Natural Bridge in southern Rockbridge County, Virginia, where the limestone arch spans Cedar Creek at the junction of U.S. Route 11 and Virginia Route 130. The operator is the Virginia Department of Conservation and Recreation (Virginia State Parks). The terms below are printed as the operator's own pages carry them. The DCR park page states that the park is open daily from 8 a.m. to dusk, weather permitting; that the Visitor Center is open daily 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. in its main-season hours as that page carries them (10 a.m. to 5 p.m. in the November-to-March hours on the same page), with the Trail Store open daily 8 a.m. to dusk; and that accessing the bridge requires a stairway of 137 steps, with accessibility accommodations available on request at the Visitor Center or by calling (540) 291-1326, as that page carries the number. The DCR fees page states that Natural Bridge charges no parking fee at the visitor center but does charge per-person admission — ages 3–12, $6; age 13 and up, $9 — and $5 per car at trail heads; that page carries an effective date of May 21, 2026, as it states it. The same park page states the park is 1,635 acres with more than 10 miles of hiking trails, including the Cedar Creek Trail from the Visitor Center under the bridge to Lace Falls. The bridge was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on November 18, 1997 (NRHP reference no. 97001401, per the NPS's own National Register record) and designated a National Historic Landmark in 1998 (see §6 on the exact date). The park itself — Virginia's 37th state park — was dedicated on September 24, 2016, per the DCR park page.

## 2. The story
The arch is what is left of a cave. The DCR park page's history tells the formation this way: several hundred thousand years ago a sinkhole opened near today's Interstate 81 and swallowed Cedar Creek, turning it into an underground river; the river carved a deep cave from the limestone until the cave grew too tall to support itself and its roof collapsed everywhere except one point — today's Natural Bridge. The National Historic Landmark nomination describes the result in plainer survey language: a block of solid limestone spanning the chasm between two steep slopes, forming a semi-elliptical arch, with Cedar Creek running under it, the creek's gradual erosion having formed the structure. The nomination dates the first written mention of the bridge by Euro-Americans to 1742 and sets the period of significance from that era to about 1860, the decades when the bridge did its cultural work.

That work was national. The nomination's statement of significance argues that in the Colonial and Early National periods Natural Bridge ranked with Niagara Falls on the top tier of the New World's natural wonders; that it drew a steady stream of artists and well-connected travelers; and that, paired with Niagara at the northern border, it helped Americans build a national self-identity tied to the scale of their own landscape. Both were on the itinerary of every European who explored the newly settled regions of America in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the nomination notes, but they diverged: Niagara lent itself to commercialization and industry, while Natural Bridge stayed simple and pure, its integrity in its form, which has not changed.

Thomas Jefferson is the human thread, and his link is documented rather than legendary. The nomination records that Jefferson probably viewed the bridge for the first time in August 1767, making notes in his Memorandum Book that became the basis for his description in *Notes on the State of Virginia*; the DCR park page carries the visit as 1767 and Jefferson's verdict in his own phrase — "the most sublime of Nature's works." In his *Notes*, published in 1785, Jefferson gave the bridge a full page, writing, as the nomination quotes him, that few men have resolution to look over into the abyss, that the spectator involuntarily falls on hands and feet and creeps to the parapet to peep over, and that looking down gave him a violent headache — while from the valley below "the rapture of the Spectator is really indiscribable" before "so beautiful an arch, so elevated, so light, and springing, as it were, up to heaven." On **July 5, 1774**, Jefferson obtained the bridge in a grant from King George III, signed by Virginia Governor Dunmore, covering 157 acres including the Natural Bridge on Cedar Creek; the DCR park page states the price as 20 shillings, and the grant's own consideration, as the patent record is quoted in the sources consulted, was "twenty shillings of good and lawful money." In 1778 Rockbridge County was formed and named for the bridge.

Jefferson treated the property as something held partly in trust. The nomination records that he surveyed the site himself in 1802 and made a map; that in 1803 he had a two-room log cabin built, one room kept open for visitors, with a book left there "for sentiments" that collected such names as Marshall, Monroe, Clay, Benton, Jackson, Van Buren, and Sam Houston; that after financial difficulties in 1809 he tried to sell the land and for a time leased it for saltpeter mining; and that in 1817 he agreed for a local freeman named Patrick Henry to live at the bridge as custodian. His 1814 lease to Philip Thornton required the tenant to make no erection and do nothing that would disfigure the bridge as a natural curiosity, and in a letter of March 15, 1815, quoted in the nomination, Jefferson wrote: "I view it in some degree as a public trust, and would on no consideration permit the bridge to be injured, defaced or masked from public view." The nomination reads those lease terms as one of the first attempts in America to establish a conservation easement. Natural Bridge passed out of Jefferson family possession in 1835, per the nomination; the DCR park page adds that the family was forced to sell after his death to recover from his debts, and that the bridge then passed from the Jefferson family to a Revolutionary War captain, a Civil War veteran and railroad tycoon, two hotel conglomerates, and finally to a Washington, D.C.-based businessman named Angelo Puglisi. Through all of it the bridge worked as a tourist destination, reached first by horseback and later by train and by car, its image carried worldwide by artists — the nomination notes an entire 1982 Washington and Lee University exhibition devoted to images of the bridge from 1787 to 1890.

The modern ending is the DCR park page's own account. In May 2013 Puglisi announced plans to sell the complex by year's end. On February 6, 2014, the nonprofit Virginia Conservation Legacy Fund came together around the property: Puglisi gifted the bridge and 188 acres — valued at $21 million, per the park page — to the fund, which agreed to pay for the remaining acreage with a loan. The state took over management of about 1,500 acres on **September 24, 2016**, without yet owning it outright. In 2022 the General Assembly voted to pay off the remaining debt, and on June 15, 2023, the park transferred fully to Virginia State Parks — Jefferson's hope to conserve the bridge realized, in the park page's closing judgment, over 200 years later.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a collapsed cave roof's last standing span — a semi-elliptical limestone arch over Cedar Creek in a steep gorge, unchanged in form, in the nomination's assessment, except for paths, railings, benches, and markers placed for visitors, and the wagon road, later U.S. Route 11, crossing its top. **Now:** the same arch at the center of a 1,635-acre state park, reached by the Cedar Creek Trail down 137 steps from the Visitor Center, continuing under the bridge past the Saltpeter Cave and the Lost River to Lace Falls, per the DCR park page.

**Then:** a private wonder — patented to Jefferson in 1774, held by his family until 1835, then passed owner to owner as a commercial attraction for the better part of two centuries. **Now:** a Virginia state park, managed by the Commonwealth from September 24, 2016, and fully transferred to Virginia State Parks on June 15, 2023, after the General Assembly paid off the remaining debt — Jefferson's "public trust" language made literal.

**Then:** an icon known mostly by its image — engravings, paintings, and travel accounts carried the arch to people who would never stand under it, and the nomination argues the image mattered more than the visitor count. **Now:** a glass dry-plate negative in the Library of Congress (§7) shows the arch exactly as the park visitor still finds it — the same opening, the same creek bed — because the feature itself does not change; only the trees do.

## 4. What's still there
- The Natural Bridge itself: a solid limestone arch spanning Cedar Creek in southern Rockbridge County, its top carrying U.S. Route 11 within a state right of way, per the National Historic Landmark nomination
- The Cedar Creek gorge and trail: from the Visitor Center down 137 steps, under the bridge, and on to the Saltpeter Cave, the Lost River, and Lace Falls (DCR park page)
- The "G.W." carving in the bridge face, outlined in a white box about 23 feet up, and a marked stone under the bridge inscribed "G.W. 1750 /1984" — both documented in the nomination, and both presented there as claimed, not proven, Washington handiwork (see §6)
- A U.S. Geological Survey bench mark set on top of the bridge in 1905, inscribed "1,150 feet above sea level," per the nomination- The Visitor Center with its Base Camp exhibits on the area's natural, historical, and cultural heritage, and more than 10 miles of park trails, including the Buck Hill, Blue Ridge, and Skyline trails (DCR park page)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Visitor Center:** Start where the DCR directs visitors to start — admission tickets and the Base Camp exhibits, before any walking.
2. **Down the 137 steps to Cedar Creek:** The approach the park is built around — the walkway following the gorge wall to the first full view of the arch, the point the nomination identifies as the landmark site's eastern boundary, where the path opens to the bridge.
3. **Under the arch:** The creek crossing beneath the span, with the marked "G.W." stone and the white-boxed carving in the far face — read with §6 open, as claimed carvings, not established fact.
4. **On along Cedar Creek:** Past the bridge to the Saltpeter Cave and the Lost River, ending at Lace Falls and its 50-foot cascade, per the DCR trail description.
5. **Back up, then the top in imagination only:** U.S. Route 11 still crosses the arch's top inside its right of way — the visitor takes that fact from below and from the park's exhibits, not from the fenced, gated top the nomination describes as closed for security.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The bridge's height has never been a single number, and the range is printed as the sources carry it. The National Historic Landmark nomination states the problem directly: because of the irregularity of the arch's shape, many different measurements have been quoted — approximately 200 feet as the commonly given figure; 198.5 feet by a 1927 measurement; 205 to 270 feet according to Thomas Jefferson; and 215 feet according to measurements made by French engineers during the American Revolution. The nomination gives the unbroken span as approximately 90 feet long, 100 feet wide, and 50 feet deep, and the rock's thickness as approximately 50 feet. The DCR park page's overview calls the bridge "the 200-foot tall Natural Bridge," while the figure most often carried in modern listings is 215 feet. No single height is printed in this draft as the height; the honest figure is the range, 198.5 to 215 feet in the measured sources, with Jefferson's higher estimates standing as his own.

The George Washington stories are legend, and the nomination says so in terms this draft adopts. Washington is claimed to have surveyed Natural Bridge about 1750, but the nomination states this "almost certainly is not true," because the site lies well south of the Fairfax line it was his job to survey, and Natural Bridge is not among the sites in the George Washington Atlas. Two legends link him to the bridge — that he carved his initials 23 feet up on the side of the bridge, and that he tossed a stone to the top — and the nomination calls both "widely accepted, though unsubstantiated." The carvings themselves are real as physical marks: the nomination documents a carving of "G.W." outlined in a white box in the far bridge face and a stone uncovered in 1927 bearing the initials "G.W." and a surveyor's cross. What is not documented is whose hand cut them. A 1926 Daughters of the American Revolution tablet at the site nonetheless commemorates the survey as fact; the tablet is a 1926 assertion, not a 1750 record, and it is printed here as such. The Jefferson patent, by contrast, is a record: a grant from King George III dated July 5, 1774, signed by Governor Dunmore, for 157 acres including the bridge, at a consideration of twenty shillings — supported by the nomination, by the DCR park page, and by the patent's quoted text, and printed in §2 as established.

The landmark dates disagree between the operator and the register. The DCR park page states the bridge "was listed on the National Register of Historic Places as a National Historic Landmark in 1988" — a sentence that does not match the NPS's own National Register record, which dates the Register listing to November 18, 1997 (NRIS 97001401), nor the Virginia DHR record, which carries the Landmark designation as August 5, 1998. The 1988 figure appears to be an error for 1998, but it is printed here as a discrepancy rather than silently corrected; the dates used in §1 are the Register record's and DHR's. The nomination itself carries two acreage figures for the landmark site — 5.86 acres in its description text and 6.86 acres in its geographical data — unexplained in the document; neither is used here for the park's size, which is the DCR's current 1,635 acres.

The first photograph's record (§7) carries a date range, not a year, and no photographer — so no capture year and no photographer are printed for it.
