## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** a beautifully preserved house can accidentally make a ranch look like a household with a nice view. The surviving building is only one part of a vanished working arrangement. The story becomes stronger when a family correction restores the distinction between domestic comfort and the spaces where other people slept, washed and worked.

Leland Roth's Oregon Historical Society encyclopedia account places the livestock and dairy enterprise in 1860 and the substantial house in 1898. Patrick Hughes died in 1901; Jane continued living there until 1923. Builder Pehr Johan Lindberg's patterned shingle work connects the residence to regional building craft. These are documented historical interpretations, not a reconstruction of Jane's private thoughts. [4]

**Named family testimony:** the National Register nomination includes corrections explicitly based on a February 18, 1981 telephone statement to the State Historic Preservation Office by **Patrick Masterson, Patrick Hughes's great-grandson**. It revises the identification of a prominent building in an approximately 1905 view: not the dairy barn first described, but a relocated washhouse and bunkhouse. Other working structures were separately identified. This is a preserved administrative summary of family testimony, not an audio recording or verbatim interview transcript. [5]

That distinction matters. Calling a building a barn erases its role in human accommodation and domestic work. Calling it a washhouse and bunkhouse reintroduces people into an architectural picture. This is an interpretive inference from the correction, not proof of a particular worker's daily schedule.

The same nomination says the ranch employed up to fourteen hands, many Irish immigrants. It does not supply a complete named workforce or payroll in the material inspected. Do not substitute the owner's family for every worker, and do not invent a ranch hand's voice. [5]

**Original visitor narration:** Look first at the house, then ask what is missing from the picture. A residence survives because somebody chose to preserve it. Workplaces and sleeping quarters can disappear while the labor that supported the house becomes harder to see. Here, a descendant's correction to an old photograph is enough to change the story: the building we thought held animals also belongs in a history of people.

No dramatized first-person speech is attributed to Jane, Patrick or a worker. The Society's living-history scenes are modern scripts, not automatically original family testimony. [2]

## 3. Then / now
- **Ranch enterprise and later residence are different milestones.** A house date is not the beginning of the family's entire business. [4]
- **Archival interpretation changed.** The nomination preserves a correction rather than concealing an initial misidentification. That is evidence of historical method, not a reason to select whichever building label makes the best story. [5]
- **Present use is selective public interpretation.** The operator identifies a historic house with seasonal tours, not a functioning public-access ranch workplace. [1]
- **The present view is incomplete.** The OHS account identifies vanished working structures, while the surviving house remains the visual anchor. A clear patch of ground is not a verified foundation, and an archive description is not an access permit. [4]

Do not re-create a visitor route through the missing buildings. The useful comparison is documentary: a working household and ranch complex then; a selectively preserved and interpreted residence now.

## 4. What's still there
The house is the public historical anchor. The Heritage Society reports a new roof in 2025 and further exterior renovation ahead; that makes maintenance part of the current story, not proof that every surface is original or every room accessible. [2]

The nomination survives as a dated preservation record with later correction sheets. It is useful for understanding why documentation itself requires review. Older plans for future use in that file are not current operating policy. [5]

Current room-by-room furnishings, individual object provenance, an installed labor-history display and the survival of any particular farm implement have not been verified. No object is promoted from illustration to an on-site promise. The house's setting may aid reflection only from lawful public space; neither the estuary nor surrounding ranch land becomes an exploration area.
