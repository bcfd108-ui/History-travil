## 2. The story
**One-minute reading.** The story is not how to find a burial mound or what was removed from it. It is what living people choose to convey about ancestral work, continuity and responsibility. A Caddo potter can show how clay is worked and a tradition renewed without displaying someone's burial belongings. Read Chase Kahwinhut Earles in his own words and the Wichita and Affiliated Tribes in their own published account, before reading the museum. [S3–S7]

**Five-minute reading.** Earles, identified by the Indian Arts and Crafts Board as Caddo, describes remaking a clay practice through research and learning to gather clay, prepare shell temper, burnish, fire and engrave. His artist statement explains that many culturally sensitive ancestral pieces shouldn't be displayed; he instead makes his own works and balances inherited methods against what he wants to express now. His own public Spiro work page names the source of an engraving design, and the Dallas Museum of Art names his modern Horse Tripod Vessel alongside the exhibition materials. Those are contemporary works with contemporary authorship, not things removed at Spiro. The Caddo Nation’s own history describes ancestral agricultural communities and the earthen structures they built, then traces forced displacement and institutional renewal. Its Language Preservation program distinguishes linguistic kinship from the separate histories and cultures of Caddoan-speaking nations. Related languages are not permission to merge Caddo and Wichita into a single voice. [S3–S5, S11]

Read the Wichita and Affiliated Tribes separately, not as a generalized “descendant voice.” Their own early-history account starts with Wichita understanding of history and is explicit about uncertainty when comparing archaeology with specific historic peoples. Their present-day account emphasizes endurance and cultural preservation after dispossession. Their official biography identifies Gary McAdams as a former cultural program planner; the original exhibition organizers credit McAdams and Terri Parton for Wichita participation and Tamara Francis and Phil Cross for Caddo participation. The credits record actual named collaboration, not endorsement of this dossier or a right to reproduce their exhibit. [S6–S9]

**Strongest story moment:** In the interpreted visitor space (or at home), read Earles’s distinction between making a work to be shared and displaying sensitive ancestral work, then turn to the Wichita-authored history instead of standing over a burial. Two sovereign communities are being heard in their own terms; a museum label is a third voice, not a substitute.

**Deep cut.** The lead museum and presentation partners credit the Caddo Nation and Wichita and Affiliated Tribes in the exhibition’s development. The OHS FAQ describes consultation with those nations concerning Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act matters, rather than presenting the collections as free documentary material. OHS and the exhibition describe the settlement and regional ties at different scales; the exhibition itself identifies differences in how scholars apply the term Mississippian in this region. Use “ancestral Spiro community” where classification is unnecessary and attribute interpretive labels. This dossier does not select a fixed ancient ethnicity on the basis of either modern sovereign identity or a museum label. [S2, S8–S10]
