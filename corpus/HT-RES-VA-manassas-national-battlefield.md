# HT-RES-VA-manassas-national-battlefield — The stone bridge in ruins, photographed the spring after the first army crossed it
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Two battles on the same Virginia farmland thirteen months apart — the first major land battle of the Civil War in July 1861, and a larger, harder fight in August 1862 that carried the Confederacy to the height of its power — both of them named for a railroad junction town and for the small stream, Bull Run, that runs across the ground.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a battlefield park, not a single building: Manassas National Battlefield Park, preserving the ground of the First Battle of Manassas (First Bull Run), fought July 21, 1861, and the Second Battle of Manassas (Second Bull Run), fought August 28–30, 1862, in Prince William County, Virginia, northwest of Manassas. The operator is the National Park Service. The terms below are printed as the NPS's own pages carry them. The NPS education page for the park states that there is no fee to visit and that the park does not charge fees. The NPS operating-hours page states that park grounds are open daily from dawn until dusk, and that the Henry Hill Visitor Center is open from 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. daily, including restrooms and the America's National Park Store, closed on Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve, Christmas Day, December 26, and New Year's Day; the park film plays hourly starting at 10:00 a.m., with the last showing at 4:00 p.m. The same page states that the Stone House reopens for the season on select dates and that the Brawner Farm Interpretive Center opens on select weekends, with the NPS directing visitors to check the park calendar or call the Henry Hill Visitor Center — (703) 361-1339, as that page carries the number — on the day of a visit to confirm; while the Brawner Farm building is closed, its grounds remain open sunrise to sunset. The NPS directs visitors to the Henry Hill Visitor Center at 6511 Sudley Road, Manassas, VA 20109. The park was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000039, per the NPS's own National Register record).

## 2. The story
In 1861 there was no town of Manassas on this ground. What mattered was Manassas Junction, where two railroads met, and the line of a small stream called Bull Run a few miles north of it. The NPS park brochure (2010) sets the first campaign plainly: on July 16, 1861, Brig. Gen. Irvin McDowell's Federal army — 35,000 strong, in the brochure's figure — marched out of Washington to capture Richmond and end the war, an army of green recruits, accompanied part of the way by citizens and congressmen with picnic baskets who expected a colorful show. Five miles ahead of the Federal advance at Centreville, Bull Run crossed the route, and guarding its fords from Union Mills to the Stone Bridge waited 22,000 Southern troops under Gen. P. G. T. Beauregard, in the brochure's figure. Beauregard asked Richmond for help, and Gen. Joseph E. Johnston, in the Shenandoah Valley with 10,000 Confederate troops, was ordered to support him if possible; Johnston slipped away from the Federal force facing him and moved his brigades toward Manassas Junction on the Manassas Gap Railroad, most of them arriving July 20 and 21.

On the morning of **July 21, 1861**, McDowell sent a diversionary attack against the Stone Bridge and marched the bulk of his force north to cross at Sudley Ford and strike the Confederate left. The NPS battle-detail summary for the battle (CWSAC reference VA005) records the shape of the day: McDowell crossed at Sudley Ford and attacked the Confederate left flank on Matthews Hill; fighting raged throughout the day as Confederate forces were driven back to Henry Hill; late in the afternoon, Confederate reinforcements — one brigade arriving by rail from the Shenandoah Valley — extended and broke the Union right flank, and the Federal retreat rapidly deteriorated into a rout. By July 22 the shattered Union army was back behind the defenses of Washington. Confederate Gen. Barnard E. Bee and Col. Francis Bartow were killed. The NPS summary's judgment is that the battle convinced the Lincoln administration the war would be a long and costly affair; McDowell was relieved and replaced by Maj. Gen. George B. McClellan. The NPS brochure adds the human measure its own way: at day's end nearly 900 men lay lifeless on the fields of Matthews Hill, Henry Hill, and Chinn Ridge, and ten hours of heavy fighting swept away any notion the war's outcome would be decided quickly. It was the first major land battle of the Civil War, and both sides' armies were, in the brochure's words, enthusiastic young volunteers who had gathered confident their foes would run at the first shot.

Thirteen months later the armies came back to the same fields as veterans. The NPS brochure's account of the second battle runs as follows. After McClellan's spring 1862 advance toward Richmond foundered, the Union created a new army under Gen. John Pope to push toward Richmond from Washington, and in August 1862 directed McClellan to transfer his forces to support Pope. Gen. Robert E. Lee, commanding the Confederate Army of Northern Virginia, moved first: in July he sent Gen. Thomas J. Jackson's corps against Pope, and in mid-August he transferred most of his army north to strike Pope before McClellan could fully reinforce him. When attempts to trap Pope between the Rapidan and Rappahannock failed, Lee split his force and sent Jackson on a fast march far around Pope's right flank to the Union supply depot at Manassas Junction. Jackson seized the junction and its supplies on **August 27, 1862**, then took up a hidden defensive line along an unfinished railroad grade about a mile northwest of the junction.

The NPS battle-detail summary for Second Manassas (CWSAC reference VA026) carries the three days. On **August 28**, to draw Pope's army into battle, Jackson attacked a Federal column passing across his front on the Warrenton Turnpike; the fighting at Brawner Farm lasted several hours and ended in stalemate. Pope became convinced he had trapped Jackson and concentrated the bulk of his army against him. On **August 29**, Pope launched a series of assaults against Jackson's position along the unfinished railroad grade; the attacks were repulsed with heavy casualties on both sides. At noon, Gen. James Longstreet arrived on the field from Thoroughfare Gap and took position on Jackson's right flank. On **August 30**, Pope renewed his attacks, seemingly unaware Longstreet was on the field; when massed Confederate artillery devastated a Union assault by Fitz John Porter's command, Longstreet's wing — 28,000 men, in the NPS summary's figure — counterattacked in what the summary calls the largest simultaneous mass assault of the war. The Union left flank was crushed and the army driven back to Bull Run. The NPS brochure's verdict is that the three days produced far greater carnage than the first battle — 3,300 killed, in its figure — and brought the Confederacy to the height of its power, paving the way for Lee's first invasion of the North; still, the battle did not weaken Northern resolve.

The ground was then kept, in stages. The NPS brochure's establishment note carries two dates: the Bull Run Recreational Demonstration Area on November 14, 1936, and Manassas National Battlefield Park on May 10, 1940. The National Register listing followed on October 15, 1966. The Virginia Department of Historic Resources records an expanded Manassas Battlefield Historic District of approximately 6,469.54 acres, of which approximately 5,073.10 acres lay within the authorized boundaries maintained by the NPS at the time of that updated nomination (VLR listed October 6, 2004; NRHP listed January 18, 2006, per the DHR register page).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a railroad junction, a turnpike, and a stream — Manassas Junction where two railroads met, the Warrenton Turnpike running east–west, and Bull Run crossing the Federal route of advance, its fords guarded from Union Mills to the Stone Bridge. **Now:** a National Battlefield Park of more than 5,000 acres in the Virginia Piedmont in Fairfax and Prince William counties, in the NPS's cultural-landscapes description, preserving the sites of both battles, with farm landscapes — the Henry Farmstead, the John Dogan Farmstead, and Chinn Ridge — kept as tangible reminders of the ground the armies fought over.

**Then:** the Stone Bridge over Bull Run, seized by the Confederates in July 1861 and defended as the anchor of their line, its passage contested again in 1862. **Now:** a stone bridge stands again on the site at the Fairfax–Prince William county line north of the Lee Highway (Warrenton Turnpike) crossing — but the bridge in the first photograph (§7) is the original in ruins in March 1862, its center span down in the creek, and the visitor should not mistake the present crossing for the structure the armies used.

**Then:** farmhouses turned to war use — the Stone House at the intersection of Sudley Road and the turnpike serving as a hospital in both battles, in the NPS's site descriptions. **Now:** the Stone House and the Brawner Farm, where the second battle opened, are park interpretive sites, open seasonally and on select dates as the NPS hours page states, their grounds open with the park from dawn to dusk.

## 4. What's still there
- The battlefield ground itself: Matthews Hill, Henry Hill, and Chinn Ridge from the first battle; Brawner Farm, the Unfinished Railroad Grade, and the Deep Cut from the second — all named in the NPS's own battle summaries and brochure
- The Stone Bridge site over Bull Run at the Fairfax–Prince William county line, where the Union retreated across after Second Manassas (NPS site descriptions)
- The Stone House, used as a hospital during both battles, near the intersection of Sudley Road and Lee Highway (NPS site descriptions)
- Brawner Farm, the opening phase of the second battle, at the western edge of the battlefield off Pageland Lane, renovated as an interpretive center for the Second Battle (NPS site descriptions)
- The Henry Hill Visitor Center at 6511 Sudley Road, with exhibits, the park film playing hourly from 10:00 a.m., and the park store (NPS hours and directions pages)
- The Bee Monument on Henry Hill, erected July 21, 1939, marking where Gen. Barnard E. Bee fell (NPS monument page; see §6 on the words it carries)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Henry Hill Visitor Center, 6511 Sudley Road:** Start where the NPS directs visitors to start — the exhibits and the park film, on the hill where the first battle was decided on the afternoon of July 21, 1861.
2. **On the first battle's morning ground:** Matthews Hill and the Sudley Ford crossing, where McDowell's flanking column came down on the Confederate left after the feint at the Stone Bridge.
3. **At the Stone Bridge:** The crossing itself, read against the March 1862 photograph in §7 — the ruined abutments and the creek as Barnard and Gibson's camera found them the spring after the battle.
4. **At the Stone House:** The turnpike landmark of both battles, hospital in each, at the Sudley Road intersection.
5. **On the second battle's ground:** Brawner Farm, where Jackson opened the fight on the evening of August 28, 1862, then the Unfinished Railroad Grade and the Deep Cut, where Jackson's line held through August 29 and 30, and finally Chinn Ridge and Henry Hill again — the ground Longstreet's counterattack swept toward on August 30, and the ground the Union held until dark to cover its retreat across Bull Run.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The strength and casualty figures do not agree across the NPS's own publications, and the disagreements are printed rather than resolved. For the first battle, the NPS battle-detail summary (VA005) gives estimated casualties of 4,878 total — 2,896 Union and 1,982 Confederate. The NPS Historical Handbook for the park, as its text is carried in the Gutenberg edition, gives Federal strength of 35,732 with losses of 2,708, and Confederate strength of 31,810 with losses of 1,982. The Confederate loss figure agrees; the Union loss figure does not — 2,896 in one NPS publication, 2,708 in another — and the opening strengths differ by source as well: the NPS brochure puts McDowell at 35,000 and Beauregard at 22,000 with Johnston's 10,000 in the valley, figures that do not sum to the handbook's. For the second battle, the NPS battle-detail summary (VA026) gives estimated casualties of 22,179 total — 13,826 Union and 8,353 Confederate — while the NPS brochure gives 3,300 killed for the three days, a killed-only figure that is not the same measure as the summary's killed, wounded, captured, and missing. All figures are carried here as their sources state them; this draft does not reconcile them.

The "Stonewall" nickname story is tradition, and it is labeled as such. The story as the NPS tells it: on Henry Hill on July 21, 1861, Gen. Barnard E. Bee, trying to rally his broken troops, pointed to Gen. Thomas J. Jackson's Virginians and declared words to the effect of "There stands Jackson like a stone wall," and the name stuck to Jackson and his brigade. Bee was mortally wounded that day and died the following day, so the words survive only as others reported them, and the NPS's own publications do not report them the same way. The Bee Monument's inscription, per the NPS monument page, reads: "Form, form, there stands Jackson like a stone wall, rally behind the Virginians!" The NPS Historical Handbook's narrative has Bee shout: "Look! There is Jackson standing like a stone wall! Rally behind the Virginians!" A park wayside version ends the sentence differently again — "Let us go to his assistance!" The event is not in dispute; the exact words are, and no wording is printed in §2 as a verified quotation for that reason.

The park's establishment carries two dates in the NPS brochure — November 14, 1936 for the Bull Run Recreational Demonstration Area and May 10, 1940 for Manassas National Battlefield Park — and both are printed as the brochure carries them; the 1936 date belongs to the federal recreational demonstration area that preceded the battlefield park, not to the park itself. The National Register record consulted (NRIS 66000039) carries no latitude or longitude, so the locator below prints none — coordinates are not taken from lead-list sources. That record's location line reads "NW of Manassas off VA 215," a road designation the current NPS pages do not use for the park (the visitor center is on Sudley Road, Route 234); the record is printed as it carries it, and the discrepancy is noted rather than corrected silently.

## 7. Photo record
**First photograph:** *Battlefield of Bull Run, ruins of the stone bridge, photographed March 1862* — an albumen print on a card mount, presented as the Library of Congress serves the digital file: in the foreground the dark, roofless abutment of the Stone Bridge stands at the left, and across Bull Run the far abutment's coursed stonework still stands while the bridge's center is down, its rubble in the creek below; a dirt road climbs away from the crossing between bare, battle-cut fields toward a low horizon of stripped trees, and a single small seated figure rests on the near abutment at the left edge. No dead, no wounded, and no wreckage of war beyond the bridge itself appear in the frame; it is the crossing as terrain and ruin. Inspected from the downloaded file before captioning.
