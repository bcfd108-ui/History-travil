## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** Preservation here is not simply keeping an old fort standing. It is making a place associated with outside control serve the people whose homeland it occupies.

**Specific sovereignty:** This dossier concerns the **White Mountain Apache Tribe**, not a generic Apache Nation. It does not substitute the histories or visitor permissions of San Carlos Apache, Fort Sill Apache or other sovereign communities. The Foundation says it was chartered by this Tribe in 1998 and assists its heritage work. It describes management transfer from the Bureau of Indian Affairs in 2007 and the effort to return the property to active community service. [2]

**Named first-person public account:** A May 13, 2021 Foundation press release quotes then-Tribal Chairwoman **Gwendena Lee-Gatewood** describing the community's stake in interpretation: **“Our community needs and deserves a safe and beautiful place to commemorate our history”**. Her statement places Apache practitioners, artists and students ahead of outside visitor-experience designers. The release also quotes Foundation CEO **Krista Beazley** welcoming deeper community engagement. [3]

**Provenance matters:** This is a release issued under the Tribe-chartered Foundation's name, hosted by partner Archaeology Southwest. It is not a document independently retrieved from the Tribal government's own server, nor an individual oral-history recording. The distinction remains explicit rather than upgrading its publishing provenance. The release reports a $100,000 NEA Our Town planning grant; a grant award is not proof that the resulting construction or visitor experience was completed. [3]

**A later named voice, independently published:** Paulette Beete's 2023 *American Artscape* article identifies Beazley as White Mountain Apache and describes listening sessions and continuing work. Beazley connects revitalization with language preservation and community needs. This is a federal arts-agency publication reporting her words, not Tribal-government publication. [4]

**Interpretive synthesis:** The strongest story is a change in who gets to set the purpose of preservation. An outsider may see old architecture; the public accounts describe a question of future use and authority. A respectful visit should keep that distinction visible without expecting community members to perform their history for a visitor.

## 3. Then / now
The Foundation's institutional history identifies the military post's establishment in 1870, its abandonment by the Army in 1922 and the beginning of the Theodore Roosevelt boarding school in 1923. [2] These dates explain the layers of institutional power; no tactical account or military reenactment is proposed.

The 2021 release describes a planned process, while the 2023 article describes ongoing community-oriented work. [3,4] Neither establishes a complete 2026 transformation. “Then/now” here compares documented institutional purposes, not a claim that historical harm has been resolved or that every reused building is public.

A museum visit can encounter an interpretation of that history without recreating the path of a student or entering a former dormitory. No current photograph has been aligned with a period image.

## 4. What's still there
The museum's public interpretive offering is directly verified on its operator's Visit page. [1] The surrounding landmark is not treated as a single open-air exhibit available without distinctions.

The 2023 NEA article discusses adapting buildings for community needs and distinguishes aspirations from projects still needing support. [4] A surviving structure may therefore serve people rather than visitors. A heritage designation does not erase occupancy, privacy or ongoing work.

The Tribe's older museum listing was visible in search results but repeatedly timed out on direct retrieval. It is logged as a corroborating lead, not used to assert a particular exhibition, fee or artifact currently on display. [5] No sacred content is reconstructed from search snippets.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Proposed indoor interpretive progression, conditional on admission; not a mapped route:**

1. Before arriving, read the named public statement and identify whose institution this is. Keep its date visible.
2. At the admitted museum, establish current visitor conditions and remain within the public space. A closed door is a reason to defer, not seek another entry.
3. Follow the interpretation actually offered. Attend to named makers, narrators and institutional authors where labels supply them; do not supply identities or ceremonial meanings that labels omit.
4. Where the interpretation addresses military or boarding-school harm, the game goes quiet. Read and reflect without collecting a proof photograph.
5. End with the strongest story moment: return to the community's stated purpose for the place. The measure of preservation is not how much a visitor can enter but how the people concerned shape its future.

This is research that prepares observation, not trivia. No answer keys, forced questions or completion test are included. No extension onto cultural, school or residential ground is proposed.
