# Almo "Horrible Indian Massacre" Monument and the paper trail of a fabricated frontier event

**Research draft · Labeled THEME SWEEP (Family 4 — Weird spots, documented hoax with a paper trail) · Almo, Cassia County, Idaho · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate

Reflection stop. The **Almo "Horrible Indian Massacre" Monument** stands in the open beside the public rural intersection of **Elba-Almo Road and East 3000 South** in the village of **Almo, Cassia County, Idaho** (near postal reference `3001 Elba-Almo Road, Almo, ID 83312`, on the right when traveling south on Elba-Almo Road). This entry covers the **1938 granite monument and the documented historical fabrication behind it**—distinct from the catalog's existing entries for the nearby California Trail and City of Rocks National Reserve (`ID-city-of-rocks`, `ID-city-of-rocks-california-trail`).

## 2. The story

Standing roughly six feet tall and carved in the outline of the State of Idaho, the stone marker at Almo's main crossroads bears a stark bronze-and-stone claim: *"Dedicated to the memory of those who lost their lives in a most horrible Indian massacre 1861. Three hundred immigrants west bound. Only five escaped."* The monument was erected in **1938** by the **Sons and Daughters of Idaho Pioneers** along the Raft River / City of Rocks approach to the California Trail. [1, 2]

Had 295 emigrants actually been killed along Almo Creek in 1861, it would have ranked second only to the 1813 Fort Mims attack in Alabama as the deadliest Indian-white frontier clash of nineteenth-century North America. Yet decades of historical scrutiny established that the massacre never occurred—making the monument a textbook example of an invented pioneer legend fixed in granite. [2, 3]

Tracing the paper trail backward, retired University of Utah historian **Brigham D. Madsen** published *"The 'Almo Massacre' Revisited"* in the Idaho State Historical Society's quarterly ***Idaho Yesterdays*** (Vol. 37, 1993). Madsen found **no contemporary newspaper account, U.S. Army War Department dispatch, or federal Indian-agent report** mentioning any such 1861 massacre near Almo—and even the local legend never fixed a calendar month or day beyond the bare year `1861`. National Park Service Chief Historian **Edwin C. Bearss** endorsed Madsen's research in January 1994, noting that a loss of nearly 300 travelers would immediately have appeared in Salt Lake City, California, and Eastern newspapers. Idaho State Historical Society historian **Merle Wells** independently confirmed Madsen's findings. [2, 3]

Madsen traced the earliest written appearance of the tale not to 1861, but to **Charles S. Walgamott's 1927 reminiscences**, in which Walgamott recalled visiting the area in 1875 with an unnamed "old trapper" who told a vivid story of besieged wagons and warriors riding into Brigham City, Utah. According to Madsen's research, the story gained forceful local momentum in the **1930s**, when area boosters and newspaper editors promoted it to attract tourists toward nearby **City of Rocks** and build support for a proposed local irrigation project—culminating in the 1938 stone monument. [2]

Public recognition of the fabrication in 1994 highlighted the monument's human and civic weight. **Keith Tinno**, chairman of the **Shoshone-Bannock Tribes** at Fort Hall, noted in January 1994 that tribal oral tradition contained no record of any Almo attack and called the monument an unjust accusation against his ancestors, requesting a public apology and suggesting—as Madsen proposed—replacing the plaque with one honoring **Chief Pocatello**. **Della Mullinix**, then 82-year-old president of the **Sons & Daughters of Idaho Pioneers**, publicly apologized to the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes on behalf of pioneer descendants if the marker caused hurt, while noting that rededicating the stone itself would be difficult for her small chapter. Meanwhile, longtime Almo residents and retired local teacher **Bill Jones** described growing up believing the tale before learning the archival record contradicted it. [2]

### Haunted history / lore

No haunted-site or ghost-sighting tradition is the focus of the historical record here; the Almo monument's strangeness lies in **documented twentieth-century mythmaking**—how a 1927 second-hand frontier yarn and 1930s booster campaign produced a permanent state-shaped stone monument to an event that left zero 1861 primary evidence. [2]
