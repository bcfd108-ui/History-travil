## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** Two peoples can share a government without becoming the same people. A museum can preserve that distinction while helping a community tell its own story.

**The Community's own framing:** SRPMIC's *The Peoples* page identifies **Onk Akimel O’odham** and **Xalychidom Piipaash** as distinct peoples with different cultures and languages, allied over generations and now joined in one federally recognized political community. It explains Onk Akimel O’odham as Salt River People, rather than treating the externally applied name Pima as the only identity. Its account similarly distinguishes Piipaash self-identification from the commonly applied Maricopa name. [3]

**Named, Tribe-published voice:** In Dodie Manuel's November 2, 2022 *O’odham Action News* report, Cultural Resources Department Director **Kelly Washington** describes a collaborative exhibition at a separate Mesa institution. He links representation to lived school memories and says: **“We’re the indigenous people of this area”**. The report concerns the old Lehi school and a 2022 event, **not this museum's building or a current invitation to another location**. [4]

The newspaper identifies SRPMIC as its publisher while expressly assigning responsibility for articles to their authors. This is Community-published reporting of a named speaker, not a Tribal resolution and not an audio recording heard here. [4]

**A second named account, about this museum:** Manuel's January 19, 2023 renovation report quotes Museum Director **Gary Owens** describing work in the former Salt River Youth Home. It reports construction beginning in 1965 and completion the next year. Public Works Senior Project Manager **Thomas Gardner** comments on the older builders' expertise: **“The people who built this knew what they were doing”**. The article describes how renovation prompted further gathering of community memories. [5]

**Interpretive synthesis:** The museum is more than a container for objects. Its own building has a social history, and the people preserving it are sources in that history. Listening does not require opening restricted spaces or assigning every memory to one indistinct culture. Nor should the visitor assume that the publicly told parts are the whole story.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a building served a Community purpose before becoming a museum; the 2023 report records how renovation made that earlier life newly visible to its caretakers. [5]

**Now:** the Community presents its own public interpretation and describes ongoing language revitalization. Its language page distinguishes O’odham's Uto-Aztecan/Tepiman background from Piipaash's Yuman background, rather than presenting them as two names for the same language. [6]

This is a comparison of documented functions and continuing authorship, not a claim that visitors can enter former residential rooms, inspect renovation work or locate an individual's memories. No original-room photographic match has been established.

## 4. What's still there
The publicly offered resource is the museum gallery. Its institutional website also describes collections and archives, but those holdings are not all public display, and their existence does not grant research access. [1]

The Community-published articles preserve named accounts of representation, building knowledge and memory work. [4,5] Those accounts can be read without visiting any original location they mention.

This entry does not substitute the similarly named Gila River institution for Salt River's museum. Related cultural contexts do not erase different governments, institutions or admission conditions. No generalized claim about all O’odham or Piipaash people is made from a single article.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Proposed interpretive sequence within admitted public space, not a mapped route:**

1. **Before entry:** read the Community's own names and distinctions. Separate the political community from the two peoples' histories and languages.
2. **At the gallery:** confirm that it is open and follow current staff and posted conditions. Do not seek a back entrance or another Community facility if it is closed.
3. **Look for attribution:** notice whether public interpretation identifies a speaker, artist, family or institutional author. Do not assign a language or affiliation where a label does not do so.
4. **Read the building account:** use the 2023 article to consider the labor of preserving memory. Do not attempt to expose or inspect original fabric.
5. **Strongest story moment:** end with Gardner's recognition of earlier builders' knowledge. History here is also living people recognizing the skill of people before them, not outsiders discovering an anonymous past.

**Optional question for staff, if welcome:** How does the current gallery keep O’odham and Piipaash accounts distinct while presenting the Community's shared history?

No participant is asked to demonstrate, translate or disclose restricted knowledge. Any accounts of loss are reflection only, without scoring or reenactment. If closed, use the sources in the same sequence as an archive-only session.
