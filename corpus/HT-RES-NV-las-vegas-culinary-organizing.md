## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** a housekeeper could finish a paid shift and still have another kind of work ahead: helping people at a different hotel defend their jobs.

**Pom Fritz**, interviewed by **Kristel Peralta and Stefani Evans on June 8, 2021**, remembers working at the Desert Inn while supporting Frontier strikers. Her union service included steward and leadership roles. Her explanation of solidarity is plain: **“I see everybody is an employee just like me.”** The interview connects that recognition with insisting on respectful treatment and learning the contract. [3, transcript pp.8–10 and 15]

This is a named worker's retrospective account, not an employer's personnel file or a verified legal interpretation of any contract. The HTML transcript was read; audio was not played. No voice performance or recording timestamp is claimed.

**Original interpretation:** visitor-facing hospitality often makes labor look effortless. This story makes the time and relationships behind that appearance visible. Unlike the Moulin Rouge dossier's public-accommodations focus, its center is workplace representation and worker-to-worker solidarity. It is also not a practical guide for an ongoing dispute.

## 3. Then / now

Culinary Local 226's own institutional history dates its charter to **November 1, 1935**. It identifies **Hattie Canty**, formerly a hotel housekeeper, as the first Black woman and first guest room attendant elected union president, in **1990**. It remembers the Frontier strike as lasting **six years, four months and ten days**. [4] These are union-published historical claims; celebratory superlatives and current membership assertions are not adopted here as independently audited facts.

UNLV separately preserves Canty's interviews with **Claytee White**, dated **February 27 and June 17, 1998**, under **OH-00334**. Its catalogue describes her discussion of worker relations, negotiations and training. [5] The full Canty PDF failed retrieval during this check, so no direct quotation from her is presented as independently read. This archival presence shows a second named perspective exists, not that this draft has exhausted it.

The university archive is a contemporary place to examine these histories, not a reconstruction of the work sites. No current union membership, employment, contract terms or employer practices are inferred from the historical interviews.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Clear the institutional gate.** Use a confirmed library visit or the remote transcript, not the entrance of an employer or union office.
2. **Start with a worker, not a skyline.** Establish who is speaking, the interview date and the difference between remembering and contemporaneous documentation.
3. **Read the short excerpt.** Let its comparison between workers carry the story without imitating the narrator's accent or staging a picket.
4. **Deep layer:** distinguish three kinds of evidence: a worker's account, a union's institutional history and independently preserved records. They can support one another while still leaving questions open.
5. **Strongest story moment:** consider the time after a paid shift. What does it mean to treat another workplace's struggle as something that concerns you, too? Leave this as reflection, not an instruction to approach present-day workers.

This proposed sequence can take place at a reading table. No route to a strike footprint, staff door or private home is needed.
