# Hydro — Lucille Hamons and the work under the bay

## 2 · The story

### The right to stay beside the road

The National Register nomination says Carl Ditmore bought the land in December 1928, erected the station in 1929, and leased it to Transcontinental Oil that year. Carl Ethel bought the property in July 1929. According to the nomination, the 1930 right-of-way sale was for five dollars with a covenant that the station would be left undisturbed. W. O. and Ida Waldroup renamed the station Provine after the 1934 sale. The nomination gives the documentary sequence and records; the original conveyance and covenant were not inspected. [4]

By 1941, the Hamons family had taken over. The living quarters above the open vehicle bay put the household directly over the business. A traveler could drive beneath the projecting floor to buy fuel. The family could live where the road brought them work. The historic register treats the combination of dwelling and station as a reason the property matters. [1,4]

### Work done after dark, and after the interstate

In journalist Dave Hoekstra’s published account of his interviews, Lucille Hamons remembered, “I’d keep canned goods for those cotton pickers.” Her description goes on to recall baking hams and people camping in tents. This was roadside grocery and food work alongside selling fuel. Hoekstra had seen her operating the station in 1991 and returned with a notebook in 1996. His article is the source of the published first-person quotation. [5]

The 1971 interstate rerouting cut the station off from direct access to the main flow of motorists. NPS describes regular customers who still found their way to the beer cooler. The nomination records the switch from Phillips 66 to Conoco gasoline. Hoekstra also records that Hamons closed her tourist cabins in the early 1980s and quotes her explanation: “It got to be too much.” Read that line from a lawful place outside. The landmark is a business whose operator made repeated choices about what one person could keep running. [1,4–5]

The Smithsonian preserves a Hamons Court neon sign. It is a distinct object in a national collection. It helps establish the lodging business through an actual artifact while the present drive-up stop is the surviving station. [6]

## 3 · Then / now

- **Historic location.** Original station and living quarters on the former U.S. 66 alignment south of Hydro, with a related cabin/motel operation that requires separate preservation and parcel records. [1,4–6]
- **Lawful physical anchor.** Station’s visitor exterior, about half a mile west of the OK-58/I-40 junction on historic Route 66 according to NPS. The associated restorer’s account invites exterior photo stops. Only use an open, clearly distinguishable off-road visitor spot alongside the station. If the spot is unclear, occupied or signed private, continue on the public road. No standing in the travel lane or crossing for the memorial crosses. [1–3]
- **What changed.** The station stopped operating. The exterior has been restored, and later reporting documents tornado damage in 2022. A restoration date and an inventory of later replacement fabric are open. [1–3,7]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- Hoekstra’s article puts the original building in 1927 by describing it as a year after the 1926 commission of Route 66. NPS and the nomination say 1929 and the nomination cites the 1928 purchase and 1929 lease and sale. The conflicting 1927 claim is retained. [1,4–5]
- The 1991 nomination interview with Lucille is cited in the form, but its audio or transcript was not retrieved. Hoekstra’s published quotations establish their own provenance. The county deeds and the 1930 covenant were not independently retrieved. [4–5]
- NPS and the affiliated restorer document the exterior invitation. An exact visitor-bay boundary, weather closure process, repair chronology, gate status and present ownership record were not independently confirmed. The draft trip instructions keep the outside stop conditional. [1–3,7]

## Connected ground — Route 66 — Springfield to Tucumcari

The Provine station at Hydro was one stop in the Oklahoma Route 66 sequence that ran west through Weatherford and Clinton to Elk City, and the 1971 interstate rerouting cut it off from the traffic it was built to serve.
