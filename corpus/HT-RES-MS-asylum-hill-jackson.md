## 2. The story

Authorized by the Mississippi Legislature in the late 1840s and opened in **1855** on high ground roughly two miles north of the old State Capitol, the **Mississippi State Lunatic Asylum** (renamed the **Mississippi State Hospital for the Insane** in **1901**) was Mississippi's primary public psychiatric institution for eighty years. Between **1855 and 1935**, the hospital served an estimated **30,000 patients** from every county in the state, including large numbers of African American residents in segregated wards as well as patients committed for treatable medical conditions such as pellagra, epilepsy, heart disease, or age-related dementia. [1, 2, 3]

When patients died and families could not be notified in time—or lacked the means to claim and transport a body before the era of hospital refrigeration—the asylum buried them within 24 hours in an institutional cemetery on the **northeast ridge of the grounds ("Asylum Hill")**, marking each grave with an impermanent wooden marker that eventually rotted away. In **1935**, the state moved the institution to its newly built campus at **Whitfield** (Rankin County), and the north Jackson property was later transferred for construction of the **University of Mississippi Medical Center (UMMC)**, which opened in **1955**. [1, 2, 3]

Over the decades, above-ground traces of the cemetery vanished beneath woodland and campus infrastructure:

- **July 15, 1992:** Utility crews trenching a steam line uncovered **44 burials**. The remains were carefully recovered and reinterred on **November 8, 1994**, in a consecrated campus plot; **31** of those graves bore surviving headstone inscriptions recorded by the Asylum Hill Project. [1]
- **Late 2012:** Road construction uncovered a pine coffin and led to archaeological recovery of **66 individuals** (plus fragmentary remains of two others) analyzed at Mississippi State University. [1, 2]
- **2013–2014:** Remote-sensing and ground-penetrating radar surveys conducted by UMMC, the University of Mississippi, and Mississippi State University's Cobb Institute of Archaeology mapped individual grave shafts across roughly twenty acres, estimating **up to 7,000 burials**. [1, 2]

In **2013**, scholars, bioarchaeologists, historians, and UMMC leaders formed the **Asylum Hill Research Consortium** (including UMMC's Dr. Ralph Didlake, lead bioarchaeologist Dr. Jennifer Mack, research coordinator Lida Gibson/Key, and MSU's Dr. Molly Zuckerman and Derek Anderson) alongside the **Mississippi Department of Archives and History (MDAH)** and Native American stakeholders. Funded by **$3.7 million** in state legislative appropriations, systematic machine-stripped, hand-excavated burial recovery began in late **2022** after clearing a four-acre wooded section behind privacy fencing. [2]

By **May 2026**, archaeologists had excavated **more than 1,000 individual graves**—confirming orderly, individual coffins (including a cast-iron casket on the ridge) rather than mass graves, and recovering buttons, pins, coins, jewelry, and clothing traces. Using surviving **1912–1935 death certificates** and biennial legislative reports at MDAH, researchers work with descendants to return identified remains to families where possible and are planning a dedicated **memorial and mausoleum** to house unclaimed remains with dignity. [2, 3]

## 4. What's still there

- **Subsurface historical resource (NON-PUBLIC / PROTECTED):** An estimated 7,000 nineteenth- and early twentieth-century asylum graves under active archaeological recovery by the Asylum Hill Project inside privacy-fenced UMMC zones, plus surviving **1912–1935 death certificates** and biennial asylum reports preserved at the **Mississippi Department of Archives and History (MDAH)** and **UMMC Medical History Museum**. [1, 2, 3]
- **Gone:** All nineteenth-century asylum buildings (replaced by UMMC facilities after 1955). [1, 2]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Pre-1912 burial registers:** While death certificates and burial lists survive for **1912–1935**, earlier patient burial registers (1855–1911) and any original cemetery plot map are missing, leaving most individual graves unmarked and unnamed pending archival/osteobiographical correlation. [2, 3]
- **Final excavation timeline & memorial funding:** Multi-year recovery was estimated at five to seven years in October 2022, and permanent mausoleum/memorial construction remained in the planning/funding stage as of May 2026 reporting. [2, 3]
- **Unresolved exact burial total:** `7,000` remains a geophysical estimate rather than a final count until all recovery sectors are completed. [2, 3]
