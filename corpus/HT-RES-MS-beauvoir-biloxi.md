# Beauvoir and the making of Jefferson Davis’s account of the Confederacy

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP22 · Labeled research draft · Quiet reflection.*

## 2. Story
James Brown had a raised Gulf Coast cottage built in the mid-nineteenth century. Its veranda and tall windows put air movement and shade to work before mechanical cooling. Rice University credits Brown with design and construction, but the nomination describes the assembled building more readily than it identifies its workforce. The workers who made or maintained it are still missing from this account. [2, 3, 4]

Sarah Anne Ellis Dorsey had already lived at Beauvoir with her husband Samuel before Jefferson Davis came to write there. According to the editors of his papers at Rice, in early 1877 he moved into her library cottage and began dictating his history to her. In its account of his later years the National Register nomination also identifies his wife Varina and daughter as helping him prepare the book. At this house, the work of writing involved more people than the author named on the title page. [2, 3]

The estate was contested. In the published Supreme Court record, a copy of Dorsey’s January 4, 1878 will calls Davis “my most honored and esteemed friend.” The same case prints relatives’ allegations of undue influence and refers to the conveyance of February 19, 1879. Read the will as reproduced in a court record and the allegations as pleadings. The Court’s disposition concerns the legal remedy and forum available in Louisiana. It does not supply a historical finding that the relatives’ allegations were true. [5]

Davis’s retrospective book took shape here. Mississippi’s declaration published upon secession in 1861 provides a contemporary public record of the cause its convention put first: “Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery”. Read the two sources in their own time: one belonged to the act of secession and the other to the defense of the defeated government. After Davis’s death, Varina prepared her own memoir. Rice traces ownership through their daughter Winnie and back to Varina, who sold the property in 1903 for use as a Confederate soldiers’ home. [3, 5, 6]

## 3. Then and now
The early photograph and survey show a front stair, long veranda, tall openings and raised occupied floor. The house survived Hurricane Katrina with serious damage and reopened to tours after restoration in 2008. The library cottage where Davis dictated and the Hayes cottage were destroyed. Rice dates completion of replicas to 2009. The present museum campus and pavilions therefore contain different kinds of evidence: surviving house fabric, repaired parts, reconstructed outbuildings and later interpretation. [1–4, 7]

The 200 West Beach Road address in the federal architectural survey is historical. Use the current operator’s 2244 Beach Boulevard admissions address. [1, 4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements
- Rice describes purchase arrangements as incomplete at Dorsey’s death. The relatives’ legal pleading in [5] identifies and attacks a February 19 conveyance. The instruments and probate record would help separate agreement, title and payment. The register’s short account stresses inheritance. [2, 3, 5]
- The 1960s-era register describes the pre-storm property. Its condition and fabric assessment cannot establish which members of the house or dependencies survived Katrina. Use the later restoration record for modern interpretation. [2, 3]
- The independent craft and labor records for the house and veterans’ home remain open. The page and plain-text endpoints for a free transcription of Davis’s first volume returned HTTP 503 in retrieval. This draft attributes the book’s work history to Rice and the nomination and takes its direct political quote from the 1861 Mississippi declaration. [2, 3, 6, 8–9]
