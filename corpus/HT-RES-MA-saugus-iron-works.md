# HT-RES-MA-saugus-iron-works — Hammersmith, 1646: Where American Iron Began

Division MA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

On the south bank of the Saugus River in Saugus, Massachusetts, the National Park Service preserves the ground where Hammersmith, the first sustained, integrated iron works in British Colonial America, operated from 1646 to 1670. The blast furnace, forge, and rolling and slitting mill a visitor walks through today are careful 1950s reconstructions raised on the excavated foundations of the original plant — and the one piece of the works that survives untouched is not a building at all, but the grass-covered pile of slag the founders threw into the river.

## 1. Trip gate

Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site is operated by the National Park Service. The park address is 244 Central Street, Saugus, MA 01906. Admission is free: the park's Fees & Passes page states "Free — No entrance pass required," and that an entrance pass is not required to access Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site.

The park's Basic Information page (last updated September 9, 2026) gives the season as May 7 – November 13. Within the season: the park grounds and nature trail are open 24/7; the Visitor Center is open Wednesday – Sunday, 9:30 am – 4:00 pm; the Saugus Iron Works Museum and the Industrial Site Buildings are open Wednesday – Sunday, 9:30 am – 4:30 pm; restrooms are open Wednesday – Sunday 9:30 am – 4:30 pm and Monday – Tuesday 9:30 am – 3:00 pm (closed Monday federal holidays). Ranger programs in season run Wednesday – Sunday: Waterwheel Demonstrations (30 minutes) at 10:00 am, the Iron Works House Tour (30 minutes) at 11:00 am, and the Industrial Site Tour (60 minutes, including the waterwheel demonstrations) at 2:00 pm. Outside the season — the park's visitor-center listing gives winter hours of November 14 – May 6 — the buildings are closed while the grounds remain open.

The visitor boundary is that of a working archaeological site, not an open field. The industrial buildings are entered only during building hours. The slag pile at the river's edge is original seventeenth-century manufacturing waste and a protected archaeological resource: it is viewed from the riverbank paths and the dock, and it is not public ground to walk on. Everything above the foundations is a 1950s reconstruction; the ground itself — foundations, wheel pits, and the slag pile — is the original record.

## 2. The story

The Massachusetts Bay colonists needed iron for the basic work of settlement — axes, saws, hoes, nails, pots, and kettles — and every piece of it had to come across the Atlantic until someone built a works in New England. John Winthrop the Younger began his ironworks venture in 1641. The enterprise was carried by a consortium of English and colonial investors, the Company of Undertakers of the Iron Works, and its first attempt was not at Saugus: an earlier English attempt at Falling Creek, Virginia (ca. 1621–1622) had failed, and the Undertakers' own first plant at Braintree, Massachusetts (c. 1644–1647), preceded the move to the Saugus River.

The second site, in what was then Lynn, Massachusetts, was called Hammersmith. It began operations in 1646, and what made it different from everything before it in America was integration: Hammersmith was the first site to successfully carry out the full range of iron production and refinement at one facility, producing cast iron, refined bars, and nails under one management on one riverbank. The National Park Service describes it as the first sustained, integrated iron works in British Colonial America, which operated on the Saugus River from 1646 to 1670. A blast furnace smelted bog ore into cast iron and slag; a forge refined pig iron into wrought-iron bars; a rolling and slitting mill cut bars into rods sized for nails and other small work. Waterwheels on the Saugus River drove the bellows, hammers, and rolls. The park's significance statement records that the works was staffed through the experiences of Puritans, English freemen, and Scots prisoners engaged for its operation, and that it initiated and sustained an advanced ironmaking technology in the New World.

Among the craftsmen was Joseph Jenks, a blacksmith and millwright who settled at Hammersmith in the mid-1640s and remained there until his death — a tenure of thirty-five years spanning the construction, operation, and demise of the ironworks. In 1646 the iron works permitted Jenks to establish a blacksmith shop on the tailrace of the blast furnace, and the manufacturing skills he brought from England spread through generations of his descendants. Roland Robbins, the archaeologist who excavated the site three centuries later, called Jenks the most exciting pioneer in America's industrial history he had ever met.

The works closed around 1670 — the park's own pages do not all give the same year, and the disagreement is printed in §6 below. After closure the site fell out of use and the plant disappeared below ground. When the National Register nomination was written, it recorded that before the reconstruction the slag pile was the only surface evidence remaining from the original ironworks: a long, grass-grown ridge of furnace waste along the river.

The ground was found again by archaeology. Excavations directed by Roland W. Robbins ran from 1948 to 1953. The slag pile gave the archaeologists their starting clue: because the pile runs in a long, straight line, following it back led them to the blast furnace, whose remnants they found very early in the excavation. Robbins' crews uncovered the furnace, its waterwheel and wheel pit, casting beds, and thousands of artifacts — the Roland W. Robbins Collection holds more than 4,000 artifacts recovered from the site of the 1646 ironworks.

On the strength of those excavations, the American Iron and Steel Institute reconstructed the primary elements of the original iron works. The Saugus Iron Works Restoration opened to the public in 1954 and was operated as a private museum by the First Iron Works Association. On April 5, 1968, the site was renamed Saugus Iron Works National Historic Site and became part of the National Park System. The National Register nomination describes the result honestly, in words this dossier adopts: a careful — though partly conjectural — reconstruction based on archaeologically excavated foundations and traces of the major portions of New England's earliest, most sophisticated, and integrated ironworking plant.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the works stood in Lynn, on the south bank of the Saugus River at the upper end of its estuary, where a fall in the river could be dammed and geared to waterwheels and vessels could reach a dock and turning basin. The plant was a connected machine: furnace, casting house, forge, and mill arranged along the water, with waste slag tipped into the river season after season until it built its own landmass. Joseph Jenks' shop stood on the tailrace below the furnace, using water that had already done work above it.

Now, the same ground lies inside the town of Saugus, a twelve-acre park (the park's Cultural Landscapes Inventory measures 12.58 acres) under Central Street, whose retaining wall still stands above the furnace area where Robbins' photographs show the 1949 excavation. The reconstructed furnace, forge, and rolling and slitting mill stand on or near the excavated positions of the originals. The river still runs past the dock and turning basin. The Iron Works House still stands on the bluff above the works. And the slag pile is still there — grass-covered, looking like a small natural ridge, extending into the river by the warehouse and dock. What has changed is everything built: the industrial buildings a visitor enters are 1950s reconstructions, the machinery in them is reproduction machinery that demonstrates the process, and the site is read today through a museum, ranger tours, and a half-mile nature trail rather than through smoke, noise, and a working dock.

## 4. What's still there

The park's Cultural Landscapes Inventory lists the industrial complex as five reconstructed stone and wood industrial buildings — a blast furnace, forge, rolling and slitting mill, wharf, and warehouse — together with the slag pile extending into the river. The park's Basic Information page describes the twelve-acre site as containing a museum, reconstructed industrial buildings, working waterwheels, a reproduction blacksmith shop, a seventeenth-century home, an herb garden, a half-mile nature trail, a picnic area, a visitor center, and a dock.

The blast furnace is the centerpiece, and it is a reconstruction: the National Register nomination lists it as Building No. 8, "the reconstructed furnace, where iron ore was separated into cast iron and slag," a stone furnace stack served by a charging bridge from the high ground, with the casting house under its broad roof. The forge and the rolling and slitting mill are likewise 1950s reconstructions on the excavated site, housing reproduction machinery — water-powered bellows, hammers, rolls, and slitters — that the park operates in demonstrations. The blacksmith shop is a reproduction standing in the tradition of Jenks' shop on the furnace tailrace; Jenks' own ground survives as archaeology, recorded in the park's inventory as the Jenks Area Foundations.

One building is original. The Iron Works House is the only structure at Saugus Iron Works that survives from the 1600s. Its date of construction is unknown — the park's place page says so plainly — and its earliest recorded occupant, Samuel Appleton Jr., bought the Saugus Iron Works in 1676, after it closed, and lived in the house from 1681 to 1688. The house's present appearance is owed to Wallace Nutting, the photographer who bought it in 1915 and restored it to what he thought it would have looked like in the 1600s; he sold the property in 1920. The house now holds the Broadhearth Visitor Center, and the museum building beside it originally served as Nutting's workshop. The museum displays artifacts found at the original iron works, including material from Robbins' excavations.

The slag pile is the other original survival, and the more important one. The park's Robbins volume calls it one of the only surviving, and largely unaltered, cultural resources from the original operation of the ironworks; the nomination records that it is composed of original materials, though presumably smaller today than it should be, since some material was removed for fill. It is not a building, not a reconstruction, and not ground open to visitors' feet: it is the works' own waste, still lying where the founders threw it.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

The site is small enough to take in one visit, and the park's own program times shape the order. Begin at the Broadhearth Visitor Center in the Iron Works House for orientation, then step into the museum to see artifacts recovered from the original works before walking the plant — the objects make the reconstructions legible. Take the 10:00 am waterwheel demonstration to see the power system turning.

From the museum, drop to the industrial site in process order, the order iron moved through Hammersmith. First the blast furnace: stand at the casting house, read the stone stack and the charging bridge, and understand that this is the 1954 reconstruction standing where Robbins excavated the original furnace in 1949. Then the forge, where pig iron became wrought-iron bars, and the rolling and slitting mill, where bars became rods for nails. Stop at the blacksmith shop on the tailrace — Jenks' ground — and then walk out to the turning basin and dock, where finished iron left by water. Across the basin, read the slag pile for what it is: original waste, the one unaltered survival, viewed from the path and not climbed.

The 11:00 am Iron Works House Tour takes the bluff and the one original building, with the Nutting restoration as part of its story. The 2:00 pm Industrial Site Tour, which includes the waterwheel demonstrations, is the park's own synthesis and works as a second pass for visitors who walked the ground self-guided in the morning. Close on the half-mile nature trail along the river, which returns the site to the water system that powered it.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The closing year of the works is not settled in the park's own publications. The Basic Information page states the works operated from 1646 to 1670. The Cultural Landscapes Inventory gives 1646 until ca. 1670. The History & Culture page says the works produced from 1646 to approximately 1670. The National Park Service's Teaching with Historic Places lesson for this site states that from 1646 until 1668, this was the site of the Saugus Iron Works. This dossier prints 1670 in §2 following the park's current visitor page, and records here that the Service's own lesson plan prints 1668; the disagreement is not resolved.

The National Register record carries its own discrepancy. The NPGallery asset record for NRHP 66000047 lists the district's significant years as 1648 and 1670 — not 1646 — while the park's narrative pages uniformly date the start of operations to 1646. No source fetched for this dossier explains the register's 1648.

Joseph Jenks' forge is dated two ways inside the National Park Service's own Robbins volume. The park's Places page states that in 1646 the iron works permitted Jenks to establish his blacksmith shop on the tailrace, and a figure caption in the volume calls it Joseph Jenks' 1646 blacksmith forge. But the 1898 Lynn Historical Society marker quoted in the same volume reports that Joseph Jenks built a forge here in 1647, and the 1930 Massachusetts Tercentenary marker repeated the 1647 date while adding that Jenks invented the modern type of scythe. The 1898 marker also credits Jenks with making, in 1652, the dies for the first silver money coined in New England. The Robbins volume presents these marker claims as claims — pronouncements that beg questions — not as findings, and this dossier does the same.

The Iron Works House carries no settled construction date. The park's place page states that its date of construction is unknown and that the company agent may have lived in it while the works operated. The Cultural Landscapes Inventory dates it to ca. 1689, built by a gentleman farmer — a date after the works closed and after Samuel Appleton Jr.'s recorded occupancy of 1681–1688. The History & Culture page calls it the site's 1680s Iron Works House. The same pages divide on Wallace Nutting's restoration: the Inventory and the place page put his purchase and restoration in 1915, while the History & Culture page states the house was restored by Nutting in 1916. All three versions stand in the park's own record.

Two lesser disagreements are printed rather than averaged. The park's visitor page describes a twelve-acre site; the Cultural Landscapes Inventory measures the park at 12.58 acres. And the "first" claim itself is worded differently across the Service's pages — first sustained, integrated iron works in British Colonial America (Basic Information), first successful, integrated iron works in the New World (History & Culture), first integrated iron works in America (Cultural Landscapes Inventory) — while the Service's own Robbins volume documents the earlier, unsuccessful attempts at Falling Creek and Braintree that those phrasings are careful to step around.

Finally, the archaeology itself contains a recorded disagreement: Robbins reasoned that a stone ramp must have led from the casting area to the slag pile, given the difference in elevation; the Reconstruction Committee did not necessarily agree. The route by which slag reached the pile is unresolved in the excavation record.

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** John Phelan's photograph of the reconstructed blast furnace at Saugus Iron Works, taken from across the river at the footbridge. In the foreground, the weathered wooden rails of the footbridge cross the lower frame, and a gravel path curves up a grass bank toward the furnace. The furnace itself is the 1950s reconstruction: a stone furnace stack with a wooden charging bridge running in from the high ground at the left, a broad weathered board roof sloping down over the casting house, and open bays beneath it carried on timber posts. Stone retaining walls, a wooden fence line, and mature trees stand behind the building under an overcast sky. The frame shows the reconstruction as it stands on the 1646 site; nothing in it is original fabric except the ground.
