# HT-RES-VT-old-constitution-house — The Republic Written in a Tavern

Division VT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

In Windsor, on the Connecticut River in the Upper Valley, stands a white clapboard tavern-house that the State of Vermont keeps as the birthplace of Vermont itself. It was Elijah West's tavern in July 1777, when delegates of the New Hampshire Grants — a people who had declared themselves independent of both Britain and New York and had no government yet but the one they were about to write — met in its rooms and adopted the first constitution of the "Free and Independent State of Vermont": the first constitution in America to prohibit adult slavery, the first to give the vote to men without property, and the first to establish a system of public schools. The republic that document founded governed itself for fourteen years before Vermont entered the Union in 1791 as the fourteenth state.

Town: Windsor, Vermont

## 1. Trip gate

The Old Constitution House is a Vermont State Historic Site, operated by the Vermont Division for Historic Preservation, at 16 North Main Street, Windsor, VT 05089, on Main Street (U.S. Route 5) toward the north end of Windsor village. It is a house museum: period rooms furnished to the building's years as an early tavern, and an interpretive area on the 1777 convention in the early-twentieth-century tea room.

The visit is seasonal in the state system's normal pattern — the state historic sites open in late spring and close in mid-October, and this house's published seasons have run late May to mid-October — but the house is not currently open on that pattern. The Division's own page states that the Old Constitution House is presently closed for renovations, with reopening planned for May 2027 and a commemoration on July 8, 2027 marking the 250th anniversary of the adoption of the Vermont Constitution. The Division's project plan for the house describes a soft opening in 2027 restricted to the first floor, and a full opening for 2028 resuming a May-to-October season, Friday to Sunday, with school groups by appointment. Current status, hours, and admission are as the Division publishes them at historicsites.vermont.gov.

The visitor boundary is the house and its lot at 16 North Main Street. The building does not stand where the convention met: West's tavern originally stood on Main Street in the center of the village and was moved twice, reaching this ground in 1914. The original site is elsewhere in the village, unmarked by the museum visit itself.

## 2. The story

The ground was disputed before it was Vermont. The land between the Connecticut River and Lake Champlain was claimed under grants issued by New Hampshire's royal governor and, after King George III ruled in 1764 that New York held jurisdiction, under New York patents as well — over the heads of settlers who had bought and cleared land under the New Hampshire Grants. The state's own history of the site records what followed: settlers faced competing claims and demands from both colonies, and Ethan Allen and the Green Mountain Boys formed a militia aligned primarily against New York. Out of that quarrel came a separate country. In January 1777 the Grants' delegates, meeting at Westminster, declared their territory a free and independent state — first under the name New Connecticut, soon renamed Vermont.

A state declared still needed a government. The National Register nomination records the sequence at Windsor: on June 4, 1777, a convention of 72 delegates met at Windsor, and on July 2 the delegates met again, this time in the tavern owned by Elijah West, to take up a constitution. The drafting ran through a week in which the war came close — Burgoyne's army was advancing from Canada, and British forces took Ticonderoga in those same days — and tradition holds that on July 8 a violent thunderstorm kept the wavering delegates in the tavern until the work was finished. That morning, July 8, 1777, in West's tavern, the convention adopted the first Constitution of Vermont. The storm story is printed here as the tradition it is; §6 takes it up.

What they adopted was, for 1777, radical. Its declaration of rights opened with the proposition that all men are born equally free and independent, with natural, inherent, and unalienable rights — and drew the consequence in the same article: no male person born in this country, or brought from over sea, was to be held by law as a servant, slave, or apprentice after the age of twenty-one, nor a female after eighteen, unless bound by their own consent. It was the first constitution in America to prohibit slavery — adult slavery, in the state's own careful wording; the clause's limits are stated plainly in §6. The frame of government gave the vote to freemen of twenty-one years, resident a year in the state and of quiet and peaceable behavior, with no requirement of property or income — universal manhood suffrage, the first established in America. And the constitution provided for a system of public schools, the first such establishment in America, in the state page's summary.

Under that constitution Vermont was a republic, and acted like one: it seated a government, and the nomination records that the first Legislature, chosen in March 1778, met at Windsor on the twelfth of that month — in the town where the constitution had been written. The United States, pressed by New York's claim, would not recognize it; Vermont governed itself anyway, unrecognized and independent, for fourteen years. In 1791 the republic ended by its own choice: Vermont was admitted to the Union as the fourteenth state, the first admitted after the original thirteen. The 1777 constitution, amended over the years, continued — in the state page's words — to provide the framework of Vermont's government.

The building went back to being a tavern. West's house had been built before 1777 — the state page dates its construction only as pre-1777, and the Division's current project plan states plainly that the exact construction date of the tavern is not known — and it stood originally on Main Street in the center of Windsor village, where it functioned as a tavern until 1848. Then the uses turned ordinary: rehabilitated for retail merchandising in 1848, moved about 1870 to a side street and converted into tenement housing, and serving as a warehouse in the last decade of the nineteenth century. The convention's room survived as a tenement and a warehouse because nobody had yet decided it was a shrine.

Preservation came in the new century. Efforts to save the former tavern began in 1901; the Old Constitution House Association was formed in 1911; the association received title to the building alone from the Fay family, and land for a new location was donated by the family of William M. Evarts. By 1914 sufficient funds had been raised, the building was moved to its present site at 16 Main Street, and restoration began under Lewis Sheldon Newton of Hartford, appointed architect for the project. To function as a museum and tea room the building was altered in the taste of its rescuers — the Division's project plan records fashionable Colonial Revival detailing, porches, and two rear ells added in that era. The Association maintained the house as a museum and public tea room until June 1961, when ownership of the house and its collections was granted to the State of Vermont. The property was entered in the National Register of Historic Places on March 11, 1971. It is now one of the Vermont State Historic Sites, kept by the Division for Historic Preservation.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the tavern stood in the center of Windsor village on Main Street, and it was a working public house, not a monument: a tap room where men gathered to drink, socialize, and argue the questions of the day — the state page repeats the old saying that more politics occurred in the tap room than at town meetings. Into that ordinary room, in July 1777, came the convention of a self-declared republic that existed, so far, mostly on paper, and the paper it produced there made the room's name.

Now, the same building stands a short move north of its first ground, at the north end of the village, kept as a state historic site. The change to read honestly is double. The building is genuinely the tavern of 1777 — the same house the delegates sat in — but it has been moved twice, used as a store, a tenement, and a warehouse, and restored and altered by its preservation-era owners, who added Colonial Revival porches and ells and a tea room that no eighteenth-century traveler ever saw. What a visitor walks through is the tavern as the early twentieth century chose to save it, furnished back toward its tavern years. The convention's Windsor is also still legible outside the fence: a Connecticut River village on its old Main Street, of which this house is the northern anchor.

## 4. What's still there

The house itself, as the National Register nomination describes it: a Colonial-style building, white clapboarded, two stories high with an ell, two end interior chimneys, a porch across the front, and the entrance at the center of the front — the description still matches the building a visitor sees, down to the green shutters and the hanging sign at the street. It stands on a concrete foundation on its 1914 lot at 16 North Main Street, the third ground it has occupied.

Inside, the state page describes period rooms that reflect the building's history as an early tavern, complete with the tap room where the political talk happened. The visitor materials for the site name the rooms: kitchen, dining room, parlor, bedchamber, and large chamber, shown with period furnishings and artifacts. The tap room holds the object the site's tradition centers on: the table believed to be the one around which the Windsor Convention delegates gathered. A large interpretive area in the early-twentieth-century tea room examines the events surrounding the 1777 adoption of the constitution — the exhibits on the convention itself — and the collection shown there includes the 1772 New York charter for the town of Windsor granted by King George III, a rare copy of the sermon delivered at the 1777 Windsor Convention, cannon ball and grapeshot from Mount Independence, and a Revolutionary War period cartridge box. The collection, the site's materials record, holds many pieces donated by area residents and by the local chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution.

The present state of the house is part of what is there. The Division's project plan records a building in a flood zone, flood impacts having affected the basement and foundation, basement windows, first-floor flooring, and the full-width front porch, and an environmental stabilization project under way against mildew and moisture, with historic window restoration — the work behind the current closure, and the reason the reopening is staged across 2027 and 2028.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

Come up Main Street from the village center and take the house from the street first: the white clapboard front, the full-length porch, and the hanging sign — "Old Constitution House 1777" — that marks the building before any label inside it. Read the front as the nomination describes it: two stories, center entrance, end chimneys, the tavern's public face to the road.

Begin inside at the tap room, the room the visit is for. Take it as a working room first — drink, talk, travelers, town business — and then as the room where the convention's work was done, with the table the site's tradition names as the delegates' own. Hold both readings; the site does.

Work the period rooms next, in the order of a tavern's day: kitchen and dining room, parlor, and the bedchambers above, including the large chamber — the house as lodging and livelihood, furnished to its early tavern years. Then finish in the tea room, which is honestly a room of the preservation era, and where the site puts its argument: the interpretive area on the events of July 1777, the 1772 Windsor charter, the convention sermon, and the Mount Independence shot. The tea room is where the house explains itself — and where its twentieth-century rescuers are present in the room's very existence.

Close the visit outside, with the two moves in mind: look back at the house on its 1914 ground, then walk down Main Street toward the village center, where the tavern first stood. The distance between the two sites is the preservation story, walked.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The building's construction date is not fixed by any primary source fetched. The National Register nomination calls it a Colonial-style building "built c. 1777." The state historic sites page states that the West tavern "was constructed pre-1777." The Division's project plan of 2026 states the position plainly: "The exact construction date of the tavern is not known." No year is asserted here beyond those three statements standing side by side; the house is certainly standing by July 1777, because the convention met in it.

How much of the 1777 fabric survives is likewise not certified. The Division's plan records that the building's status following a town fire, various uses, and two relocations is unclear, and that its museum-era conversion altered it with Colonial Revival detailing, porches, and two rear ells. The nomination, written in 1970, says only that over the years there had been slight alterations to interior and exterior and that both had been skillfully restored. This dossier therefore describes the house as the tavern building, moved and restored — never as untouched 1777 fabric.

The slavery clause deserves its exact limits, because the site's fame rests on it. The state page's own wording is that the constitution was the first in America "to prohibit adult slavery." The 1777 text prohibits holding a male as servant, slave, or apprentice after twenty-one and a female after eighteen, unless bound by their own consent: it ended legal adult slavery in Vermont while leaving minors bound and leaving consent bindings standing, and it was a constitutional clause, not an emancipation of people then held, who were freed by reaching the stated ages. Nothing broader is claimed here.

The thunderstorm of July 8, 1777 — the storm said to have kept the delegates in the tavern until the constitution was finished — is tradition. It appears in retellings of the convention, but no primary record fetched for this dossier (the nomination, the state site's pages, the Division's plan) tells it, and §2 prints it as tradition, not as record.

The address is styled three ways in the records: the nomination prints "16 No. Main Street," the Division's project plan prints "16 Main Street," and the site's current usage, followed here, is "16 North Main Street." The street address itself — number 16, Main Street, Windsor — is constant across all three.

Finally, no coordinates are printed in §10. The nomination-era record defines the property by its street address, and no primary source fetched for this dossier prints a point scoped to this property; the coordinates circulating on aggregator and map sites were not used.
