# Guymon — the agent who kept count
Research draft · Oklahoma OP04 · Dash review pending

## 2 · The story

### A town moved toward the railroad

The Oklahoma Historical Society places the planned town at Sanford Switch on the Rock Island route. It records an early post office called Sanford and its quick renaming in 1901 for Edward T. Guymon of the Inter-State Land and Town Company. It also names Richard B. Quinn among those who moved a business from Hardesty to the growing railroad town. His *Hardesty Herald* became the *Guymon Herald*. The older town lost much of its commercial activity. The railway shifted where goods and messages were handled as well as the path travelers took. [2–4]

A reader in Guymon in February 1905 could find the Rock Island schedule in the local newspaper. “Local Freight” appears in the printed timetable heading alongside passenger service. The advertisement is the railway’s published schedule, without independent evidence that every train ran on time. In the December 25, 1913 issue, a Rock Island notice prints “BISSELL, Agent.” Both primary sources were read as archived newspaper-page text. [5–6]

### Two hundred fifty-six loads in August

By September 1921, the *Herald* reported a count supplied by D. P. Bissell, the local Rock Island agent. Its headline reads “256 Cars of Wheat in August.” It is a contemporary report with an identified source, and it gives the work a scale without claiming a complete annual total. The article states the monthly figure for Guymon elevators. One shipment could depend on receiving from local growers, keeping grain handled and accounted for, finding cars, and releasing loaded cars to the railroad. That sequence is an interpretation of the freight system. A Bissell diary, station log or individual shipper’s receipt was not found. [7]

The same town later housed a county courthouse, with construction beginning in 1926 in the OHS account. County offices still use the address. The Main Street civic frontage offers a lawful place to read what the newspaper said about a town connected to the agricultural market through its rail agent. [1–2,7–8]

## 3 · Then / now

- **Historic location.** Guymon’s Rock Island station and elevator operations. The rail corridor remains a transportation corridor. Source precision for the depot footprint or which loading doors contributed to Bissell’s reported August total is insufficient for a visitor pin. [2–3,7]
- **Lawful physical anchor.** Public North Main sidewalk at the county courthouse block, 319 N Main. Use lawful street parking where posted and a short public walk. County operations offices and rail property are outside the research stop. [1–2]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Use the North Main sidewalk at the county courthouse. Read the short account of the Sanford post office and Quinn’s newspaper move. [1–4]
2. Read the old Rock Island notice with Bissell’s printed role. Take note of the different evidence offered by a railway advertisement and a news article. [5–7]
3. **Strongest story moment — The number the agent gave the paper.** At the public courthouse frontage, read the headline on the wheat cars. Picture all the separate grain deliveries behind the monthly number, then look along the public civic street where a new railroad town developed its own institutions. [1–2,7]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The OHS encyclopedia dates Rock Island laying track southwest from Liberal toward Texhoma to **1902**. The SHPO historic-context study puts the extension in **1901**. The 2001 town marker also gives **1901** for construction through the Panhandle. The exact local milestones for track laying, first train, completion, and timetable service remain unreconciled. [2–3,9]
- The July 1905 and other claims about arrival or railway employment need their own station records. The read sources verify Bissell in 1913 notice text and 1921 by attribution. Individual elevator workers’ names and first-person records were not retrieved. Newspaper text is rendered OCR; figures were cross-checked against the headline and surrounding text. [5–7]
- The city parks directory did not return usable specific text about Centennial Park. HMdb is a marker locator and does not establish present city access. The county public sidewalk is the only draft physical anchor. [1,9–10]
