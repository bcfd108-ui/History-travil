## 2. The story

**The daughter is the witness.** **Kate Roberts Pelissier**, daughter of **Margaret Barr Roberts**, wrote *Reminiscences of a Pioneer Mother*, published in *North Dakota History* **24(3), July 1957, pp.129–138**. The state education site reproduces pages **133–136**, directly retrieved and visually read for this dossier. This is retrospective household testimony, not a recording or a contemporary account book. [6][7][8][9]

**Her own work.** Kate recalls helping prepare food as a young child and later joining her sisters in gathering produce and doing sewing. The household combined its own food supply with sales and services to Medora. She also remembers an older hired helper teaching her arithmetic at the family table. Her concise description of Margaret is **“And she was not afraid of work.”** [7][8]

**Original interpretation.** A ranch household was more than a cattle owner's enterprise. It was a place where food, clothing, income, education and care had to be produced together. The kitchen table matters as much to this story as a saddle. The daughter’s memory makes the family active participants, not picturesque neighbors in a president’s biography.

**A separate institutional account.** Dickinson State University’s Theodore Roosevelt Center describes Margaret supporting five daughters after her husband disappeared in 1886, selling produce and domestic services and later running a Dickinson boarding house. It identifies her as a rancher and entrepreneur before describing her Roosevelt connection. This supports the broader context but is not Margaret’s firsthand voice. [10]

## 3. Then / now

**Do not exchange one household for another.** The public Maltese Cross Cabin is Roosevelt’s cabin, now at the visitor center. It is not the Roberts family home, nor evidence of the layout of their kitchen, garden or hired worker’s accommodation. The household narrative is a reading companion, not a reconstruction label. [11]

**The regional economy had many scales.** The Center’s account connects Margaret’s work to neighboring ranches and Medora establishments. Keeping that economic agency in view avoids making Roosevelt’s friendship the explanation for everything she accomplished. [10]

**A memory of hardship is not a hardship game.** The daughter’s affectionate retrospective framing should be preserved as her perspective. It does not establish that every household prospered, all workers had similar arrangements, or all community members experienced settlement in the same way.

## 4. What's still there

**Public interior:** South Unit Visitor Center, subject to current hours and official visitor restrictions. NPS describes its museum and film generally; no Roberts-related object or recording is verified. [2]

**Optional public historic building:** Maltese Cross Cabin, which NPS offers for daytime self-guided visits during visitor-center hours. Its presence is useful for a clearly labeled comparison only, never a substitute Roberts site. Entrance fees may apply even though the cabin activity has no separate activity charge. [11]

**Accessibility distinction:** NPS calls the visitor centers wheelchair accessible and provides captioned films. The cabin-specific page warns of a **25-inch doorway** that may not accommodate every assistive device, despite the general accessibility statement. An interior cabin visit is optional and not required to experience the documentary story. [12][11]

**Excluded:** Sloping Bottom Ranch, former household land, remote Elkhorn access, private ranches, riverside approaches, staff housing and work areas. No permission or route is inferred from historical geography.

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**Start with the daughter, before the president.** Read the short household account before arriving. It supplies the human thread that a broad park visit might otherwise lose.

**Orient in the public center.** Use the staffed interpretation venue during confirmed hours. Establish which material concerns Roosevelt and which concerns wider ranch life. This editorial distinction is not a claim about a specific installed exhibit.

**Keep the comparison honest.** If the cabin is open and accessible for the visitor, consider it only as another household building from the regional history. Do not photograph a feature and label it Margaret’s possession. No cabin visit is necessary if weather, closure or access makes it unsuitable.

**End at the table in the account.** The strongest story moment is ordinary learning sustained amid work and uncertainty. Finish by recognizing the family and helpers as makers of daily life. Do not follow historical place references to find the family’s original ground.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

**Extent examined:** three state-hosted scans, covering printed pages 133–136, not the entire 129–138 article. The state identifies the text as written to Ethel Roosevelt Derby. The precise date of composition and any editorial changes before 1957 publication were not established. [6]

**No new eyewitness claim:** Margaret’s own account is mentioned by the Center but was not retrieved. Kate’s account remains the primary household voice. The interpretation does not turn it into testimony by her mother or an unnamed hired worker. [10]

**Unresolved disappearance:** the Center describes Margaret’s belief that her husband had been murdered. Do not convert belief or later speculation into a proven finding. No investigative or grave-site stop follows. [10]

**Seasonal uncertainty:** the holiday component and incomplete dynamic page require a fresh check for Thanksgiving. Published accessibility is not an inspected snow-free entrance. No present Roberts exhibit, archival original availability or specific photo-reproduction permission is confirmed.

**Scope limit:** this family’s settler account is not an Indigenous history of the Badlands. No claims about Tribal experience, cultural property or access are made on its authority.
