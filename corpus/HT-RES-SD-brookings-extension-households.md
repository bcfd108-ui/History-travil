## 2. The story

### Household work enters the annual report

The July 1918 State College Extension circular links wartime food needs to emergency funding that reached counties without extension organization under state law. The repository’s text excerpt calls the women employed in this work “home demonstration agents.” The annual report speaks for the institution that funded and organized the work. What it calls an agent’s task lands in the household as instruction that somebody has to adapt, practice and share. The quoted phrase was read in SDSU’s published extract. The full linked scan returned HTTP 403. [2]

### From statewide instruction to a club notebook

The university’s finding aid names Mary Dolve as the specialist who led a late-1920s inquiry into homemakers’ food and nutrition needs. It reports questionnaires sent to 875 clubs and discussions at schools and meetings. It also names Mrs. Perry Clifford of Cresbard as chair at the September 1928 founding meeting and records a February 1929 organizational meeting with 19 delegates from 14 counties. This is an archive’s historical description. It locates a paper trail rather than providing the women’s unmediated voices. [3]

Brookings County’s Du Cum Extension Club appears in a later generation of that record. SDSU’s catalog lists a folder of 1966–70 programs, two parts of a scrapbook dated 1948–2000 and later secretary’s record books. A traveling lesson can vanish after the meeting. A program and a secretary’s book can show what someone chose to keep, even before the research resolves who wrote the minutes. This dossier has read the repository descriptions, not the original pages. [3–5]

## 3. Then / now

The college created and recorded a public program, while local clubs maintained their own records. SDSU’s museum now provides a public venue for interpreting agricultural history. The university archive is the separate repository for the club material. No currently installed Du Cum exhibit was verified. [1,3–5]
