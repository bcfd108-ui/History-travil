# Lakeport Plantation Museum
HT-RES-AR-lakeport-plantation-freed-workers · Arkansas OP02 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

A Chicot County bill of sale in 1832 records human beings in the same transaction as land. Arkansas State University’s Lakeport history reproduces a portion of the names and descriptions: “Bill a brick mason, Thomas a Carpenter, Louis a Blacksmith.” The words survive through the museum’s excerpt, with the wording and capitals as published there. The writer reduced people to property and still had to record expertise that a plantation depended on. The skills are specific. The surviving house entered the story decades later, and the excerpt supplies no evidence that any of those three worked on its construction. [3–5]

An 1860 letter quoted by the operator adds a different kind of evidence. Nancy Sherrard, a tutor in the Johnson household, describes dining service and the purchase of the unnamed man, his wife and a young child. Her account is a household observer’s testimony, from an excerpt in the museum history. It preserves the cost placed on that family but does not record their names or words. Reading it beside the bill of sale shows why the presence or absence of a name in a deed is so consequential. [3]

After emancipation, a plantation whose owners still held land had to find workers under a different legal order. The university describes Lycurgus Johnson using both cash wages and crop shares. Flood, crop failure, shortage of cash and complaints about broken contracts shaped the first years of bargaining. The Lake Village and Luna Landing Freedmen’s Bureau register survives as a named archival volume covering April 1867–August 1868. It has separate chronological contract entries and landholder remarks. Its catalogue establishes where to investigate those arrangements. A matched contract or signature for Bill, Thomas, Louis or the 1860 household remains missing, so their individual postwar lives stay open. [3, 6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The museum stands on the historical plantation grounds, but the landholding, the former steamboat landing, and the museum visitor compound are distinct. The former landing is documentary ground here, and the location of antebellum workers’ quarters is not established in the sources read. [3–5, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Drive up to the A-State visitor center at 601 AR-142. Use the designated parking and short signed path to the house under the posted guided or self-guided terms. End at the edge of the signed visitor area. Fields, tenant or home sites, the levee, old landing, riverbank and unverified burial ground are outside the trip. [1, 7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The original state nomination dates the house circa 1850, while the Mississippi River Parkway Commission lists 1859. The Encyclopedia of Arkansas places the beginning of construction around 1858–59. The National Register’s 1850 metadata is consistent with the early nomination’s date, while the museum and later interpretations use a narrower date. Do not assign an exact construction day or the names of builders. [3–5, 8, 9]
- A-State’s contact page lists Monday–Saturday. Its detailed visit page lists Monday–Friday for both kinds of tours, with dated holiday closures. The trip gate uses weekdays. A Saturday interior or exterior opening and access outside operating hours remain unconfirmed. [1, 2]
- The original 1832 deed folio and Nancy Sherrard letter were not retrieved; quotations are from A-State’s public extracts. The Smithsonian item was retrieved as a catalog record for volume 104, not a verified Lakeport page image. The full identity and after-emancipation history of each named person are open. [3, 6]
