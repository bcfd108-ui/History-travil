# Lower Mississippi River Museum and the crew of a working river

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March OP07 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

**Drive-up to the Washington Street museum entrance.** On November 20, 2026 the Corps listed free admission, Monday–Saturday 9 a.m.–4 p.m. and Sunday 1–4 p.m., with Thanksgiving and the following day, Christmas Eve and Day, and New Year’s Eve and Day closed. Start with the indoor museum. Deck access and outdoor model use follow posted operational and weather limits. Remain at the street entrance and managed museum grounds. No shore itinerary. [1–2]

## 2. Story

Gevotta Oliver was attending Cherokee Elementary School in Vicksburg when the Mississippi flooded in 1927. In her January 17, 1977 oral history with Daisy Greene, she recalled that “A lot of refugees from Greenville came to Vicksburg.” The Red Cross camp across from where she lived gave children and other people food. Her perspective is that of a child living on the Vicksburg side of a mass displacement. Her account places her near a camp in 1927. The present Washington Street museum is a later institution. Its own audio installation is described by the operator as a family flood account. It has yet to be matched to Oliver’s independently archived voice. [1,3]

Decades later a diesel towboat, M/V Mississippi IV, became a work platform on the same river. Corps records date its working span to 1961–93. It towed barges for the Memphis District and also carried people conducting Mississippi River Commission business. On a workboat, safe navigation depended on bridge and deck crews in communication. A barge tow, a lock passage and a public hearing are different uses of the same vessel. The Corps gallery contains examples of work aboard. The roster and role of each person in the retrieved gallery records remain unverified. [4–5]

Kavanaugh Breazeale’s contemporary Corps report records the August 24, 2012 opening. The project brought the retired vessel to a permanent downtown display and paired it with a model of the river between Greenville and Vicksburg. The exhibit is a way to see the choices behind flood engineering near the people who lived with their consequences. [3,6–7]

## 3. Then and now

Then, the Mississippi IV worked afloat; today it is a preserved vessel at a land-based federal museum. The museum’s opening account distinguishes the vessel from the flood model and describes the relationship of riverbanks, levees and flow. A scaled model illustrates selected relationships. It does not recreate a flood family’s individual path or identify where Oliver’s camp stood. [1,3–7]

## 5. Public progression

Use the Washington Street public entrance. Start with the river and flood galleries. Continue to the vessel viewing area through the museum’s approved circulation and use the outdoor model only if staff and conditions allow. **Strongest story moment — Vicksburg, a town receiving people.** Pair the map’s Greenville-to-Vicksburg span with Oliver’s memory of the camp across from her home. End with the towboat’s pilots and deck workers as the next generation working with the river. [1–7]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The 2012 advance report describes operation from the 1960s; the Corps photo caption gives a precise 1961 start. These can be reconciled as broad and specific dates. Earlier publicity describes onboard tours and the pilot-house simulator. Current posted museum hours alone do not guarantee particular compartments or programs. The operator’s individual image page and detailed Exhibits page returned HTTP 403 when opened through the web reader. The higher-level operator material and source metadata were readable. [1–2,4–7]

Oliver remembers returning to Greenville in 1929 in one line and gives 1929 or 1930 earlier in the same interview. Neither changes her direct recollection that she was in Vicksburg in 1927. The transcript cover and catalog passage display different oral-history numbering. The stable digital file is linked directly. Exact camp parcel, tape audio, towboat crew rosters and current vessel compartment access remain open. [3]
