# International Museum of Muslim Cultures and the people who made room for the written word

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March OP10 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

**Public gallery through the operator’s temporary entrance.** The International Museum of Muslim Cultures lists the Mississippi Arts Center, 201 East Pascagoula Street, Jackson. Its visit page says renovation blocks the main Pascagoula Street entrance and directs visitors to the Mississippi Museum of Art garden-space entrance. The museum pages agree on Tuesday–Thursday and Saturday 10 a.m.–5 p.m. Use Friday 10 a.m.–1 p.m. as the overlap and confirm Sunday directly. The homepage lists Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year’s Day closures. Indoor galleries and public garden-side access are subject to operator notices and weather controls. [1–3]

## 2. Story

A traveling exhibition about Spain prompted two Jackson residents to ask whose history the city was about to see. Okolo Rashid and Emad Al-Turk organized a companion exhibition about Muslim-ruled Iberia and its legacies. Museum records say a steering group took shape in December 2000 and that the exhibition opened in April 2001. In a 2017 interview, Rashid recalled visitors telling her that they had known little about Islam’s influence in Spain. She also described the decision to keep the exhibition going as a museum, following community, education and interfaith support. Mississippi Today dates that transition to May 2002. April 2001 and May 2002 describe separate institutional steps. The sources also use different venue descriptions for the launch, discussed below. [4–5]

The Timbuktu gallery rests on a partnership with the Mamma Haidara library. Its modern interpretive work depends on earlier labor: scholars writing in Arabic, copyists keeping copies available, and craftsmen preparing bindings and decoration. Those jobs made books usable over generations. The Jackson museum identifies the partnership and credits a team that included scholars John Hunwick, Sylviane Diouf and others. It describes models, manuscripts and media that place reading and writing within the economies that sustained them. The cited exhibit page explains the project but does not provide a current loan roster. [6]

A related, traceable manuscript is the work cataloged in English by the Library of Congress as *The Important Stars Among the Multitude of the Heavens*. The 1733 copy traces to the Mamma Haidara Commemorative Library. The named author is Nasir al-Din Abu al-Abbas Ahmad ibn al-Hajj al-Amin al-Tawathi al-Ghalawi. The catalog describes a diagram of the rotation of the heavens. This example connects manuscript labor to a specific teaching object, and the 2003 Library of Congress exhibition checklist independently identifies the copy. Its inclusion in a Washington exhibition does not document its presence in Jackson today. [7–9]

## 3. Then and now

The 2001 program was a companion exhibition in downtown Jackson. The Arts Center galleries are the current public museum venue. The physical display puts written material alongside models and new media, so readers should check each object label for status, loan credit and date. The Library of Congress example illustrates a related collection and remains a remote reference unless the museum confirms an item match. The original event venue and today’s approach should not be merged. [1–9]

Start at the museum’s posted garden-side entrance and ask the desk which exhibitions are open. In the Timbuktu gallery, start with how a book was produced and continue to an object or reproduction with a legible credit. **Story landing — A text someone had to copy by hand.** Follow the museum label from a manuscript’s named writer or copyist to the collection that preserved it. ## 6. Uncertainty and disputes

The homepage lists Friday 10 a.m.–1 p.m. and Sunday by appointment. The ticket page instead gives Tuesday–Sunday 10 a.m.–5 p.m. The gallery’s temporary entrance is confirmed in its dedicated visit page. Reopen the Pascagoula approach only with fresh operator evidence. The museum’s founding account places the launch alongside the Spanish exhibition at the Mississippi Arts Pavilion. Mississippi Today’s retrospective calls its own launch venue the Jackson Convention Complex. An original April 2001 opening program or venue agreement would settle whether the names refer to different spaces. Exact current exhibit holdings, present accessibility details on the temporary approach and current photo permissions remain open. [1–6]
