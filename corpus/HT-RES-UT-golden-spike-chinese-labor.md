## 2. The story
**Named descendant voice:** Connie Young Yu's self-authored **“History from Descendants: An Introduction to Oral History Interviews”**, published by Stanford University's Chinese Railroad Workers in North America Project in 2019, identifies her great-grandfather **Lee Wong Sang** as a Central Pacific worker who arrived from Taishan in 1866. Her testimony is descendant knowledge and research, not an 1860s worker diary. [4]

Her own concise statement is **“It is the oral histories of Chinese Americans that connect the dots”**. She explains how family knowledge can connect institutions, migration and railroad labor. Her account also describes her parents' participation in the 1969 centennial and the exclusion of Chinese Historical Society of America president Phil Choy from its official program. These are her published recollections and interpretation, kept attributed. [4]

**Original interpretive angle:** finishing a railroad and finishing its public history are different achievements. A ceremony can commemorate extraordinary work while leaving the workers unnamed. A descendant's account changes the visitor's task from admiring an achievement in the abstract to asking how particular people became visible in its story.

Do not stage an invented first-person speech for Lee Wong Sang. His descendant's voice is strong enough without pretending that an original interview with him survives. Nor should family history be dismissed because it is not a corporate payroll: the important question is what kind of claim each source can support, and what remains to be corroborated.

This entry is about labor, family transmission and commemoration. It does not provide construction methods, worksite access or a recreation of hazardous labor. Loss and discrimination are treated reflectively, without games, triumphalist reenactment or a search for burial locations.

## 3. Then / now
**Construction-era history and later memory are separate layers.** A nineteenth-century worker's career, a family's inherited account, a twentieth-century anniversary and a twenty-first-century publication are not interchangeable evidence.

**Present interpretation:** NPS maintains a public visitor center with exhibitions and a theater. That does not prove Yu's essay, her family's photograph or a specific original document is on display today. [1][2]

**Representation versus survival:** the park's operating steam locomotives are replicas according to NPS. Their interpretive presence does not make them original witnesses to the historic event, and their ability to move does not make tracks an ordinary visitor path. [5]

Original comparison prompt: *What can a reconstructed scene help you imagine, and what can only a named source help you know?* The answer should keep representation and evidence distinct rather than rejecting either.

6. **End at the strongest story moment:** a major engineering achievement becomes a fuller human history when the people who built it are no longer anonymous.
