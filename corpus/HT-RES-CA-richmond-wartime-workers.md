## 2. The story
**Directly read primary oral history:** Betty Reid Soskin interviewed by **Nadine Wilmot in2002**, Bancroft Library's Rosie the Riveter World War II American Homefront Oral History Project, issued2007. Selected printed pp62–65 describe her work in the Black union auxiliary, **Local A-36**. She remembers clerical work and friendships, distinguishes the office from shipbuilding, and reflects on the segregation she understood differently later. [4]

Her account places clerical employment against the domestic day work common among women in her mother's circle. This is her comparison of constrained opportunities, not a claim that care and housekeeping lacked skill or value. It also does not establish her own childcare arrangements, wage or a universal Black woman's wartime experience. [4]

**Original interpretive angle:** the familiar industrial worker image can obscure office labor and the domestic/care economy around it. A fuller account asks both who obtained a job and what boundaries remained after employment began. Work could offer advancement while still taking place within segregation.

**One-minute hook, original:** Not every woman on the home front held a riveting tool. One worked with records in a segregated union office, and later helped the public recognize why her story did not fit the familiar picture.

NPS independently identifies Soskin's wartime file-clerk role and later public interpretation work. Do not call her a shipyard riveter or promise an appearance: NPS records her death on **December21,2025**. [5]

## 3. Then / now
Then, wartime workers experienced the same city from unequal positions. Now, the visitor center provides an institutional setting for comparing experiences without pretending one triumphant image includes everyone.

**Original distinction:** testimony can preserve both a remembered feeling and a later judgment about that feeling. Keeping those together is more honest than rewriting a young worker as already possessing her later analysis. It is also more useful than treating pride in a job as proof that discrimination had disappeared.

This visit is not a reconstruction of Soskin's actual office. The center's historic building and her workplace are not interchangeable, even though both contribute to home-front interpretation. [2,5]
