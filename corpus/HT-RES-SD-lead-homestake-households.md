## 2. The story

**The household is the unit of history, not the tonnage.** In Erin Lorraine Broberg’s March 21, 2022 SURF profile, Martin and Eileen Brosnahan describe lives spanning Homestake and its research successor. Martin worked in several manual and technical roles; Eileen moved from clerical assignments into personnel. They raised two sons in Lead. During layoffs, her work involved other workers’ benefits and departures. Her remembered summary is the short reading voice for this stop:

> “There were hundreds of jobs lost, and I heard each person’s story.” [4]

The article says Martin lost his job in 2004. In September 2007 Eileen processed her own departure from Homestake on a Friday and entered SDSTA employment the following Monday. The piece anticipated their April 2022 retirement. These are published interview recollections mediated by an institutional writer, not payroll records independently audited here. No audio was heard. [4]

A second first-person thread broadens the household beyond the miner underground. In SURF’s edited Q&A, Steven T. Mitchell recalls a maternal great-grandfather arriving from Finland in 1889 and relatives working for Homestake across generations. His maternal grandmother spent 35 years in the Employment Office. Mitchell’s family account gives clerical labor a place beside mining labor; its reported service lengths remain his testimony, not independently reconciled personnel files. [5]

Interpretive synthesis: employment could support a household and organize its sense of belonging, while closure could destabilize both. Neither family stands for every worker, immigrant community or Indigenous relationship to the Black Hills. These employer-published accounts are valuable firsthand voices, but not an independent community-wide verdict on the company.

## 3. Then / now

Then: the narratives center wages, work identity, office labor and whether a family could remain in its home community. Now: a public institution interprets a mining-to-science transition. That comparison is a reading framework, not proof that laboratory employment replaced every lost job or repaired every loss. [1,4,5]

The contemporary visitor center is an interpretive anchor, **not a preserved Brosnahan household or a documented location of their offices**. No household address is supplied. No specific historic street view has been aligned with a current photograph, and no camera position is inferred from a town map.

The open-cut view advertised by the visitor operator is not permission to approach the excavation. This dossier stays with indoor public interpretation; a view, if available, does not become a mine route. [2]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

**One public institution, not a townwide industrial or house walk.** This is a proposed interpretive sequence, not a claim that particular exhibits currently stand in a known order.

1. Before travel, recheck the dedicated visitor page and dated holiday information. If access remains uncertain, complete the digital reading instead.
2. At an open public visitor center, establish the distinction between the interpretation building and the working research site. Use only the admitted public exhibit area.
3. Read the grandmother’s employment-office detail alongside whatever visitor interpretation is actually present. Notice how a mine depended on work outside extraction as well as inside it. Do not invent an object to illustrate the point.
4. Finish with the household reading in section 2. **Strongest story moment:** the transition from processing other people’s departures to processing one’s own. This is a proposed reflective ending drawn from the account, not a reenactment or an identified physical desk. [4,5]

Optional discussion prompt, not a quiz or required answer: What work becomes invisible when a community’s history is told only through its largest machines? No employee interview, personal-history solicitation or contact was initiated.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The visitor center’s dedicated pages say Monday–Saturday, while SURF’s general homepage uses “open daily.” Prefer the specific visitor schedule; do not promise Sunday access. [1,2,6]
- Museum homepage dates are current to the 2026–27 off-season; its rates page still mixes a May 2026 opening with 2024–25 winter language. This is a publication mismatch, not evidence of a winter reopening. [3,7]
- Mitchell’s article introduction says mine closure in 2001; other accounts follow employment and transition activities later. A production endpoint, a layoff date, company wind-down and laboratory appointment are not the same event. This dossier does not force them into one closure date. [4,5]
- The books discussed in the Q&A were not read in full. No photograph permissions or personal records were obtained.
- These published sources are retrospective and institutionally selected. A complete account still needs additional worker, household and specifically Tribal-published perspectives. No generalized “Native voice” is supplied by substituting company interpretation.
- Road conditions, temporary closures, holiday exceptions and current exhibit contents remain unverified. No operational directions or underground procedures belong in this entry.
