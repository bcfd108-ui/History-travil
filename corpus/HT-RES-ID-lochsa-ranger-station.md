## 2. The story
This is a supply and communications story, not simply a group of picturesque cabins. The station supported remote fire lookouts and crews through pack trails and telephone connections. A visitor can ask: before a road reached the station, how did a district keep people fed, equipped and connected? [1]

The original federal nomination, prepared by district ranger **Jon B. Bledsoe**, describes the Combination Building as separately constructed rooms eventually joined under one roof. It attributes that sequence partly to a **$1,500 limit on individual projects**. Kitchen, office, toolroom and commissary embodied different pieces of the job. The record also documents **Job Corps** foundation reconstruction in 1968; later restoration should not be mistaken for untouched original fabric. [5]

**Louis F. Hartig**, a former district ranger, provides the strongest first-hand human account in *Lochsa: The Story of a Ranger District and Its People in Clearwater National Forest* (1989), edited by Shirley Moore. On printed p. 98 he recalled: **“my wife and I moved the last of our household goods”** to Kooskia in June 1957. He names **Kelsey Smith**, who moved into the Lochsa dwelling, and describes continuing work-center use after headquarters moved. This is retrospective personal testimony, not a contemporaneous moving inventory. His account also credits **Earnest Hartman** with the first root cellar in 1929. [6]

For a second named worker, the Forest Service brochure identifies **packer Charley Smith** in a 1924 supply photograph. It also reproduces whipsawing at the station in 1928. These are useful evidence leads for physical labor, not proof every log building was a CCC project. [4]

The landscape's history did not start with the Forest Service. The Nez Perce Tribe's **Spring 2025 General Council report**, p. 14, describes its Cultural Resource Program's ethnographic work on the Lochsa Corridor and continuing protection of culturally significant places. This published Tribal voice gives present-tense cultural context without borrowing private oral knowledge. [7] No sacred location, gathering area or redacted cultural-site coordinate is a hunt destination.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** Pack stock, hand tools, food storage and message transmission made a dispersed administrative system work. Forest Service regional history places the station among a network of support structures, not isolated architecture. It describes a root cellar, tack and tool spaces, and supplies moved by animals before road access changed operations. [8]

**Now:** A seasonal interpretive site rather than a public invitation into every work area. The 2016 brochure says wilderness crews sometimes worked from the site and stock occasionally used the corral. That dated statement explains why an interpretive complex and working spaces can coexist; it does not establish current occupancy or public entry to the corral. [4]

A productive comparison is between the visible room layout and the invisible distribution network it served. The proposal is to read a kitchen or supply space as infrastructure, not to romanticize difficult work or describe the setting as historically empty wilderness.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Current season vs boilerplate:** The 2026 opening dates differ from older Labor Day descriptions and stale 2024 fields. Preserve the discrepancy; do not extend the 2026 season. [1,4]
- **Headquarters dates:** The nomination uses 1922–1957; the current operator narrative uses 1925 to late 1950s; regional history describes establishment/reassembly in 1926 and headquarters move in 1956. Hartig recalls the household move in June 1957 and later staff relocation in 1958. These may concern different administrative/building milestones. Do not flatten them into one unquestioned start/end date. [1,5–6,8]
- **Dedication vs listing:** July 3, 1976 dedication is not the National Register date. NPS catalog lists June 9, 1978. A heritage-grant page conflates the two; retain the federal record distinction. [5,10–11]
- **CCC attribution:** A university CCC catalog entry gives year 1930, which alone cannot identify builders or prove a CCC-built station. Use the specific labor records instead. [12]
- No current interior inventory, accessibility audit, winter external-ground permission or photo station verified. Historic furniture does not mean every item is original.
- The federal catalog labels address restricted. Use only independently published operator visitor directions, not suppressed/reconstructed cultural coordinates. [1,10]
