# Brownville’s Missouri River crossings

Nebraska OP04 · Research draft for Dash · November 19, 2026

## 2. The story

### A town sold as a place to cross

Richard Brown arrived in 1854 and the town was platted in 1855. The nomination describes what happened next in terms of work: moving people and freight from the river onto an overland road, milling and serving other settlements. The bridge you may have crossed to get here belongs to a later transportation system. Early Brownville’s fortunes depended on people and cargo getting between the water and town. [3–5]

In his 1859 sketch, editor Robert W. Furnas called his neighbors “about one thousand moral, industrious and enterprising inhabitants.” He had arrived in 1856 and published the *Nebraska Advertiser*. The historical society excerpt preserves his language and his sales pitch. He counted labor, cultivation, schooling and corn as reasons to come. The district nomination gives a federal census figure of 425 in 1860. For a traveler reading both, the gap is a reminder to ask what an editor meant by town and whom a census enumerator could count. The boundaries and methods in the sources do not reconcile the figures. [3,4]

### Getting a wagon onto a boat

Marion Marsh Brown’s 1974 study for the state historical society reproduces a Brownville Steam Ferry advertisement from John Codington & Co. Dated November 11, 1857 in the clipping, it promises the “BEST CROSSING ON THE MISSOURI RIVER.” The firm was selling a California-bound wagon party a route to Fort Kearny as well as passage across the water. Brown traces the transition from the flatboat to the steam ferry *Nemaha* and reports scheduled half-hour service from its replacement, the *Idona*, in 1866. Steam made possible a promise the early crossing could hardly make. It took usable landings, a crew and enough passengers and freight to keep the boat working. [5]

### A boat’s working life was short

Brown’s account of the *Mary J. Arnold* follows a ferry that struck a snag near its wharf, made shore under its own power and unloaded teams and passengers before it sank. On July 18, 1880, the replacement *Belle of Brownville* arrived with a band and a crowd. In the surviving photograph, people, horses and wagons occupy the same working platform. The engines, landings, labor and credit that kept a crossing open belonged to the same system. The district nomination links Brownville’s broader decline to several pressures, including declining steam traffic, weak railroad connections, debt and loss of the county seat. [3,5]

## 3. Then / now

The village and Missouri River still share the setting documented by the district nomination. Main Street’s surviving buildings offer a public way to read the settlement. The working wharf and successive ferry landings have yet to be located with parcel-level evidence. The Society’s railroad depot interprets a different phase of access and commerce. [1–3,5]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- Furnas’s 1859 estimate was about 1,000. The 1860 census count reported in the nomination was 425. The two dates and unverified enumeration boundaries prevent a firm reconciliation. [3,4]
- The November 11, 1857 date appears on the advertisement reproduced by Marion Marsh Brown. Its issue masthead was not independently verified. An April 26, 1860 issue surfaced in Library of Congress search, but opening the issue record returned HTTP 403. No text from that issue was quoted. [5,6]
- The historical district register number identifies the district. No verified map ties a given boat and year to a specific modern landing coordinate, or establishes a public observation site at the water. Society contact and depot pages provide no verified November exhibit hours in this retrieval. [1–3,5]
- The quoted Furnas wording is the Society’s excerpt from his publication. The full 1859 original was not retrieved. [4]

## 7. Historical photo record

The Nebraska State Historical Society’s digitized Brownville study reproduces a period photograph captioned as the *Belle of Brownville*, which served in the 1880s. The craft and its load are identifiable in the published plate. A precise exposure date, repository item number, shoreline landmark match and lawful present camera station remain unestablished. No Photo Point. [5] The HABS catalog additionally identifies Charles W. Steinbaugh’s April 6, 1934 northwest view of Furnas’s house, survey NE-35-17. The search record was available and the direct Library of Congress item request returned HTTP 403. Camera placement and a lawful present repeat view could not be checked. [10,11]
