# B.B. King’s Indianola, from the cotton economy to a public museum

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP18*

## 2. Story

In a 1980 public history interview recorded at Indianola’s Gentry High School Library, Riley B. King let his father’s version of his birthplace stand. He then told interviewer John Jones, “I’ve always claimed Indianola my home.” The words are from the Mississippi Department of Archives and History’s published transcript. The distinction between the places he was born, grew up, and publicly called home was his own. The museum’s account situates his early life among sharecropping and tractor work. The preserved district’s records show how cotton-related industry influenced where people worked and lived in a segregated town. [1–3]

In the interview King describes moving between the sounds and audiences of churches and street work. That movement did not require giving up the influences he learned in the Delta. He names Bukka White and Muddy Waters, among others, when Jones asks whose music mattered to him. Christine Wilson’s MDAH account tracks his early trips to Memphis, a return to tractor work to earn money, and the later radio work at WDIA. That trajectory helps explain why the Indianola museum puts rural labor, local performance and broadcasting in the same story while using exhibits to interpret places beyond its walls. [1][2][4]

The museum’s historic brick building keeps part of the town’s workscape in view. The original Indianola Historic District nomination calls the retained building a cotton warehouse at 400 Second Street with a 2008 museum phase. The architectural account by Baughn, Fazio and Miller calls it the Gilmore Ginnery and associates it with King’s work before fame. Cotton storage and ginning are separate functions. The building can reliably be read as surviving cotton-industry fabric in the museum complex while the precise building function and work assignment need stronger original documentation. MDAH’s account dates the museum opening to September 2008 and names Mike Moore as a steering committee cochairman in its groundbreaking material. [3–5]

## 3. Then and now

The museum site combines early cotton-industry fabric with a modern museum addition. The original district nomination identifies a circa-1925 historic component and 2008 work. The separate architectural history gives circa 1920. Operator galleries cover the Delta childhood, radio and later performing life. An early-life farm scene or a replica radio studio interprets an event elsewhere. [1][3–5]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

Enter the staffed museum and start with the early-life and Delta work exhibits. Then read the broadcast interpretation if open. **Strongest story moment — What the name Indianola meant.** Bring King’s 1980 sentence about home to the gallery. The Delta story and the radio story become different stages of a working life, held together by the place King chose to name. [1][2][4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- King said his father remembered a birth near Itta Bena. King describes Indianola as home. MDAH’s historical article also places the birth near Itta Bena. No exact birth location is added to the locator. [2][4]
- The original district nomination classifies the retained circa-1925 component as a warehouse. The architectural source calls it a circa-1920 cotton gin and associates King with work there. The parent business, actual building functions by date and King’s exact job at this building remain open pending employment and parcel records. [3][5]
- PBS’s archival program page on the WDIA start returned HTTP 403. The state-published oral history and MDAH account supply the evidence used here. [2][4][6]
