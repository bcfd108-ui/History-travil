## 1. Trip gate
The current Idaho Parks and Recreation bulletin lists the Oregon Trail History Center **Tuesday–Saturday, 9 a.m.–4 p.m., closed holidays**. Wagon Wheel campground is listed open all year; Cottonwood and Sage cabins close November–mid-March. Cabins require advance reservations. Published motor-vehicle entrance fee is $7; general park rules say closure at sunset. Address: **1083 S. Three Island Park Drive, Glenns Ferry**. Use Mountain Time. [1]

This is published winter operating information, not proof a particular icy path, restroom or campsite is usable today. No booking or direct contact occurred. This dossier offers **land-based observation only: no wading, swimming, island crossing, launch or private-road approach**. A historic ford is not a contemporary safe route.

## 2. The story
The essential story is a decision with no easy answer. Emigrant parties could attempt the Snake River crossing to gain the northern route, or remain on the south side. BLM's trail atlas distinguishes the northbound route toward the Boise River from the South Alternate, also called the Dry Route; these later rejoined west of Fort Boise. Do not confuse those historic alternatives with today's driving directions. [2]

The 1968 Idaho State Historical Society reference identifies an older Indigenous and fur-trading route and explains that the emigrant ford used **two of the three islands**. Islands and gravel bars were aids to a difficult passage, not stepping stones guaranteeing safety. The Society also distinguishes later ferry traffic from the wagon tracks leading to the ford. [3]

**Eli Casey Cooley**, in the August 9, **1845** diary transcription prepared by Michael F. and Mary Lou Cooley for the Officer-Cooley Family Association, describes water that **“is quite deep and runs verry swift.”** He records a wagon saved but some of its load lost; his party finished moving wagons and cattle the next morning. This makes the crossing a prolonged collective operation rather than a single dramatic splash. Original manuscript pages were not inspected: the evidence is an attributed family transcription, with editorial notes distinguishable from diary text. [4]

This river was not merely an obstacle awaiting emigrants. The Shoshone-Paiute Tribes' own Cultural Department describes ancestral territories across Idaho, Nevada and Oregon, the taking of land and resources, and continuing Tribal life. That published voice supplies regional context without pretending every modern Tribal band or reservation boundary maps neatly onto one ford. [5] The archaeological research record at Three Island includes earlier occupation, fish remains and domestic material, further cautioning against starting the site's history in 1845. Precise excavation locations are intentionally not turned into visitor finds. [6]

**Ford and ferry are different.** A ford uses the riverbed and channels; a ferry carries travelers on a vessel. The park account dates Gus Glenn's ferry to **1869**, about two miles upstream, and says some people continued fording to avoid paying. This is a later service at a different location, not evidence that Cooley used a ferry here in 1845. [1]

## 3. Then / now
Then, animals, vehicles, goods and people had to negotiate moving water. Now, interpretation can be undertaken without entering it. The present park, a displayed wagon and a public overlook are not interchangeable with the exact historic channel, a survivor from a particular wagon train or the ferry landing.

For material perspective, Idaho Heritage Trust documents its **1993** assistance repairing two wagons used for interpretation. That establishes stewardship of interpretive objects, not that these wagons crossed here during the migration. [7] A photograph of a historical reenactment similarly must not be captioned as nineteenth-century documentary evidence.

The ISHS reference reports wagon ruts south of the crossing. This dossier does **not** supply an unverified southern road or private-land route to them. View distant features only where a signed public facility permits it; do not follow presumed ruts across fences. [3]

A separate **Three Island Crossing Wildlife Habitat Area** is listed by Idaho Fish and Game as watercraft access, with no camping or campfires. It is not a pedestrian shortcut from the state park, and no watercraft visit is proposed. [9]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Proposed interpretive order, not a surveyed or timed walk:
1. Start at the History Center during published hours. Establish whose homeland this is and distinguish the ford, ferry and alternate trail on interpretation.
2. Examine a wagon exhibit only from the permitted viewing area. Ask what belongs to interpretation and what has documented provenance.
3. End at a signed, open public river-viewing place within the park. Read Cooley's short description and consider the load lost even though the wagon survived. **Strongest story moment: a successful crossing did not mean arriving unchanged.** [4]

If weather or a closure blocks a river viewpoint, finish indoors with the river map. Do not substitute river entry, island access, a shoulder stop or a private lane to complete the story. No exact present viewing station is verified here.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- The name says three islands; the 1968 reference says the ford used two, and Cooley's account mentions two. These are not necessarily contradictory. Do not infer a fixed modern island count or a safe line through them. [3,4]
- Park history dates Glenn's ferry to 1869; ISHS describes it more broadly as following Boise Basin settlement after 1862. Retain the operator's 1869 attribution but flag the original ferry license/business record as unexamined. No earlier exact date is guessed. [1,3]
- Cooley's contemporary observation and the transcribers' modern site-identification commentary must remain separate. This pass has not georeferenced his route. [4]
- No independently checked present water depth, ford conditions, south-bank access, ferry remnant, original diary scan or nineteenth-century site photograph. No consultation/endorsement or named Indigenous guide is invented.
- Historical park brochures may describe discontinued rides or animals. They are not current offerings. Current bulletin controls the trip gate.
