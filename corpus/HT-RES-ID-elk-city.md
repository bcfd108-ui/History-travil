## 2. The story
Elk City's story is a settlement repeatedly dependent on access: prospectors could arrive before the supply system that would keep them there. ISHS marker 168 places discovery by twenty-two prospectors from Pierce in May 1861 and describes brush shelters followed by permanent log construction that August. Its account also says many miners moved on that fall. The rush and the durable community were not the same thing. [2]

Mining did not enter empty land. The **Nez Perce Tribe's own published history** explains that gold discovery brought trespass and theft within the reservation, followed by the imposed 1863 reduction. It also describes retained off-reservation rights and continuing sovereignty. This is necessary regional context; it is not an invented account of a particular Elk City encounter. [6]

An individual document makes the supply problem concrete. ISHS Reference Series 143 reproduces an **Idaho County Free Press notice of May 12, 1893, page 4, column 1**, naming **Ezra Baird and Felix Warren** in a proposed stage-and-saddle service. The plan combined a stage journey with horseback travel, extending the stage only when a wagon road was completed. Its telling qualification was **“as soon as the trail is passable for horses.”** This is a newspaper passage preserved in an official transcription; the original newspaper page was not inspected. [7]

The same historical source identifies Frank Coston as the line's operator and describes winter mail carried on snowshoes. Do not turn that labor into a romantic suggestion that a present visitor should push through a closed route. A service depended on seasonal conditions and people willing to carry it, not merely a line drawn on a map. [7]

The people were more than a generic crowd of miners. A 1908 archival photograph names **Charlie Wong Gee**, alongside Billie Allen, Little Gus Gustafson, Henry Holzman, Martin Hickman and Fred Franklin, outside the Old Blue Front Saloon. The caption does not establish their occupations or personal histories; names must not become invented biographies. [8]

## 3. Then / now
The old supply problem has modern echoes, but not an unchanged road system. ITD's SH-14 mill-and-inlay project page is now marked complete. That says something about a pavement project, not that winter ice, slides or closures are impossible. [4]

A 1904 panorama offers a town-scale historical reference. It cannot establish which current structures survive or where a visitor should stand. [9] The Red River platform offers a different comparison: contemporary stewardship of a working landscape rather than a fabricated view of an 1861 camp.

## 4. What's still there
- **Red River public viewing platform:** operator-confirmed viewing facility. IDFG describes restoration focused on spring Chinook spawning habitat, with federal, state, Tribal, nonprofit and private participation. Do not claim that this platform interprets mining or that all environmental changes were caused by mining. [1]
- **Elk City marker:** documented in the official marker guide; current placement and safe access remain unverified. No shoulder parking instruction. [2]
- **Town buildings:** no particular historic building's present fabric or public interior access was confirmed. The 1908 photo catalog says its saloon burned in 1924; that building must not be sold as a surviving attraction. [8]
- **Mines and mills:** excluded. A nomination, historical machinery description or mapped claim is not an invitation to enter.
