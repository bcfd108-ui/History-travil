# HT-RES-OH-marietta-campus-martius — A whole pioneer house, kept indoors, on the ground where Ohio began
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

At the corner of Second and Washington Streets in Marietta stands a brick museum built around a wooden house. The house is the last surviving dwelling of Campus Martius, the Ohio Company's fortified settlement of 1788 — the first permanent American settlement in the Northwest Territory — and it still stands on its original foundation, indoors. Beside the museum stands the little log Ohio Company Land Office, where the Company's purchase was surveyed and sold. This entry's ground is that Campus Martius site and its museum.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is the Campus Martius Museum in Marietta, Washington County, Ohio. What stands there now, stated plainly: the Campus Martius Museum building encloses the Rufus Putnam House — the only surviving dwelling of the Campus Martius fortification, on its original location, per the Ohio History Connection — and the Ohio Company Land Office stands on the museum grounds beside it, moved there in 1953 for its preservation, per the Northwest Territory Museum Society (the local manager). The balance of the 1788–1791 stockade does not survive; the museum building, raised by the State of Ohio in 1928 per the Ohio History Connection (but see §6), marks and shelters the site.

The site is owned by the Ohio History Connection and managed locally by the Northwest Territory Museum Society, per the Ohio History Connection's site page. The terms below are printed only as the operator-side sources state them. The Ohio History Connection's page states: hours Monday–Saturday 9:30 a.m.–5 p.m. and Sunday noon–5 p.m.; admission Adult $10.00, Children and Students $5.00, Veterans $5.00, Children under 6 free, Ohio History Connection and Friends of the Museums members free; average visit time, allow 1+ hours. The local manager's plan-your-visit page (Marietta Museums) states the same hours and adds the closures — New Year's Day, Easter, Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve, and Christmas Day — and phrases the rates as: Adult $10, Students K to College (with ID) $5, Children 5 and under (excludes groups) free, Active Duty Military & Veterans (with ID) $5.00, members free. The address as both operator sources carry it is 601 2nd Street, Marietta, OH. Where the two operator phrasings differ, §6 prints the difference.

## 2. The story
The legal frame came first. On **July 13, 1787**, Congress adopted the Ordinance of 1787, setting up a government for the territory northwest of the Ohio River; two weeks later, on **July 27, 1787**, Congress authorized the sale to the Ohio Company of Associates of a great tract in that territory — about a million and a half acres for the Company itself, per the Ohio History Journal's account of the transaction. The Company was a venture of New England Revolutionary War officers and soldiers, and Rufus Putnam — a general in that war and a surveyor — was its superintendent on the ground.

The first party landed on **April 7, 1788**. The National Register nomination for the Ohio Company Land Office, filed with the National Archives, states it directly: on that day 47 New England men landed at the mouth of the Muskingum River, members and employees of the Ohio Company. They laid out a town and named it Marietta, for Marie Antoinette of France — a gesture toward France's help in the Revolution — and the nomination calls it the first permanent settlement and seat of government established in the Northwest Territory after the Ordinance of 1787. (The party's count is disputed — 47 against the usually stated 48 — and §6 prints that dispute unresolved.) Across the Muskingum already stood Fort Harmar, a federal post built in 1785; the new town rose on the east bank, on a high bluff above the river.

Government arrived by water. Arthur St. Clair, chosen governor of the territory on **October 5, 1787**, reached Fort Harmar on **July 9, 1788**, and on **July 15, 1788** was formally received at Marietta, where Putnam answered his address on the colony's behalf.

Defense came next, and it gave this entry its name. On the bluff the settlers built Campus Martius — Latin for "Field of Mars," after the training ground of Rome's legions — a civilian fortification raised in stages between 1788 and **1791**, per the Ohio History Connection: a regular square of two-story dwelling houses with a blockhouse at each corner, sheltering the Company's families through the Northwest Indian War. The settlement's working office rose alongside: the Ohio Company Land Office, a one-story hewn-log building measuring 27 by 19 feet with a single fireplace, per its National Register nomination, which judges it among the first buildings erected by the Company, in the summer and fall of 1788. There Putnam, as superintendent, directed the surveying and dividing of the purchase.

Putnam's house stood in the stockade's wall, next to the southeast blockhouse. Its construction history is genuinely tangled, and §6 prints the tangle rather than a single clean date: the Register record gives 1788 as its significant year; Summers, in 1903, wrote that the building commonly called the Old Block-House was erected about the close of the Indian War, doubted it was ever itself a blockhouse, and noted that part of its timber had been part of the original blockhouse. What is not disputed is the occupancy: Putnam made it his home, and he and his wife Persis remained in the house until their deaths, per the local museum's history page — his in **1824**, per Summers.

The fortification outlived its war by very little. With the signing of the Treaty of Greenville in **1795**, the National Park Service's place record states, the rest of the stockade was torn down — the Putnam House, alone, was not. The Land Office's afterlife: after the Company sold its lots, the building was Putnam's own by **1796**, per its nomination; in **1953** the Colonial Dames of America presented it to the State of Ohio, and it was moved to the museum grounds and repaired and furnished by the Ohio Historical Society.

Preservation of the house was a local campaign before it was a state one. The Marietta chapter of the Daughters of the American Revolution leased the house in **1905** for meeting rooms, per Daniel R. Porter's account in the Ohio History Journal; in **1917** the Ohio General Assembly authorized the state to acquire the Campus Martius site and placed it with the state historical society. The state then built the Campus Martius Museum around the house, on the site, in 1928 per the Ohio History Connection (the Land Office nomination says the museum building was erected in 1930; §6 prints both). The house was restored again by the Ohio Historical Society between **1966** and **1972**, per the local museum's history page. Both the Rufus Putnam House and the Ohio Company Land Office were listed on the National Register of Historic Places on **November 10, 1970**, per their National Park Service Register records.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a regular square stockade on a high bluff above the Muskingum — two-story dwelling houses forming the walls, a blockhouse at each corner, built in stages from 1788 to 1791 to shelter the Ohio Company's families through a frontier war. **Now:** the stockade is gone except for one dwelling. The Rufus Putnam House stands on its original foundation inside the south wing of a brick museum built around it, and the ground outside is a city corner at Second and Washington Streets.

**Then:** a 27-by-19-foot hewn-log office just south of the stockade walls, where the Company's superintendent surveyed and divided a purchase of more than a million acres and the earliest maps of the Northwest Territory were drawn. **Now:** the same log office, moved onto the museum grounds in 1953, restored and furnished, standing beside the museum.

**Then:** a landing at the mouth of the Muskingum where 47 (or 48) New England men came ashore on April 7, 1788, opposite a federal fort. **Now:** a river town that keeps its founding indoors — the surviving fabric of 1788 is not a ruin field but two furnished wooden buildings, interpreted by three floors of museum exhibits on settlement, migration, and river life.

## 4. What's still there
- The Rufus Putnam House, enclosed within the Campus Martius Museum on its original location — the only surviving dwelling of the fortification (Ohio History Connection site page; National Park Service place record)
- The Ohio Company Land Office, the 1788 hewn-log office, on the museum grounds since 1953, restored and furnished (National Register nomination, National Archives; Northwest Territory Museum Society)
- The Campus Martius site ground at the corner of 2nd and Washington Streets, on the bluff above the Muskingum River (Ohio History Connection; National Register records)
- Three floors of exhibits tracing the early settlement of Ohio and later migrations, per the Ohio History Connection's site page
- The Putnam House interior, restored and furnished with pieces donated by Marietta pioneer families (Porter, Ohio History Journal; Northwest Territory Museum Society)

2. **Inside, at the Rufus Putnam House:** The core stop. 3. **Through the galleries:** The exhibits on the Ohio Company settlement and the Ordinance of 1787 — the paper trail of surveys, deeds, and maps that the Land Office next door was built to produce.

5. **On the bluff:** End above the Muskingum, reading the ground as the first party found it on April 7, 1788 — river mouth below, Fort Harmar's shore opposite, the stockade's square behind you under a city corner.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The landing party's size is disputed in the sources consulted, and it is printed here unresolved. The National Register nomination for the Ohio Company Land Office (National Archives) states that 47 New England men landed on April 7, 1788. E. O. Randall's Ohio History Journal account of the same landing writes "the forty-seven — (usually stated forty-eight)"; the 48-man figure is the one most retellings carry. This draft prints 47 as the nomination states it, flags 48 as the usual figure, and resolves neither.

The Putnam House's building date is a genuine tangle, printed as one. The National Park Service Register record gives 1788 as its significant year, and the Ohio History Connection presents the house as part of the 1788 stockade. Summers, in his 1903 *History of Marietta* (p. 311), wrote of the building "commonly called" the Old Block-House that it "was erected about the close of the Indian War, by General Rufus Putnam" — about 1795 — that "it was ever a block-house is reasonably doubted," and that "a portion of the timber of the building was a part of the original block-house." A house begun in the stockade in 1788 and a house erected about 1795 from blockhouse timber are different claims about the same walls; the sources consulted do not reconcile them, and this draft does not either.

The museum building's date conflicts between operator and Register sources: the Ohio History Connection's site page and the local museum both date the Campus Martius Museum to 1928, while the Ohio Company Land Office's National Register nomination (National Archives) describes the Land Office as "now located behind the Campus Martius Museum building, erected in 1930." Both dates print; neither is adopted here.

"Oldest building" claims around this site conflict and are printed as claims, not facts. The Ohio History Connection's site page calls the Land Office "reportedly the oldest building in Ohio"; the local museum's history page calls it "the oldest building in the Northwest Territory"; Summers in 1903 called it "generally believed to be the oldest house now standing in the State." The hedges in the sources ("reportedly," "generally believed") are part of the record, and this entry repeats the claims only with those hedges attached.
Operator terms differ in phrasing between the two operator-side sources (§1): the Ohio History Connection lists "Veterans - $5.00" and "Children under 6 - Free," while the local manager's page lists "Active Duty Military & Veterans (with ID): $5.00" and "Children 5 and under (excludes groups): Free," and only the local page prints the holiday closure list. The rates agree in amount; the categories are printed as each source states them.

No HABS survey of the Rufus Putnam House or the Ohio Company Land Office was found in the Library of Congress Pictures collection in this pass — a gap, stated as such. No coordinates were taken from any lead list: the locator's point comes solely from the Land Office nomination's own geographical-data section, scoped there. The Land Office's exact positions over its life before its 1953 move to the museum grounds were not verified from a primary source in this pass and are flagged as a gap.
