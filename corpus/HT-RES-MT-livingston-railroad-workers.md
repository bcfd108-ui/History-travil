## 2. The story

This stop asks what railroad employment meant after the train had left the station. Montana Historical Society's published account draws on named oral histories, including **Rose F. Toscano**, interviewed by **Julia Katherine Keffer on May 29, 1997**, catalog **OH 376-009**. It reproduces her recollection of **“the best pay in town”** for women. That five-word excerpt is a firsthand recollection mediated through the historian's article; the original recording was not heard here. [5]

A second named source is **Natalina Indendi Parisi, 1981 interview, OH 376-015**, interviewer unidentified. [5]

A separate museum finding aid records another working family: **Warren McGee** began as a Northern Pacific brakeman in Livingston in 1936, returned after wartime service, became a conductor in 1946 and retired in 1975. His father Howard had worked as an engineer. Warren later donated extensive historical material. This is the museum's biographical account, not an interview quotation from McGee. [6]

Together, the sources change the scale of the story. A railway could be a tourist's brief arrival but a resident's working lifetime. For a family, wages, occupational continuity and childcare could matter more than the elegance of a waiting room. That is this dossier's interpretive proposition, not a claim that every employee shared the same opportunities or experience.

The University of Montana separately catalogs Keffer's 1998 anthropology thesis, *Changing patterns of everyday living in Livingston Montana: Oral histories of women who worked for the railroad*, repository item **4887**. Its full-text download failed during this pass. The catalog confirms the research exists; it does not authorize inventing further testimony or page citations. [7]

When steam gave way to diesel, the change reached down to the shop floor. The county collection guide describes a 1957 building with tracks raised about three feet so workers could get beneath the locomotives. Compare that design with the tasks recalled by engine wipers. One generation turned steam locomotives on the roundhouse turntable. Another worked underneath diesels. The guide dates demolition of the last three roundhouse stalls to 1983. [9]

The dates depend on which facility closed. A university study places the depot and maintenance-yard closure in 1979, while the Historical Society places shop closure in 1986. The county guide dates railroad arrival to 1882 and the start of shop construction to 1883. The university study gives 1883 for arrival. Contemporary operating notices are still needed to resolve the different scopes. [5,9,10]

## 3. Then / now

**Then:** rail labor was daily employment and household income, documented through named recollections and employee-created collections. **Now:** the museum is a place to interpret that labor without entering its historic or contemporary workplaces. These are different sites and different kinds of access.

Rather than trying to duplicate a historic pose, consider how a named-worker photograph changes what you notice in a transportation display.

The Depot survives as a restored station and seasonal museum, not as evidence that an operating railroad welcomes visitors. [2] No original camera position, present-day alignment or publicly accessible view into former shops is established.

## 4. What's still there

The county museum advertises permanent transportation interpretation, particularly railway history, and an original Northern Pacific caboose among its outdoor exhibits. [8] That is evidence of the collection, **not** a guarantee that the caboose is open inside, snow-free or accessible during this winter visit. The indoor museum is the chosen public anchor.

The Depot's operator documents the restored station and its rail exhibit, *Rails Across the Rockies*. [2] It remains a separate, seasonal option, not an additional November interior stop. Do not improvise access through another tenant or an event entrance.

The museum also preserves a substantial railroad archival collection. Its finding aid warns that sensitive employee/personnel material is restricted for 75 years from creation. [9] This dossier uses published descriptions, not private employee files. Archive custody does not imply a publicly displayed object or unrestricted reproduction.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Before arrival:** read the short named-worker account [5]. Bring its source citation, not a presumed recording or an invented transcript.
2. **Inside the open county museum:** begin with transportation interpretation. Keep the visit inside currently admitted public areas; there is no connecting railway route in this plan.
3. **Look for the human record:** if an employee photograph or labor label is actually displayed, distinguish its date, author and named subject from the general story. If none is displayed, use the published source rather than claiming an exhibit omission without checking.
4. **Finish with the strongest story moment:** return to Toscano's valuation of the job. Consider a railroad not only as a machine that moved visitors, but as an employer whose pay entered a family's everyday decisions. This conclusion needs no trackside view.

**Onsite question for the visitor to consider:** Does the interpretation let me distinguish passenger experience from the experience of the people whose work made it possible? No outreach to staff or former workers has been undertaken.

Allow a flexible single-museum visit, not a timed circuit. If holiday or weather access cannot be confirmed, the source-reading version stands on its own. Do not replace a closed museum with a yard, shop or private residence.

No employee is assumed to have worked in the public Depot building simply because they worked for the railroad. Neither historic workplace ownership nor present access to it has been established. No generalized Indigenous account is inserted without an appropriate published Tribal source; this bounded labor dossier is not a complete account of regional land history.
