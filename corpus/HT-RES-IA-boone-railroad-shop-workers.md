# Boone — Nine hours in the new shops

Iowa OP05 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

**A locomotive shop ran on a hidden network.** In January 1912, Railway and Locomotive Engineering printed A. B. Silliman’s signed correspondence on completed improvements at the Chicago & North Western shops at Boone. It named J. W. Towne as maintenance engineer in charge and H. Rettinghouse as the Boone division engineer who assisted. The observer describes a pipe tunnel carrying steam, air and water, with a boiler shop, machine and carpenter shops above. Heated air passed through concrete tunnels to workers in the enlarged engine house. Silliman wrote, “Formerly the winter was a serious time with us.” The letter speaks from the mechanical department; Silliman’s job title was not independently located. The page image prints Rettinghouse, while the searchable text gives Rethinghouse. In the new machine shop, a crane moved heavy loads above the engine stalls and a powered transfer table moved locomotives from bay to bay. These details are evidence for what the plant was designed to do and what mechanics had at hand. They are no invitation to the active railroad. [5]

**What the hiring list says about division of labor.** A March 1912 notice in the same journal enumerated 20 machinists and 20 machinists’ helpers, five boilermakers and five boilermakers’ helpers among roughly 80 additions that also included other crafts. It reported, “The new shops are working nine (9) hours per day.” That is the trade journal's wording about the shop schedule. It does not say each worker clocked nine paid hours or supply wages. The January account's 36 engine-house stalls and 12 machine-shop engine stalls describe different spaces. A 1928 company directory independently lists the Boone roundhouse foreman and other division roles. It is later evidence, outside the 1912 hiring count. [5, 6]

**A rail town contains more than one railroad.** The historic works belonged to C&NW. The nearby Boone & Scenic Valley museum is the public interpretation anchor. Its excursion corridor has a separate Fort Dodge, Des Moines & Southern history. Use the museum as a place to understand railroad trades and equipment ownership over time. The museum or its visitor tracks do not by themselves locate Boone’s historic C&NW shop rooms. [1, 4, 5, 7]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic place.** The 1912 C&NW yard and shop compound in Boone. The Railway Historical Society database distinguishes several Boone roundhouses and cites maps for their location and survival. The 1916 map sequence is a locator for further research instead of a single universal shop pin. [5, 7, 8]

**Lawful public anchors.** In season, James H. Andrew Railroad Museum, 225 10th Street, a drive-up and short walk from legal parking to the signed public visitor entrance. Outside the season, use the ordinary downtown Story Street sidewalk on the south side of the rail corridor, with no need to cross the tracks or approach barriers. The municipal plan documents the working rail corridor through downtown. Skip if the sidewalk is obstructed or a clear stance is absent. [1–4]

## 5. A short visit

Read the two 1912 excerpts together. Start at the seasonal public museum, or read from downtown public pavement when safe, and use the surviving rail corridor to orient to the scale of railroad work in town. Land on **A shop shift under the crane** with the named crafts and a distinction between the old work buildings and the route trains use today. [1–5]
