## 2. Story

The museum uses three relocated Allendale cabins to tell a story that outlasted emancipation. Its accounts name Maria Jackson as a field worker, Harriet Jackson as a cook, and Stephen Jackson as a driver, a position with responsibility for supervising field labor in a system run by an overseer. The museum’s interpretation places the three relatives at Allendale during the years before the Civil War. The descriptions matter because the words “plantation labor” can otherwise make different jobs, obligations and exposures sound interchangeable. At each step, keep the museum’s research attribution attached to the names. No household testimony or payroll naming the Jacksons was read for this return. [2–4]

During the war, the museum says Union occupation was followed by the burning of the mill and main house. Some enslaved people were taken away by Barnabus Stevens, who managed the property while Henry Watkins Allen was away. The museum records that others fled or were dispersed. After the war, the buildings, ownership and forms of labor changed. The 1908–09 trade directory pairs “Kahao, M. J.” with Allendale and Lobdell in West Baton Rouge. Those printed entries show how an industry directory followed the business. Look for the people who produced the cane in a different archive. LSU’s Mss. 3824 finding aid identifies a cane record book and business papers, along with photographs and some copied material. A finding aid identifies where records may be found. It cannot establish what a particular worker was paid. [3–7]

A 1996 preservation survey also records an elder’s recollection that two cabins moved in the 1950s after most of the surviving buildings had settled into positions around the 1930s. The elder is unnamed in the printed passage. The later move into Port Allen brought some structures into a public museum with staff interpretation. Writing as the museum’s director, Julia Rose calls the work “interpreting difficult knowledge.” The supported comparison in these rooms is about changed labor and family life. It does not require putting a named Jackson relative in a room without a verified household record. [2, 5, 8]

## 3. Then and now

Then — Allendale’s plantation workers lived in a quarters landscape reshaped by agricultural change, floods and levee works. The 1996 nomination describes most of its surveyed cabins as approximately 1870–1900 and often arranged to house one family. Its dates apply to the inspected buildings in that nomination at that time. [3, 5]

Now — Port Allen’s public museum places selected relocated structures in an interpretive campus. It uses cabins to explore slavery, later wage labor and the civil-rights era. Separate the approximate age of a structure from the year of any furnished exhibit. The source properties and their private surroundings lie outside this visit. [1–5, 8]

## 5. Public progression

Check in at the main museum. Ask which structures staff can open on today's tour. Follow the signed public campus path to the Allendale cabins and, if escorted, compare the rooms staff make available. A gallery-only visit is a weather alternative during museum opening. [1–2]

**Strongest story moment — A cabin through two labor systems.** Pause at the cabin interpretation with the museum’s three named Jackson workers and the postwar directory’s entry for Kahao in mind. The surviving rooms help establish scale and layout. Named testimony and payroll would be needed to trace the residents’ own experience through the change. Quiet reflection. [2–8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- The museum labels one displayed cabin circa 1850, while the nomination’s surveyed set generally dates to circa 1870–1900. No cabin-by-cabin concordance connecting the museum labels to the nomination numbers was obtained. Preserve both scopes and leave the campus property’s register number blank. [2, 4–5]
- The parish marker page presents a 1852 purchase from William Nolan. The 1996 nomination describes an 1852 joint acquisition with Nolan and a 1855 division. The museum follows the broader 1852 acquisition chronology. The original deed was not retrieved, so the draft uses the nomination’s more qualified sequence. [2, 5, 10]
- The 1908–09 trade directory was read through a transcription. No independent scan of that page or attributable recording of an Allendale worker was obtained. Museum and LSU collections remain leads for named firsthand evidence. [6–9]
