## 2. The story
**Named firsthand worker:** Bob Wright's museum-hosted oral-history transcript records an interview with **Bea Mayes on January 30, 2004**, transcribed by Lola Beatlebrox. Pages 20–21 describe Wright's Snow Park patrol involvement and later part-time patrol work at Park City Resort. Asked whether the Snow Park position was paid, he answers **“Volunteer.”** This is an important distinction: a remembered work role is not automatically evidence of wages. [2]

His account also recalls learning from Mel Fletcher. The transcript's dates are not perfectly aligned: it places his high-school graduation in 1948 and subsequent patrol work, but later recalls patrolling from 1947. Preserve that discrepancy rather than inventing an exact employment start. No patrol procedures, incident reenactments or operational practices are reproduced here. [2]

**Community perspective:** Dalton Gackle's January 7, 2026 museum article excerpts a 2013 interview of Gary Kimball by Larry Warren. Kimball remembers employing Charlie Brown to paint lettering on souvenir objects for the Grub Steak Grocery. This is a small, specific example of labor around the visitor economy, not evidence that Brown worked on a ski patrol or for a resort. The original complete recording was not retrieved. [3]

**Original interpretive angle:** a resort town is not built only by the people whose names appear on its buildings. It also depends on the person helping visitors, the person making something they take home, and the person remembering how a familiar town became a new kind of workplace. A holiday and a workday can occupy the same town at the same time.

The point is not to romanticize unpaid service or label every old resident welcoming or hostile. These are particular people describing particular relationships. Neither source supplies a representative wage survey, every employee's experience, or a verdict on today's employers. The distinction between enjoyment and responsibility is the story's center.

In 1966 the *Salt Lake Tribune* put a name to a job a skier could easily overlook. Its caption identifies Frank Christensen as the man operating the Thayne Shaft hoist. A companion photograph shows the train waiting at the Spiro Tunnel. A museum guide preserves an unnamed rider’s comparison, “It’s just like riding the subway in New York.” The two accounts describe the same system from opposite ends. One person had bought a ride. Somebody else had to raise that person to the slopes. Read the caption before looking at the car in the museum if it is on view. [7,8]

## 3. Then / now
Historical ski labor belongs in the history of economic change, not just a list of lifts and opening dates. A participant's own recollection can establish how he understood his role while leaving payroll, dates and organizational arrangements unresolved.

Today the museum is the public interpretive anchor. It is not the Snow Park workplace described in the interview, and its gallery is not a resort staff area. The museum's institutional article connects the 1963 resort opening with later community change; its social labels are editorial framing, not neutral categories for every resident. [3]

The name Snow Park in an old interview must not be silently transferred into a current visitor route. A published private interview address is likewise provenance, not an invitation to visit that home. It is deliberately omitted from the locator.
