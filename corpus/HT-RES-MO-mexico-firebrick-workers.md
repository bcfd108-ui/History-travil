# Mexico — What the work ticket counted

Missouri OP05 · Draft for independent Dash review · Quiet witness for occupational loss

## 2. Story

A firebrick has to keep its shape where ordinary building material would fail. The Audrain County Historical Society’s Fire Brick Museum explains the local clay and shows equipment, raw material, records and bricks. In 1964 a federal appeals court described a factory supplying heat-resistant products to customers in the steel, glass, chemical and oil industries. Its account gives a measure of the site’s industrial reach, with 23 acres under roof at the plant then under review. Production at that size still passed through the hands of someone carrying cards to a timekeeper at the end of a shift. [2, 4]

Earl F. Docekal had worked at A. P. Green since 1941 and moved loads on a lift truck. The appeals court's opinion describes him as employed “on a piecework basis with a guaranteed minimum hourly rate.” That language is from the published judicial text. Docekal told the proceedings he could sign his name but could neither read nor write. The opinion describes foremen and timekeepers reading and altering tickets for him when work changed. The production count and the calculation behind the paycheck came from records that another person might have to read aloud. [4]

In 1961 Docekal sought help organizing the plant. A first election was set aside, then a second vote went against union representation. The company dismissed him in March 1962 after disputed entries in three tickets. The court records the company's assertion of dishonesty. Reviewing the Labor Board's findings, it upheld the finding that anti-union discrimination caused the discharge and sustained the Board's order. The 1965 court record concerns a distinct period after employees elected union representation and a labor agreement had been signed. It does not erase the 1961 election results. [4–5]

Another work record ends with a family claim. The 1957 Missouri Supreme Court opinion names Joe Sam Staples, employed from 1933 to 1943, and his widow and children. The court describes the parties' stipulation that he became disabled by silico-tuberculosis arising from the employment. The lower commission had granted a death benefit, but the circuit court reversed, and the Missouri Supreme Court affirmed under the historic statute’s timing limit. This is the court’s account of the agreed facts and ruling. It does not place Staples at a particular kiln or specify the machinery he worked. [6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historical locations.** The factory and employee meetings belonged to working Mexico. The company plant, its shipping floor and the union meeting room are documentary subjects. No former plant approach, specific loading bay or meeting-room entrance is verified as ordinary visitor ground. [4–7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Fire Brick Museum, Audrain County Historical Society, at the Robert S. Green Park museum complex, 501 South Muldrow, Mexico. A signed drive-up visitor arrival connects to the open museum entrance and museum-side walk. The society identifies its Fire Brick Museum as a 2008 addition and describes a brick walk bearing workers’ and companies’ names. The separate Graceland house is a historic resource within the complex. Its registration does not identify the factory or the 2008 museum wing. [1–3, 9]

## 5. A short visit

Begin in the Fire Brick Museum during ordinary opening and look at the progression from raw clay to heat-resistant material. Continue on the named brick walk if open and dry. Finish with **The ticket at the end of the shift**. Read the account of Docekal and the timekeeper alongside the names underfoot. The job becomes a person and a pay calculation. Keep the Staples family account a moment of quiet reflection. If the walk is icy, finish inside, or use the exhibits’ public descriptions and printed opinions remotely. [1–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

The museum’s peak figure exceeds 5,000 for the industry over its history. The federal opinion documents a 641-employee election scope at one plant in 1961. The figures belong to different scopes. The Board and appeals court described discrimination where management alleged dishonesty, including when work-ticket errors and correction practices were disputed. The transcript of testimony, original work tickets and Board decision 140 NLRB No. 101 were not retrieved. The appellate opinion is a republication of the original judicial content. The earlier Staples employment record and underlying stipulation were not retrieved independently. The company papers C2960 and the photograph finding aids establish holdings, with those individual materials still held for item lookup and reuse. [2, 4–8]
