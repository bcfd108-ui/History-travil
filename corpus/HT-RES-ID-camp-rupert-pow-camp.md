# Camp Rupert Prisoner of War Camp and the workers who passed through Paul

**Research draft · Labeled THEME SWEEP (Family 5 — Military installations, WWII POW & internee labor) · Near Paul, Minidoka County, Idaho · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate

Reflection stop. Two lawful public anchors serve Camp Rupert, with different winter availability:

1. **Idaho Historical Marker #506 ("Camp Rupert"):** Year-round outdoor roadside marker on **Idaho State Highway 25 (100 South Road)** near **1150 West Road**, roughly 2.5–6.7 miles west of Paul depending on highway measurement (located on the right when traveling east on ID-25). View from public roadside right-of-way only; do not enter adjacent private farmland. [1]
2. **Minidoka County Historical Society Museum (`99 E. Baseline Road, Rupert, ID 83350`):** Holds Camp Rupert artifacts and copies/records related to the camp's German-language POW newspaper *Der Neue Weg*. Free admission (donations appreciated). **Winter caveat:** Regular public hours (**Tuesday–Saturday, 10:00 a.m.–3:00 p.m. MT**) are seasonal summer hours; as of current county facility listings, the museum is **closed for the winter season and accepting tour requests by appointment** (`208-436-0336` or `208-260-2613`). Confirm an appointment before traveling to Rupert. [2, 3, 4]

The former 300-acre camp compound north of ID-25 is **private agricultural/commercial ground with no public camp tour**. [1]

## 2. The story

The ground west of Paul carried three distinct chapters of federal confinement and agricultural labor between the Great Depression and the end of World War II. Originally built in the 1930s as a **Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC)** camp on the Minidoka irrigation project, the facility was transferred during the early war years to the **Farm Security Administration (FSA)** as a migratory farm-labor camp. In the spring and summer of 1942—and again on temporary agricultural work furloughs into 1943—the camp housed **Japanese-American workers**, including families and young men recruited from West Coast assembly centers and the **Manzanar** War Relocation Center to thin and harvest Magic Valley sugar beets and potatoes. Library of Congress photographer Russell Lee documented Japanese-American residents inside these converted CCC timber barracks in July 1942, living among camp cots, Burnside coal stoves, and improvised crate furniture. [5, 6, 7]

In late 1943, the U.S. War Department converted and vastly expanded the compound into **Prisoner of War Camp Rupert**, Idaho's largest World War II POW installation. Built at an initial Army construction cost of roughly **$500,000**, the base expanded across **300 acres** to over **170 buildings**—including barracks, mess halls, a hospital, a field house, a chapel, commissaries, large warehouses, and recreation centers—staffed by nearly **1,000 Army personnel and civilians**. [1]

Camp Rupert functioned as the regional headquarters for **24 branch camps across Idaho, Oregon, and Montana**, housing roughly **4,000** prisoners (with peak and cumulative totals varying by period across German Afrika Korps and European-theater soldiers as well as Italian POWs; in 1944, agricultural-history records also note more than 500 Italian and German POWs at work locally). Under Geneva Convention labor rules, enlisted prisoners worked on surrounding Magic Valley farms—harvesting sugar beets, potatoes, onions, and hay—and maintained regional irrigation canals during acute wartime farm-labor shortages. [1, 6]

Inside the wire, German prisoners published their own weekly camp newspaper, ***Der Neue Weg*** (*The New Way*), launched **June 10, 1945** by the camp's *Arbeitsgemeinschaft "Der Neuer Weg"* and preserved in Library of Congress newspaper holdings (`sn88080889`). Following Germany's surrender and post-war repatriation, Camp Rupert closed in 1946 and its temporary buildings were dismantled, sold, or moved off-site, returning the tract to Magic Valley agriculture. [1, 8]

### Haunted history / lore

No primary-sourced haunted history or ghost tradition appears in military, FSA/WRA, newspaper, or Idaho Historical Society records for Camp Rupert. This entry treats the site strictly as documented wartime internment, furlough-labor, and POW history. [1, 5, 8]

| Period | Use & population | Surviving footprint |
|---|---|---|
| 1930s | CCC camp supporting Minidoka Project reclamation | Converted into FSA camp; no standing CCC barracks verified on-site [5] |

## 4. What's still there

- **Publicly accessible — Idaho Historical Marker #506 (`ID-25` / `100 S. Rd.` near `1150 W. Rd.`):** Standing metal state historical marker erected by the Idaho State Historical Society and Idaho Transportation Department along the public highway shoulder. [1]
- **Publicly accessible by season/appointment — Minidoka County Historical Society Museum (`99 E. Baseline Rd., Rupert`):** Preserves local POW camp artifacts and *Der Neue Weg* newspaper materials; winter visits require advance appointment. [2, 3]
- **Excluded — Former 300-acre camp footprint:** Private agricultural parcels near Paul/Hynes; no walking trail or preserved barracks compound open to the public. [1]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Camp population counts:** Marker #506 summarizes Camp Rupert as housing "some 4000 POWs," while period/archival summaries of the Paul camp in 1944 note "more than 500 Italian and German POWs" at a given snapshot alongside the broader 24-branch-camp network across three states. Both figures reflect different scopes (total camp/branch system capacity vs. single-season local roll) and await War Department Provost Marshal General monthly strength rosters for exact date-by-date counts. [1, 6]
- **Exact acreage overlap (1942 FSA camp vs. 1943–1946 POW camp):** Period sources confirm the 1942 FSA/CCC camp at Rupert housed Japanese-American workers before the 1943 Army POW construction, and Densho/SABR records place 1943 Manzanar furlough workers at Camp Rupert near Paul before POW arrival. Whether the 300-acre POW footprint completely absorbed the older CCC/FSA barracks or adjoined them on an expanded tract awaits the 1943 USACE real-estate plat. [1, 5, 6]
- ***Der Neue Weg* end date:** Library of Congress cataloging (`sn88080889`) verifies Issue 1 began **June 10, 1945**, but leaves the final issue date open (`1945-194?`). [8]
