# Old Joliet Prison — the Illinois State Penitentiary at Joliet, 1858–2002
HT-RES-IL-old-joliet-prison · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The prison at Joliet replaced Illinois' first state penitentiary at Alton, a privately managed institution on a Mississippi bluff that the record describes as having degraded into horrendous conditions. In 1857 the General Assembly appropriated funds for a new 1,000-cell prison at Joliet, and the state built it in the era's characteristic way: with convict labor, leased by the state to contractor Lorenzo P. Sanger and warden Samuel K. Casey, in limestone quarried on the site itself. The design, by Chicago architect William W. Boyington — the architect of the Chicago Water Tower — was Castellated Gothic Revival: a fortress of Joliet limestone, built by the men who would be held inside it. The first prisoners arrived from Alton in May 1858 to begin construction around themselves, and the transfer from Alton was completed by July 1860. During the Civil War the penitentiary held both convicted men and prisoners of war. The National Register nomination, listed September 25, 2023, judges the district significant under Criterion A as the oldest and longest-operating penitentiary complex in Illinois. [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]

The state's own corrections history supplies the labor system that ran inside the walls, and this dossier does not soften it. The leasing of prisoners — first to a lessee who furnished supplies, handled the products of convict labor and exercised the warden's general powers, then, after the state took direct control, under a successor system that let fixed numbers of prisoners to private contractors by the day for work in specified industries — built fortunes, in the state history's own judgment, in the shoe, shirt and furniture factories and the foundries of the old Joliet prison. Organized labor's opposition ended contract labor in 1904, replaced by a state prison-industries system that was itself attacked and reduced over the following decades. The men who quarried the limestone and raised the walls, and the men who later worked the prison industries, are the ground's first population; their individual names are largely absent from the sources checked this run. [6]

Women were held here from the beginning, in the arrangement the era made for them: inside a men's prison. The original plans included a one-hundred-cell Female Cell House within the male penitentiary; women were housed adjacent to the men's cells from 1859 to 1870, then on the fourth floor of the central administration building. In 1896 a separate one-hundred-cell Joliet Women's Prison was built across Collins Street, an exact miniature replica of the men's prison in design. In 1933 all women prisoners were transferred to the Oakdale Women's Reformatory (later Dwight Correctional Center), and the women's building across the street passed to housing men — a building that is part of the listed historic district, whose full name in the nomination is the Illinois State Penitentiary-Joliet Historic District, including the Illinois Women's Prison. [5, 7]

The main prison outlived every plan to close it. By 1878 it was filled well over capacity, with nearly 2,000 inmates and reports of unsanitary and dangerous conditions; calls for closure came by 1905, and the construction of Stateville Penitentiary in what is now Crest Hill in the 1920s was intended to replace it. It did not close. It operated on — through the whole arc of twentieth-century American incarceration, with minority populations historically overrepresented inside it, a fact the City's National Register grant application states plainly and builds the nomination around — until Governor George Ryan closed it in 2002 as a budgetary measure, after 144 years. Inmates and most staff transferred to Stateville. [5, 7, 8, 9]

What followed was the familiar American sequence for a closed prison, and then a less familiar turn. The vacant complex deteriorated and was vandalized; on May 30, 2017, the mattress factory building was heavily damaged by a fire later determined to be arson, the third building severely damaged or destroyed by fire at the facility (see Gaps for a dating variance in secondary accounts). In December 2017 the City of Joliet secured control of the property from the state — a five-year intergovernmental lease in one account, a taking of ownership in another, printed as a conflict below — and in 2018 the Joliet Area Historical Museum began running tours, opening the decommissioned prison to the public for the first time as a historic site. A partnership the museum assembled, the Old Joliet Prison Preservation Coalition, drew thousands of volunteer hours and substantial in-kind labor and donations. In 2020 the National Park Service awarded the City a $35,000 Underrepresented Communities grant to prepare the National Register nomination expressly to detail the penitentiary's role in racial bias, equity and criminal justice reform, past and present; the district was listed on September 25, 2023 (reference SG100009371). The museum now interprets the prison as a site of conscience, in the framing carried by its Google Arts & Culture exhibit — a place to discuss the harms of mass incarceration and the need for reform — alongside its Route 66 fame. That fame is real and is recorded here as record, not as the story: the prison's limestone gate is the opening image of the 1980 film *The Blues Brothers*, whose Jake Blues takes his nickname, "Joliet Jake," from release at this gate, and the complex later stood in as Fox River State Penitentiary in the television series *Prison Break* and appeared in other productions. [2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The penitentiary stands at 1125 Collins Street, approximately 1.5 miles north of downtown Joliet and just east of the Des Plaines River, in the same canal-and-rail industrial corridor as the Joliet Iron Works, whose ruins lie to the south — the prison's walls are visible from the Iron Works ground, and the two dossiers are a pair: the works that employed Joliet's free immigrant labor, and the institution that held its leased and confined labor, within sight of each other. The nomination describes a complex whose tour footprint is given as 16 acres in operator-facing material, while the grant record describes a 180-acre penitentiary resource; the variance is printed in Gaps and neither figure is merged. [2, 3, 7]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Old Joliet Prison Historic Site, 1125 Collins St., Joliet, IL 60432 — entered only on the operator's tour gate: self-guided and guided tours in season, through the East Gate / gift-shop check-in the booking listings describe, on the operator's posted days and hours, with the seasonal closure stated in the trip gate. There is no lawful walk-in access outside the tour program, no access at all in the closed season, and no version of this visit that involves entering any part of the complex outside a ticketed tour. The commercial haunted attraction across Collins Street described in press reporting is a separate operation and is not this anchor. Reflection posture: an active preservation site and a place where people lived confined, worked, and died; conduct and language on site should match that. [1, 2, 3]

## 5. Public progression

In season, and only on a confirmed tour day, take the self-guided route first and the guided history tour if the calendar offers one on your day — the guided tour reaches areas the self-guided route does not, per visitor-facing operator material, and its arc is the dossier's arc, 1858 to 2002. Walk it in the institution's own order. Begin at the gate and the Administration Building: the castellated limestone front is Boyington's design and the prisoners' own quarry work at once, authority and labor in a single wall. Move to the cell houses and read them quantitatively before qualitatively — a 1,000-cell plan, nearly 2,000 men by 1878, cells built for an earlier century's discipline holding a later century's population. Stand in the industries ground and hold the state's own account in mind: leased men, then contract men, then state-industry men, in shoe, shirt and furniture factories and foundries, on this footprint. Include the women's prison in the reading even from outside its gate if entry is not offered that day: for its first thirty-eight years this institution held women inside a men's prison, and the small replica across the street is the physical form of that afterthought. **Strongest story moment · Built by the men inside it.** The limestone was quarried on the site and the walls raised by leased convict labor under Sanger and Casey; the first 1858 arrivals built the prison around themselves. Every later story here — overcrowding, industry, film fame, preservation — happens inside work that confined men did with their hands. End at the gate where the *Blues Brothers* opening was filmed and let the two registers sit side by side without merging: a beloved film's first joke, and a site of conscience interpreting 144 years of confinement, are the same stones. Leave by the tour route, in season hours, and if the season has closed, the lawful visit is the exterior from the public street and this dossier's reading — nothing more. [1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Seasonal-hours conflict, printed in full, with the closure that governs: the operator's own site checked this run gives Thursday–Monday 10 a.m.–4 p.m. and a seasonal closing date of November 23rd, weather permitting. A museum press release carried in October press gives standard daytime tours Monday–Wednesday 10 a.m.–1 p.m. and Thursday–Sunday 10 a.m.–4 p.m. A place-data listing gives 10 a.m.–4 p.m. seven days. A tour-summary page states the site closes for the winter in November and reopens by April, weather permitting. No source checked this run reconciles the day-patterns. Because the checked date (Nov. 21) falls two days before the operator's stated seasonal close, the trip gate treats ALL hours as confirm-with-operator and the season as ending; no hours line is published as settled. [1, 2, 3]
- First-arrivals number conflict: one summary line gives the first 33 inmates arriving from Alton in May 1858 to begin construction; the museum's own exhibit account and another summary give 53 prisoners arriving on May 22, 1858. Both figures are in circulation from the same reference tradition; the nomination text checked this run was not read to the page that would settle it. The Story therefore claims the May 1858 arrival without a number. [5, 9]
- Closure-year conflict: the operator, the museum exhibit and the standard history give closure in 2002 (144 years, 1858–2002, under Governor Ryan's budget measures); a Route 66 news report on the Register listing states the state closed it in 2004. The dossier uses 2002 — the operator's and nomination tradition's date — and prints 2004 here as the outlier. A 2002 holding-prison closure with residual functions ending later would reconcile the two, but no source checked this run states that reconciliation, so it is offered as a possibility, not a finding. [7, 9, 10]
- City-control instrument conflict: one account records that in December 2017 the City secured a five-year intergovernmental lease on the prison; another records that in 2017 the City took ownership from the state; a third that the mayor petitioned the state to allow the City to take control. The operator is consistently the Joliet Area Historical Museum in partnership with the City across all accounts; the legal instrument is not settled in this record and the Locator names the operator, not an owner. [1, 8, 9]
- Acreage variance: operator-facing tour material gives a 16-acre footprint for the tour experience; the federal grant announcement describes the Illinois State Penitentiary–Joliet as a 180-acre historic resource. These most plausibly measure the walled tour core against the full penitentiary grounds including the women's prison parcel, but no source checked this run states the boundary definitions; both figures are printed and neither is used for the other. [2, 8]
- Fire-date variance: contemporary reporting dates the mattress-factory arson fire to May 30, 2017, and notes it was the third building severely damaged or destroyed by fire at the facility; a later summary dates "an arson at the prison" to August 2017. The dossier uses May 30, 2017 for the mattress factory and prints the August date as an unreconciled secondary variant. Earlier fires are asserted ("third building") but not individually dated in the sources checked this run. [10]
- Address form variance: the operator and the Register listing give 1125 Collins St.; the nomination text in places gives 1125 West Collins Street. Same property; the operator's form governs the gate. [1, 7]
- Women's Prison access gap: the women's prison across Collins Street is a named component of the listed district, but no tour listing checked this run states whether any tour enters it. The dossier promises no entry and treats it as district fabric read from the lawful tour ground/street unless the operator confirms access. [3, 7]
- Named-inmate gap and rule: famous-inmate lists circulate widely for this prison in popular sources. No individual inmate's presence, sentence or conduct is claimed in this dossier, because none was verified against the nomination or a corrections record this run — and on incarceration ground, popular notoriety lists are not evidence. The *Blues Brothers* and *Prison Break* filming record, by contrast, is carried by the museum's own exhibit and multiple independent accounts and is stated as record. [9]
- Haunted claims, filed as lore: the prison is the object of extensive paranormal programming and claims, including a commercial haunted attraction across Collins Street that press reporting distinguishes from the museum's operation. No claim of that kind is a documented event; all of it is filed here as lore and commercial programming, kept separate from the documented record, and none of it changes the gate — entry is by the museum's ticketed tours, in season, only. [2, 3]
- Accessibility gap: preservation accounts note the complex is still undergoing preservation, lacks utilities throughout, and the tour resembles an outdoor hike over a 16-acre footprint. No step-free route or accessible-tour specification was published in the pages checked this run; a visitor who needs one must ask the operator before booking, and this dossier does not promise one. [2]
