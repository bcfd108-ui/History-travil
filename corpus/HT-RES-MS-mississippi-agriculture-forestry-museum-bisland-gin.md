# Mississippi Agriculture and Forestry Museum — the men who put the Bisland gin back to work

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP11 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

The public stop is the Mississippi Agriculture and Forestry Museum, 1150 Lakeland Drive, Jackson. Begin at museum admissions. Regular hours are Monday–Saturday, 9 a.m.–5 p.m. The museum charges admission. Its November 10–14, 2026 Harvest Fest advertised gin demonstrations. Check the museum calendar for another demonstration before planning around moving machinery. [1–3]

## 2. Story

A machine designed to stay in one place had to be taken apart to survive. Peter Bisland built his gin in 1892 in Cannonsburg, northeast of Natchez. Growers brought in seed cotton. Inside, the gin stands separated lint from seed so the lint could be pressed into bales. Ginning was a distinct job after the field work of growing and picking. Three Gullett stands still tell that part of the story. So does their power source: the gin began on steam, then acquired a single-cylinder oil engine built in 1929. The machine in Jackson preserves more than one generation of the trade. [4–5]

The gin stopped processing the community’s crop in 1954. Its later owner, Charles McMahon, hoped to keep it in Cannonsburg. He eventually donated it to the museum. By then the pine building had deteriorated beyond moving. Gin mechanics Bobby and Hilton Tarver and their father took on the job of moving and reassembling the equipment. On August 14, 1984, it produced its first bale in Jackson. That date marks the success of a second crew, almost a century after the first installation. [4–6]

## 3. Then and now

At Cannonsburg the gin served farms with cotton ready to sell. In Jackson it sits in an educational reconstruction called Small Town, Mississippi. The museum assembled the town to represent the businesses a rural crossroads could support. The gin’s machinery came from a real business elsewhere. Its surrounding buildings do not reconstruct a particular Cannonsburg street. [2,4–6]

## 4. Surviving features and access

The three gin stands and the later engine are the central artifacts. The housing in Jackson replaces the lost gin building. Use the public exhibit areas the museum opens that day. A demonstration requires an operating crew and scheduled access. On a quiet day the Heritage Gallery provides indoor interpretation of agriculture and transport. [1–5]

## 5. Public progression and strongest story moment

**Listen for how two rooms worked together.** Reporting from a museum demonstration, *Farm Progress* identified Matt Temple and Chaston Bullock at the engine, and Matt’s father Fred Temple at the gin. Once the engine was ready, the mechanics sounded one whistle blast. Fred answered with two. The exchange carried information a visitor could miss by looking at a finished bale. It belongs to a witnessed museum demonstration; the earliest Cannonsburg crew is still unnamed here. [5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The foundation dates the sale to McMahon to 1952. The trade account places it after the 1954 shutdown. Both document his later gift. A bill of sale would settle the purchase date. The 2026 advertisement establishes what was scheduled, and supplies no attendance or proof of a particular run. Neither the foundation’s qualified national age claim nor a name for an 1892 field worker can be secured from these records. [3–5]

## 7. Historical photograph

Carol M. Highsmith documented the Jackson machinery on November 4, 2017. The Library of Congress identifies item 2017883583, digital file LC-DIG-highsm-46861, and lists no known publication restrictions. The catalog subject heading says Jackson County while the museum is in Jackson, Hinds County. The item would illustrate the museum installation in 2017. At the museum, a dated note about whether the gin exhibit was open, static, or scheduled to run would help the next visitor. A photograph of its public exhibit label is optional if staff permit it. Credit and permission would be requested before use.
