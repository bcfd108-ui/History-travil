## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** a glamorous room could mean employment and neighborhood pride while the city's other front doors remained unequal.

**Anna Bailey**, a Black professional dancer, was interviewed by **Claytee D. White on March 3, 1997**. UNLV publishes a clip with transcription in which she recalls her arrival at the Moulin Rouge and the neighborhood's expectations. Her shortest explanation is also its center: **“There was so much hope. Everybody was so optimistic. It was employment.”** [4]

The transcription was read; the recording was not heard. Her account treats the venue as a workplace and a community possibility, not simply a backdrop for famous visitors. It also records concern about the location and pride in the service and presentation. Her explanation for the closure is explicitly a personal belief, not established financial causation. [4]

The collection catalogue, **OH-00096**, describes further testimony about segregation and her later work in an integrated dance line at the Flamingo. That broader description is catalogue evidence, not a claim that the entire transcript was read. [5]

**Original interpretation:** the important contrast is not old Las Vegas versus new Las Vegas. It is the distinction between being hired to produce hospitality and being permitted to receive it. A public archive allows that question to be explored without making anyone's present workplace a spectacle.

## 3. Then / now

UNLV's historical exhibit dates the Moulin Rouge's initial operation from **May 24 to October 1955**, and reports receivership for unpaid goods and services. It labels the idea that competing late shows caused the closure as rumor. [6] Preserve the difference between community memory and a documented business event; neither erases the other.

**1955 opening, March 1960 desegregation negotiations and 1971 employment action are different milestones.** The university's timeline associates the Moulin Rouge agreement with public access to previously segregated casino establishments. [7] Its curriculum distinguishes that from later employment changes associated with a federal consent decree. [8] Equal access as a patron and equal employment opportunity were not one completed event.

The present UNLV archive is a custodian of these accounts, not the former hotel-casino and not a reconstruction. No current condition, ownership or visitor opening of the historic Moulin Rouge grounds was established. The original footprint is deliberately excluded from the trip.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Resolve public access first.** Choose a confirmed archive appointment where needed; otherwise keep this digital. Do not replace a closed reading room with a visit to the original grounds.
2. **Begin with the voice, not the skyline.** Read the short transcript passage with its interviewer and interview date visible. Leave the screen silent unless choosing to play the recording separately; this dossier has not verified its audio.
3. **Place the worker beside the record.** If a supplied image or catalogue item is available, identify whether it documents performance, employment, public accommodation or later remembrance. These are different claims.
4. **Compare the milestones.** Use the institutional histories to separate the opening of one venue from a citywide agreement and subsequent employment struggle. Do not turn progress into a claim that exclusion simply ended.
5. **Strongest moment:** return to the idea that a job could carry hopes for a neighborhood. Ask what evidence would show that those hopes became durable opportunities. End with reflection, not a triumphant completion stamp.

This is a single archive encounter, not an activated civil-rights walking route. The design does not require anyone to enter a gambling venue or spend money there.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

UNLV's agreement timeline displays **March 25, 1960**, while its curriculum describes the meeting as **March 26**. [7,8] The original meeting record or contemporary reporting has not been checked to settle that one-day difference. Retain “March 1960” in the main narrative.

The curriculum PDF's direct retrieval returned 404; its indexed institutional text was readable. Its employment summary must not be treated as the original court decree or as proof that no Black person held any front-of-house position before 1971. [8] A repository-hosted Roosevelt Fitzgerald manuscript separately discusses the 1971 negotiations, but it is historical analysis, not the executed decree. [9]

The full Bailey interview remains unexamined. The catalogue's person dates were not independently checked and are not repeated here. Winter access and the two official address/hour versions need trip-date reconciliation. No physical-site inspection or current exhibition inventory was performed.
