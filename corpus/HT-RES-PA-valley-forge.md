# HT-RES-PA-valley-forge — The stone house at the edge of a log city of twelve thousand
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

For six months a stone farmhouse rented from a miller's family was the headquarters of an army that built itself a log city in the fields around it — and the National Park Service's own history of that winter is a correction of the starving, snowbound myth the place is famous for.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a park, not a point: Valley Forge National Historical Park, preserving the ground of the Continental Army's winter encampment of December 19, 1777 – June 19, 1778, on both sides of the Schuylkill River northwest of Philadelphia, operated by the National Park Service. Within it, the anchor building is Washington's Headquarters — the Isaac Potts House — which the Park Service opens seasonally, with summer and winter hours; the operator's headquarters page directs visitors to its Operating Hours & Seasons page for details and states no specific hours itself, and no hours or fees are printed here beyond that. The Park Service also carries a 10-mile Encampment Tour Route, a driving loop of nine major stops, as its own framing of the ground.

## 2. The story
Before it was a camp it was a working landscape. Indigenous peoples occupied the river valley as early as 10,000–8,000 years before present; the last native people to inhabit the area were the Lenape, also known as the Delaware, and Europeans settling from the late 17th century pushed them out. By 1777 most of the land had been cleared for agriculture: within what is now the park, 18 landowners held farms, and along Valley Creek an iron works named Valley Forge had grown a small industrial village — charcoal houses, a saw mill, a grist mill, and a company store. Three months before the army arrived, a British raid had burned the forges themselves; a soldier arriving in December 1777 would have seen their ruins among the farm fields, fences, and hedgerows.

The Philadelphia campaign put the army there. In the fall of 1777 the British under Sir William Howe — nearly 17,000 troops landed at the head of Chesapeake Bay — took the American capital at Philadelphia despite hard-fought American stands at Brandywine and Germantown. Washington's roughly 12,000-man army had lost both battles and the capital, and, in the Park Service's reading of the record, emerged confident rather than broken: they had come close to beating the British and knew it. Washington had to balance Congress's wish for a winter campaign against the needs of a weary, poorly supplied army, and by December 12 he had decided on Valley Forge — 18 miles northwest of Philadelphia, close enough to maintain pressure on the enemy in the capital and protect the countryside, its military stores, and the Congress that had fled to York, yet far enough to prevent a surprise attack; a naturally defensible plateau, with the Schuylkill River to the north and high ground between Mount Joy and Mount Misery.

Washington and his campaign-weary army marched into camp on **December 19, 1777** — more than 12,000 soldiers and 400 women and children. The Park Service states the correction directly: contrary to popular myth, the soldiers marching in were not downtrodden, just exhausted and ill-supplied. The winter of 1777–78 was neither the coldest nor the snowiest of the war; what made conditions extremely difficult was the cycle of freezing and thawing, intermittent snow and rain, and shortages of provisions, clothing, and shoes.

The army built its shelter itself. Valley Forge was the first winter encampment where many thousands of men had to build their own huts: construction squads raised cabins to a 14-foot by 16-foot model, set in parallel lines until the camp, in one officer's words, "had the appearance of a little city" — in all, more than 1,500 log huts (the Park Service's history also gives a 2,000-odd figure; see §6), two miles of fortifications, five earthen forts, trenches, and a bridge over the Schuylkill built on a Roman design. The encampment was, in effect, the fourth largest city in America at the time, and as diverse as any city: free and enslaved African American soldiers and civilians, Indigenous people, wealthy officers, impoverished enlisted men, European immigrants, speakers of several languages, adherents of multiple religions.

Supply, not weather, was the crisis, and it came in waves. Army records of food shipped to camp in January 1778 show an average daily ration of one-half pound of beef per man. The most serious food crisis came in February, when the men went without meat for several days at a stretch. Clothing shortages caused severe hardship for a number of men — though many soldiers had a full uniform — and at the worst point, in early March, the army listed 2,898 men as unfit for duty for lack of clothing; well-equipped units took their places on patrol and forage. There was never a battle at Valley Forge. Disease — influenza and typhoid among it, spread through a concentrated camp — killed nearly 2,000 people during the encampment.

At the camp's edge stood the headquarters. The Isaac Potts House, a two-story stone house built by the Potts family — the National Register nomination for the house dates it 1758 and names Isaac Potts as builder; the Park Service's current place page dates construction only to sometime in the 1760s or early 1770s (see §6) — was rented at the time of the encampment by Deborah Hewes, a relative of Isaac Potts, who rented the entire house with some furnishings to Washington and his military family. That household included aides-de-camp John Laurens and Alexander Hamilton, and salaried and enslaved servants including Elizabeth Thompson and Hannah Till — everyone living and working together in the house. The National Register nomination carries Washington's tenure as Christmas Eve 1777 to June 18, 1778. The Park Service estimates that much of the structure standing today is original to the encampment era; the house's own nomination puts the original fabric at about 90 percent.

The winter's work was reform as much as endurance. Washington persuaded Congress to reform the supply system, and on **February 23, 1778** Baron von Steuben arrived at Valley Forge with a letter of introduction from the President of Congress, Henry Laurens; he trained a model company and then the army at large, and the reforms in supply, tactics, hygiene, and organization that came out of the encampment became, in the Park Service's assessment, the foundation of the modern United States Army. In May word came that the alliance with France was secured. The British evacuated Philadelphia, and on **June 19, 1778** Washington's troops marched out of Valley Forge in pursuit — a more unified force than the one that had marched in.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a farmed, partly industrial valley — 18 farms, an iron forge village on Valley Creek, and the forge ruins a British raid had left three months before. **Now:** a National Historical Park holding the encampment ground on both sides of the Schuylkill, its headquarters house and tour route operated by the National Park Service.

**Then:** a log city raised in weeks — more than 1,500 huts in parallel lines, two miles of fortifications, five redoubts, a Roman-design bridge — housing over 12,000 soldiers and 400 women and children. **Now:** open encampment ground; the huts are gone, and what stands from the winter itself is the stone headquarters house, estimated by the Park Service to be largely original to the encampment era.

**Then:** the famous image — a starving, ragged army leaving bloody footprints in the snow. **Now:** the Park Service's own history rejects that image as a disservice to the soldiers: an exhausted, ill-supplied, confident army, in a winter that was neither the coldest nor the snowiest of the war, whose worst enemy was a broken supply system and disease.

## 4. What's still there
- The encampment ground itself: the defensible plateau south of the Schuylkill River between Mount Joy and Mount Misery, 18 miles northwest of Philadelphia, preserved as Valley Forge National Historical Park
- Washington's Headquarters — the Isaac Potts House — the stone house Washington's household occupied, much of it estimated original to the encampment era, open to visit seasonally per the operator
- The headquarters' setting at the confluence country of Valley Creek and the Schuylkill, where the forge village stood and the forge ruins lay when the army arrived
- The lines of the camp as the Park Service interprets them: the 10-mile Encampment Tour Route, nine major stops across the ground the brigades occupied
- The Grand Parade ground tradition: the broad valley around which the brigades were arrayed and where von Steuben's training was carried out (per the park's cultural landscape record)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the park's edge, reading the ground first:** Schuylkill River to the north, Mount Joy and Mount Misery, the plateau between — the defensive geometry that was the reason for the camp before it was the scene of it.
2. **On the encampment ground:** Hold the log city in mind at scale — 1,500-plus huts in parallel lines, two miles of fortifications — against the open ground that remains.
3. **At Washington's Headquarters (the Isaac Potts House):** The one headquarters building that survives from the winter, read as a rented farmhouse worked by a mixed household of aides, salaried servants, and enslaved servants — not a mansion.
4. **Along the operator's Encampment Tour Route:** The Park Service's own 10-mile, nine-stop framing of the ground, taken as the park presents it — a conditional progression, not an activated route of this record.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The "starvation winter" is a dispute the Park Service itself adjudicates, and this record prints its ruling rather than the myth. Against the popular image — helpless troops in rags, bloody footprints in the snow, exceptional misery — the Park Service's history states: the winter of 1777–78 was neither the coldest nor the snowiest of the war; the men who marched in were exhausted and ill-supplied, not downtrodden; provisions were available in the early months, with army records showing an average daily ration of one-half pound of beef per man in January 1778; the worst food crisis was February's several days without meat; and hardship at Valley Forge was, in the context of the war, "suffering as usual" — privation was the Continental soldier's constant companion. What the record supports is a supply-system failure with a February crisis point, a clothing shortage that left 2,898 men unfit for duty at the early-March worst, and disease — not battle, and not a single winter of unbroken starvation — killing nearly 2,000 people. The myth is printed here as myth because the operator's history names it one, while noting that the myth itself has become part of what the place means.

The hut count varies inside the Park Service's own history: one passage places "the 2,000-odd huts in parallel lines," another states the encampment "included more than 1,500 log huts." Both figures are printed as carried; this record does not reconcile them.

The headquarters house carries two construction dates. Its National Register nomination states the house was built in **1758** by Isaac Potts, a Quaker, and is about 90 percent original. The National Park Service's current place page states the building was constructed "sometime in the 1760s or early 1770s" by the Potts family, with much of the structure estimated original to the encampment era. The dates are printed as a dispute; this record does not pick one. The nomination likewise carries Washington's tenure at the house as Christmas Eve 1777 to June 18, 1778, while the army's encampment as a whole runs December 19, 1777 to June 19, 1778 in the Park Service's history — the house dates and the camp dates are not the same claim and are not merged here.

The park's acreage is likewise date-stamped by its sources: the National Register nomination for the then-state park describes approximately 2,255 acres; the modern park is larger. No single acreage is printed as timeless fact.
