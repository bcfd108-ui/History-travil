## 2. The story
**Nat Love supplies a direct Ogden connection in his own published account.** In chapter XXI of his 1907 autobiography, he describes transferring to the Ogden division and writes that he **“always looked on Ogden as my home station.”** The phrase occurs on printed page 149, following the transfer discussion on page 148. The scanned book was downloaded and the relevant text read after an initial retrieval failure. This is not a national porter anecdote attached to Ogden by inference. [4]

Love's discussion emphasizes relationships with supervisors and the continuity of a home division even while trips began or ended elsewhere. It is a self-authored account of his working life, not a payroll roster or neutral evaluation of employment conditions. The book's extensive cowboy adventures and racialized depictions elsewhere are not imported into this interpretation. No precise Ogden service start/end years are inferred from the chapter. [4]

A later local voice adds an essential occupational distinction. **Joe L. McQueen**, interviewed by **Sarah Langsdon on July 25, 2013**, describes starting work in Ogden on August 5, 1950 and spending ten years as a **redcap**. He recalls meeting passengers and handling baggage, and explains that tips made the work financially attractive compared with his preceding job. These details come from the published transcript, printed pp.18–19; the interview recording was not listened to. McQueen's terminal work is not relabeled as employment aboard a Pullman sleeper. [5]

The local community connection extends beyond the shift. Weber State's digital exhibition describes the **Porters and Waiters Club** as lodging and a gathering place for Black railroad workers, and identifies **Billy and Anna Belle Weakley** with its management and community support. This is a university interpretation, not Anna Belle's own quoted testimony. It documents why the off-duty part of a railroad town matters. [6]

The original story proposition is a contrast: passenger comfort depended on workers whose own rest, hospitality and social life also needed a place. A depot can be read as a workplace and a community connection rather than merely a display of large machines. Do not infer that Love used the later club or that McQueen's work conditions represent every Pullman employee.

## 3. Then / now
- **1907:** Love's published autobiography records his Ogden-division experience retrospectively. Publication year is not a service date. [4]
- **1912 / 1947:** Weber's interpretation places the club's opening under Billy Weakley and Frank Turner in 1912, and Anna Belle joining management in 1947. Its heading “1947–1959” is not the full lifespan of every earlier rooming-house activity. [6]
- **1950–60:** McQueen's stated redcap employment span, not independently checked against personnel records. [5]
- **1959 / 1969:** the Weber exhibit distinguishes closure of the club from a later restaurant fire. These are not one demolition date. [6]
- **Present:** the city identifies the Utah State Railroad Museum among the museums at Union Station. The collection includes photographs and railroad ephemera, but this does not prove a specific porter object is on view today. [7]

No assertion is made that Love worked inside today's museum building or that a preserved car is one he staffed.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Occupational precision:** McQueen uses porter informally in describing redcaps. This dossier retains his own job title and does not treat that as a Pullman employment record. [5]

- **Transcript inconsistency:** McQueen gives 1950–60 for redcap employment but also discusses a 1962 Cadillac in a related passenger anecdote. The vehicle story is not used to extend his employment dates. [5]
- **Museum hours conflict:** current city information supersedes the older domain's different schedule and prices. Holiday-adjacent and emergency closures remain unverified. [1–3]
