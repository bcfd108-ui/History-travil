## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A famous trading post also depended on somebody knowing where every ordinary thing had gone. One clerk complained that he was expected to know the inventory without ever being given one.

**Named contemporary account actually read:** In his **January 4, 1852** journal entry, **Rudolph Friederich Kurz** describes routine tasks and his frustration with manager **Edwin Denig**. On printed page **258**, he writes: **“When I was given the keys there was no inventory from which I could inform myself”**. The passage describes demands that he account for tools and stores while his interests remained with painting. [5, pp. 257–258] Page 258 was rendered and visually examined; this is not a quotation taken solely from a search snippet.

**Publication discipline:** The consulted English version is **1937**, translated by **Myrtis Jarrell** and edited by **J. N. B. Hewitt**, Smithsonian Bureau of American Ethnology Bulletin **115**. It is a translated period journal, not an English sentence written in 1852. Smithsonian's catalog identifies **NAA.MS2522A** as a German transcript prepared from the Bern original and sent in **1907**. [6] The original handwritten German was not examined; no audio was heard.

**Interpretive synthesis:** Kurz gives a worker's account of conflicting expectations, not an audited employment investigation. His complaint cannot establish that Denig never kept records or that every worker had identical duties. Its value is the specific friction between responsibility and information. A picturesque building becomes a workplace where somebody had to notice, count, remember and answer for things.

**Trade was not solely company work.** NPS describes the Assiniboine as the principal trading partner and separately identifies Apsáalooke/Crow participation, including women's robe-processing skill. [7] This is NPS interpretation, not direct Tribal testimony. The commercial journal does not speak for those producers or define the value of their labor.

**Separate sovereign context:** The Fort Peck Tribes' own history identifies **Assiniboine and Sioux as two distinct nations**, and describes later deprivation following buffalo loss and inadequate rations. [8] That institutional Tribal account is not a firsthand Fort Union ledger, nor does it license treating all trading peoples as one Nation. Its later chronology prevents the post's commerce from becoming a timeless story of uncomplicated mutual prosperity. No Montana community stop is added.

## 3. Then / now
Fort Union operated as a commercial trading establishment in **1828–1867**, not primarily as a military fort. NPS explains that the original post was dismantled and that modern interpretation includes reconstructed portions. [9] The present visitor experience is not an intact 1852 workplace.

A clerk's inventory complaint can illuminate how a room was used, but cannot authenticate every reconstructed shelf, doorway or object. The journal, archaeology, institutional interpretation and reconstructed fabric are different kinds of evidence. Keep their labels visible.

The comparison here is documentary and spatial in a general sense: a period worker's account read against an interpreted commercial setting. No precise journal writing position or camera alignment is claimed. No trade price is converted into an unsupported present purchasing-power claim.

## 4. What's still there
NPS describes reconstructed walls, bastion, Trade House and Bourgeois House. [9] These are interpretive structures, not surviving nineteenth-century timbers. Specific rooms may have their own seasonal admission gates.

The Trade House interpretation distinguishes reception, shop and clerk's office; it connects the latter with inventory, expenses and accounts. [10] That supports reading Kurz's workplace complaint in the public interpretive setting, **if that room is open**, without inventing access to an office or storage space.

The journal is available digitally from the Smithsonian repository. [5] It provides the reliable fallback if an interior is closed. No object from its inventory discussion was identified as currently on display. A reconstructed nail or tool would not become Kurz's personal possession merely because it fits his description.
