## 2. The story
Atlanta's richest story is not simply gold discovered. It is the work required to make remote ore pay. ISHS's mining history describes delayed machinery transport and refractory ores that resisted early processing. Arrastras and stamp mills were attempts to bridge that gap, not interchangeable picturesque props. [6] The district nomination records an 1867 mill, closures in 1869 and renewed investment in 1877. Its interpretation joins distant capital to difficult recovery and transport. [1]

A specific individual and document make the labor tangible. ISHS Reference Series 494 identifies **A. R. Jayne**, a Salt Lake City mechanical engineer, installing Atlanta Miners Company generating machinery in January 1907. It reproduces a **Mountain Home Maverick, January 10, 1907** report: **“its completion within the 60 days called for in the contract was a matter of congratulation.”** That is a newspaper voice preserved in a later transcription, not Jayne speaking. The original newspaper page has not been inspected. [7]

Power linked water to mine lighting, compression, hoisting and milling. The same account distinguishes mine/mill electricity near the end of May 1907 from town electricity on October 1. Its appended notice says the dam and power plant burned June 27, 1983, superseding its earlier description of surviving equipment. No intact 1907 generating plant is promised. [7]

Labor remains unevenly visible: the engineer and company are named; freight handlers, builders and many mine workers are not. The 1937 WPA road photograph below supplies a second primary record of infrastructure work, but its publicity provenance is not a worker's testimony. Do not invent wages, ethnic composition, injuries, union activity or a personal recollection. [8]

## 3. Then / now
Then: movement of machinery, people and supplies was part of production itself. Now: reaching the settlement still requires deliberate logistics, but historical hardship is not a recommendation to attempt a hazardous trip.

The historic inventory is a dated survey, not a present condition report. It records homes, an assay office, garage, cabin and jail, including reused materials and later alterations. [1] The useful comparison is a working community versus a visitor's imagined abandoned camp. An occupied house is not a set, and a ruin is not an invitation.
