## 2. Story
**National Museum of the Mighty Eighth Air Force, Pooler · Research draft · Fred**

The museum's two histories begin in different places. The wartime command took shape in 1942 and large numbers of Americans served through the air war in Europe. The museum today is in Pooler. Its account describes Maj. Gen. Lewis E. Lyle as one of the people who made the case for permanent preservation. He had flown with the Eighth. The museum records that after the war he helped families seeking information about the dead, including finding graves and handling paperwork. Another veteran, Frank D. Murphy, explained the intent to preserve the service “from discredit or oblivion,” as quoted in the operator's account. That is a published recollection of the museum's purpose, distinct from a wartime unit order. [3–5]

The large object in the Combat Gallery has a second biography. The operator says the wartime airplane named City of Savannah was the 5,000th aircraft processed through Hunter in 1944. It describes the Pooler airplane as a B-17G restored in tribute to that namesake. Volunteers began restoration in 2009 and gave many thousands of hours to the work. This is an actual historic aircraft whose modern name memorializes a different original object. The wartime and preservation jobs can be understood together without attaching an unproven aircraft identity to the display. [6–7]

The Library of Congress preserves records of Eighth Air Force service, including a memoir by pilot Lester H. Aufmuth of the 544th Bomb Squadron, and a separate record of Keith McComb's interview. The latter's service record includes Savannah, later European service and multiple units. Neither archival record makes those individuals crew of the City of Savannah. [8–9]

## 3. Then and now
The wartime Savannah connection concerns military formation and aircraft processing. The current publicly visitable subject is the Pooler museum, its exhibit objects and memorial interpretation. The museum aircraft and the original namesake are separate. [1–7]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts
- The original wartime namesake and the restored exhibit cannot share a serial or combat record without specific evidence. The operator identifies the display as a tribute. An independent processing ledger for the 5,000th aircraft was not retrieved. [6–7]
- Air Force 1968 lineage gives 1942 constitution and activation dates, and subsequent changes in organizational name. The current exhibit describes the continuing story of the wartime Eighth. Those are different scopes and the anniversary lineage is cited as such. [3, 5]
- The two LOC collection records were accessible. Full interview audio and a passage of the memoir were not independently transcribed or quoted. Modern gallery images do not establish reuse rights for historical photographs. [6, 8–9]
