# Louisiana State Penitentiary (Angola) — Tunica Hills plantation prison, Red Hat Cell Block, and Point Lookout

**Research draft · Labeled THEME SWEEP (Family 1 — Prisons, penitentiaries, jails) · Angola, West Feliciana Parish, Louisiana · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate

**Louisiana State Penitentiary (Angola)** is an active, working maximum-security state prison at the dead end of 20-mile LA Highway 66 (`17544 Tunica Trace, Angola, LA 70712`). Walk-in visitors are **never** routed onto working prison grounds, residential camps, dormitories, **Red Hat Cell Block**, or the **Point Lookout** prison cemeteries. [1, 2, 3]

The **two lawful public anchors** sit outside the secure maximum-security compounds:

1. **Louisiana Prison Museum & Cultural Center** (`17544 Tunica Trace, Angola, LA 70712`, outside the main front gate at the end of LA Hwy 66): operated by the Angola Museum Foundation, open **Monday–Friday, 8:00 AM–4:30 PM**, closed weekends and Louisiana state holidays. Admission is listed at $5 in secondary tourism directories (confirm current rate and group-tour rules ahead of travel at `(225) 655-2592` or `angolamuseum@angolamuseum.org`). The museum exhibits the **Red Hat Cell Block** history, the **Traveling Electric Chair** (on loan from the Louisiana State Museum system), **Angola Prison Rodeo** history, and inmate arts/music. [1, 2]
2. **Angola Prison Rodeo & Arts and Crafts Festival** (`angolarodeo.com`): public ticketed seasonal events held in the rodeo arena complex outside the prison's residential compounds on **Sundays in October** and a **select April weekend** (check `angolarodeo.com` for exact 2027 spring and fall dates). Posted recent ticketing lists **$25** general rodeo admission (or **$10** Sunday "Hobby Craft Only" entry; arena gates open 9:00 AM, rodeo 2:00 PM). [4]

**Working-grounds boundary:** The Louisiana Department of Public Safety & Corrections states that **Red Hat Cell Block** and the prison grounds are accessible only via **pre-arranged group bus tours** for school, church, or criminal-justice organizations (scheduled in advance through the Warden's Office), which visit the museum, drive past the six main camps without entering dorms, and stop at Red Hat Cell Block. Individual visitors must remain at the front-gate museum and (on rodeo dates) the public rodeo arena. [1, 2]

**Anchor ruling: ANCHORED (Louisiana Prison Museum & Cultural Center outside main gate + public ticketed Angola Prison Rodeo; working prison grounds, Red Hat Cell Block, and Point Lookout cemeteries strictly unrouted for walk-in visitors).**

## 2. The story

The 18,000-acre peninsula bounded on three sides by the Mississippi River and on the east by the Tunica Hills began as a cluster of plantations bought in the 1830s–1840s and named **"Angola"** after the African homeland of people enslaved on the estate. In **1869–1880**, Confederate veteran **Major Samuel Lawrence James** acquired the 8,000-acre Angola tract under Louisiana's post–Civil War **convict-lease system** (`1869–1901`), using leased state prisoners for agricultural and levee labor under conditions notorious for mortality and brutality. [1, 5]

The State of Louisiana ended the lease and resumed direct control of the prison in **1901**, expanding the farm to more than 18,000 acres by 1940. Because state appropriations were minimal, Angola relied from **1915 into the 1970s** on armed **convict guards** ("trusties") to police fellow prisoners in the fields and camp dormitories. [1, 5]

A wave of **113 escapes between 1932 and 1934**—culminating in a **September 1933** break in which two guards/employees were killed—prompted the state to construct Angola's first reinforced-concrete cellblock in **1935**. Built as a single-story narrow wing roughly **18 feet wide by 130–140 feet long** with a square front receiving bay, **Red Hat Cell Block** contained **40 cells** along a central corridor, each fitted with **5-by-7-foot poured concrete bunks** and tiny upper slit windows. Prisoners assigned to hard agricultural labor wore red-painted straw hats, giving the punishment block its name. [5]

In **1951**, conditions in Red Hat and the camps reached national headlines when **31 inmates severed their own Achilles tendons** with razor blades in a coordinated protest against forced labor and solitary confinement. Construction of a modern main complex ("New Angola") replaced Red Hat as the primary cellblock in **1955**, after which Red Hat shifted to temporary punishment and **execution holding**. [1, 5]

From **1941 to 1956**, Louisiana carried out capital punishment by **traveling electric chair**, hauling the oak chair parish-to-parish by truck (first used September 11, 1941, in Livingston Parish). In **1956**, state law moved all executions to Angola, installing the chair permanently in a structure beside Red Hat Cell Block. Following the *Furman v. Georgia* moratorium and a later shift to lethal injection (`1991`), the electric chair was retired and placed on museum loan. **Red Hat Cell Block** was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on **February 7, 2003** (`NRHP 03000014`). [2, 5]

Just east of the main compound on the rising loess ridge of the Tunica Hills lie **Point Lookout I** and **Point Lookout II**, Angola's prison cemeteries for deceased inmates whose bodies went unclaimed by families, marked by rows of uniform white concrete crosses beneath a wooden fence and bluff-top hardwood trees. [1, 6]

### Haunted history / lore

No primary-sourced haunted-place folklore is adopted here. The site's documented human toll—convict leasing, solitary cells at Red Hat, the 1951 self-mutilation protest, capital punishment, and the unclaimed graves at Point Lookout—stands solely on historical and archival records. [1, 5]

## 4. What's still there

- **Public (outside working compounds):** The **Louisiana Prison Museum & Cultural Center** (`17544 Tunica Trace`), housing Red Hat historical interpretation, the retired **Traveling Electric Chair**, rodeo memorabilia, and inmate craft/music history; plus the **Angola Prison Rodeo Arena** on scheduled rodeo weekends. [2, 4]
- **Inside working prison perimeter (UNROUTED — pre-arranged official group tours only):** **Red Hat Cell Block** (`1935`, `NRHP 03000014`), the active residential prison camps, and **Point Lookout I & II** cemeteries on the Tunica Hills ridge. [1, 5, 6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Drive the full length of LA Highway 66** northwest from US-61 across the Tunica Hills bluff line to its dead-end terminus at the Angola front-gate complex (`17544 Tunica Trace`). [1, 2, 7]
2. **Park at the Louisiana Prison Museum & Cultural Center** (Mon–Fri, 8:00 AM–4:30 PM) **outside the main security gate**. Do **not** drive past security checkpoints or onto interior prison roads without prior official authorization. [1, 2]
3. **Tour the museum exhibits** covering convict leasing, Red Hat Cell Block, the 1951 protest, the retired electric chair, and Point Lookout's unclaimed burials. [2, 5]
4. **Optional seasonal extension:** Return on a scheduled October Sunday or April rodeo weekend with an `angolarodeo.com` ticket to enter the public Rodeo Arena and Hobby Craft grounds—keeping strictly inside public event fencing. [4]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Electric chair tenure & execution count:** The 2003 NRHP nomination (`03000014`) states the traveling chair moved permanently to Angola in **1956** (citing 1956 legislation ending parish-by-parish use), while the Angola Museum site describes it as operating at Red Hat beginning in **1957** (`11` executions between 1957 and 1961, plus post-*Gregg* executions 1983–1991 totaling 87 electrocutions statewide in its overall 1941–1991 run). Both 1956 and 1957 dates are printed here; original Louisiana DOC execution logs were not inspected directly. [2, 5]
- **Museum admission fee:** `angolamuseum.org` posts operating hours (`Mon–Fri 8:00 AM–4:30 PM`) and contact details but does not clearly display an online ticket schedule; `$5` admission appears in secondary tourism guides and should be confirmed by phone (`(225) 655-2592`). [2]
- **Point Lookout II & full burial count:** Total interments across Point Lookout I and II were not retrieved from a primary prison burial ledger during this run.
