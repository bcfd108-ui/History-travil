# Old Ursuline Convent — the French colonial convent, school and orphanage on Chartres Street
HT-RES-LA-old-ursuline-convent · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

Twelve religious women crossed the Atlantic to do work a nine-year-old town could not yet do for itself. The Ursulines of Rouen sailed under a contract with the Company of the Indies: in exchange for staffing the New Orleans hospital, they would receive a convent and a school for girls. Mother Marie Tranchepain of St. Augustine, the superior, recorded the party by name and rank in her own account — eight professed nuns drawn from Rouen, Le Havre, Vannes, Ploërmel, Elbeuf and Hennebont, one novice, Marie-Madeleine Hachard, whose letters home became a second primary record of the crossing, and two seculars. The voyage took five months. They founded the monastery at New Orleans on August 7, 1727, nine years after the town itself was founded. [8, 9]

What they built, in sequence, was an institution before it was a building. From temporary quarters they ran the hospital, opened a school for girls — the Ursuline school founded in 1727 is carried forward today by Ursuline Academy — and took in orphans. The first purpose-built convent, designed by the royal engineer Ignace François Broutin in 1727 and under construction by 1732, was a large half-timbered structure, drawn in elevation and plan by André de Batz that year. Its exposed timber walls were already a defect in the humid climate by 1737, and by 1745 the building had deteriorated so badly that Broutin prepared plans for a replacement in brick. That replacement is the building that stands today. [4, 6, 8]

The standing convent is the third building the nuns occupied in the Quarter, and the operator's own history is precise about the sequence: the Kolly House from arrival until 1734, a first convent on the present grounds, closer to the river, from 1734 to 1752/1753, and then this building, authorized by King Louis XV in 1745 and completed in 1752 or 1753. It measures about 160 by 48 feet. Its twelve-bay facade, two floors and three-dormer attic are built in brick-between-post construction covered in white plaster scored to simulate stone at the corners and central bay, with doors and windows set directly opposite one another so the rooms cross-ventilate — a climate answer built into the plan, not decoration. The National Register nomination calls it the only public building still standing in New Orleans known definitely to date from the French regime, and the National Park Service has called it the finest surviving example of French colonial public architecture in the country. The operator and the landmark literature alike describe it as the oldest building in the Mississippi River Valley. It survived the great Quarter fires of 1788 and 1794 that destroyed most of its contemporaries. [1, 2, 4, 6, 7]

The nuns' century in the building was also a history of who was taught, healed and housed there — and on whose labor. The founding contract itself provided that the sisters would receive a plantation worked by enslaved Africans until they were self-supporting, a clause printed in the treaty text reproduced in the Louisiana Anthology; the school and hospital work that made the Ursulines foundational to the city rested, from the first agreement, inside the colony's system of enslavement. That fact belongs in the main story, not in a footnote, and the fuller record of the enslaved people attached to the Ursuline establishment — names, work, families — was not retrieved in this pass and is printed below as a gap. [8]

The nuns left Chartres Street in 1824, moving downriver to a Dauphine Street convent on ground later cut by the Industrial Canal, and the old building began a second life that is almost a syllabus of the city's institutions. It became the residence of the bishops and archbishops of New Orleans until 1899 — Archbishop Antoine Blanc commissioned the adjoining St. Mary's Church, built in 1845 to the design associated with J. N. B. de Pouilly, as the bishops' chapel — and it also served, at different times, as a Catholic boys' school, as the city's first public school, as the archdiocesan central office and archives, as a residence for priests serving the Italian community, and, from 1831 to 1834, as a meeting place of the Louisiana Legislature. Restored in 1973, it now houses the archdiocesan historical archives and a museum, and forms, with St. Louis Cathedral and St. Mary's Church, the Catholic Cultural Center of New Orleans. Its museum rooms carry the Ursuline story itself and rotating exhibits; the newest exhibit named on the operator's homepage this run is "Beyond the Veil." [1, 2, 6]

The modern building has taken damage and repair in public view. In Hurricane Katrina a chimney facing Chartres Street crashed through the convent roof and severed the sprinkler system, the church roof lifted slightly from its walls, roof tiles on both buildings had to be replaced, and the automobile gate on Chartres was torn from its moorings. The complex was closed for sixteen months for repairs and reopened in January 2007. [2]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The Chartres Street block between Ursulines Avenue and Gov. Nicholls Street in the French Quarter, New Orleans, Orleans Parish. The present building stands on the grounds of the 1734 first convent, set back from the river-side position of its predecessor. The National Register file gives the address as 1114 Chartres Street; the operator gives 1112 Chartres Street; older listings give 1100 Chartres Street. All three numbers describe the same walled complex. [1, 3, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Old Ursuline Convent Museum, 1112 Chartres St., New Orleans, LA 70116, 504-529-3040 — staffed museum entry during published hours, plus the adjoining St. Mary's Church at 1116 Chartres St. as an active church. Enter only through the museum's public entry in museum hours; the archives, offices and any closed courtyard or work areas are not visitor ground. [1, 2]

## 5. Public progression

Enter through the museum in its short published window — Tuesday to Saturday, 10 to 3 — and read the building from the street inward. Start on Chartres at the gatehouse and the twelve-bay facade: the plainness is the point, a French provincial public building that outlasted an empire, two fires and a hurricane. Inside, take the Ursuline rooms first, in the order the women lived them — arrival in 1727, hospital, schoolroom, orphanage — and hold the founding contract's enslaved-labor clause in mind while you do, because the institution and that clause were signed together. **Strongest story moment · Five months to August 7, 1727.** A superior who listed her sisters by name and hometown, a novice whose letters home survive, and a town nine years old waiting for a hospital and a girls' school: the convent begins as a passenger list, not a monument. End in St. Mary's Church, quietly and outside Mass unless you are there to worship, in the 1845 chapel the archbishops built beside the nuns' house after the nuns had gone. [1, 2, 8, 9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Completion date.** The operator's history gives completed 1752 or 1753 (and its homepage gives 1752); the National Register nomination gives the new building completed about 1750, with specific dates 1745–1750; the Society of Architectural Historians gives 1749–1752; the landscape record gives built 1745–1750. This dossier prints the operator's 1752/1753 in the story and records the 1750 completion reading here; no source inspected reconciles them. [1, 2, 4, 6, 7]
- **Landmark year.** The National Archives / National Park Service record shows National Historic Landmark designation on October 9, 1960 and National Register listing on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference 66000376). The operator's history page states the building "became a Registered National Historic Landmark" in 1968, and a secondary museum summary states it was designated a landmark in 1966. Both conflict with the federal record; this dossier follows the federal record and prints the conflict. [2, 3, 4]
- **Address.** 1112 Chartres (operator, current), 1114 Chartres (National Register file), and 1100 Chartres (older listings and the sweep lead) are all in print for the same complex. The trip gate uses the operator's current 1112. [1, 3, 4]
- **Hours in third-party listings disagree with the operator** — older guides print Monday–Friday 10–4 and Saturday 9–3, and aggregators print Tuesday–Saturday 10–2. The operator's own site this run prints Tuesday–Saturday 10 a.m.–3 p.m. Follow the operator, and note the operator's homepage also prints a Thanksgiving closure as "Thursday, Nov. 26, and Friday, Nov. 28" — in 2026 Nov. 28 is a Saturday; the day/date pairing on the operator page is internally inconsistent and is flagged rather than corrected here. [1]
- **Party size on arrival.** The superior's own account lists eight professed nuns, one novice and two seculars; the National Park Service event summary describes 12 Ursulines arriving in 1727; other accounts give fourteen participants in the journey with seven names known. The counts count different categories (professed, novices, postulants, seculars) and are printed side by side, not merged. [8, 9]
- **Enslaved people of the Ursuline establishment.** The founding contract's provision of a plantation worked by enslaved Africans is documented in the treaty text. No names, numbers beyond that clause, or household-level record for the Chartres Street convent itself was retrieved in this pass. The gap is printed; nothing is invented to fill it. [8]
- **First pharmacist tradition.** Secondary accounts credit an Ursuline sister, Francis Xavier, as an early — sometimes "first" — pharmacist in the United States, tied to the convent herb garden. No primary record for the superlative was inspected in this pass; it is recorded as tradition, not as established fact.

## 7. Historical photo records
