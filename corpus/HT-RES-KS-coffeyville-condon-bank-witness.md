# Coffeyville Condon Bank — The words that reached the plaza

## 2. The story

**The working plaza.** The 1890 Perkins Block housed a bank, professional offices and other businesses. The property’s National Register form distinguishes the 1890 building from the earlier site history suggested by the date “1871” on the frontage. The plaza mattered on a normal day because people went by storefronts and worked within sight of one another. That regular activity is what the contemporary newspaper made visible when something extraordinary interrupted it on October 5, 1892. [4–6]

**A voice carries.** In the National Park Service’s edited transcription of the October 7 Coffeyville Journal account, local businessman Aleck McKenna recognized someone on the plaza. The paper reports the warning “the bank was being robbed.” This is speech as reported by a local newspaper two days after the event, not a modern reconstruction of his exact position or the words of a recording. The article then follows word spreading across the square. A later National Register nomination for Charles M. Ball’s home identifies Ball as the Condon cashier present that day. The home itself is outside this visit. [4,7,8]

**Who did not return home.** The Historical Society names the four Coffeyville residents killed: George Cubine, Charles Brown, Lucius Baldwin and town marshal Charles T. Connelly. Some records use a shortened form of the marshal’s name. The Journal and later institutional sources also account for four raiders killed and a surviving raider. The historical society says the museum opened in 1963 in memory of residents who died. This stop uses the story of people living and working in the square, and their losses, as its center. [3,7,9,10]

## 3. Then / now

**Historic location.** C. M. Condon & Company Bank in the 1890 Perkins Block. The register records 811 Walnut and the north angle of Union and Walnut. The present occupant publishes 807 S Walnut as its visitor address. Both sources identify the Perkins building, and the visitor address controls the approach. The historical First National Bank and other raid locations remain documentary context. [1,2,5,6]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Public Walnut Street sidewalk at the Perkins Building. Use legal downtown parking and signed crossings for the short approach. The Chamber advertises an ordinary staffed interior tour as a secondary option when open. The Coffeyville Historical Society museum at 814 Walnut is a separate optional interior for historical interpretation during its own posted hours. No private frontage, alley, roadbed or cemetery route is included. [1–3]

The bank moved in 1953. NPS describes an appearance-focused restoration in 1992 and reports the holes visible in post-raid photographs were lost in that restoration. The surviving masonry form and corner position can still speak to the relation between a bank and a town plaza. The altered entrance is a place to compare images, with the actual photographic viewpoint unverified. [4–6,11]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The October 7 Journal account reproduced by NPS estimates about ten minutes for the engagement. The Historical Society’s account calls it a 12-minute battle. These are different estimates. The counts of four citizens and four raiders killed agree in the accounts used. [7,9]
- The old bank register prints 811 Walnut. The Chamber publishes visitor address 807 S Walnut. The 1890 construction chronology comes from the nomination; the date 1871 on the building is explained as a possible earlier ownership or structure date. The record does not settle that interpretation. [1,2,5,6]
- The original October 7 newspaper page was not inspected. The local public library’s 1892 Elliott booklet catalog was read. The booklet scan was not recovered as readable text here. The NPS text is an edited transcription and names McKenna as reported by the Journal. The Ball nomination supplies the cashier name through later oral and documentary sources. [7,8,10]
- Exact step-free thresholds, holiday Chamber schedule, a lawful repeat-camera position and photo rights remain outstanding. Use the resident names and public exterior reflection without an interior promise on a holiday. [1–4,11]
