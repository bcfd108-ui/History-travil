## 2. The story
Ketchum's rail story is not only about the arrival of resort passengers. Sheep had to reach market, and that meant coordinating animals, feed, pens, trucks and trains.

A published primary voice makes the sequence tangible. The Community Library holds **John Breckenridge's oral history, OH-11, 12 and 13**, interviewed by **Miriam Breckenridge**. In the transcript's “Sheep Transportation” passage he recalls Bill Newman trucking market lambs into Ketchum in the early 1930s. He explicitly cannot recall the year. Lambs gathered in the Livestock Association's river pens before shipment. His phrase **“and then load them on the railroad”** describes the connection. [4, PDF pp.5–6]

This is retrospective testimony, not a freight ledger. It supplies a remembered operating sequence without proving annual volumes, truck capacity or a particular railcar schedule. The seemingly new technology of trucking did not immediately eliminate the train; in his account it initially fed it. That is the independent interpretive angle here.

A 1938 stockyard photograph, **F 00001**, supplies a different form of evidence. Sheep fill the foreground; fencing, handling structures, a small roofed building and the mountain backdrop frame the yard. It gives material form to a gathering-and-transfer operation, but cannot by itself date the first shipment or identify every person who worked there. [5]

Original narration: *The journey to market did not start when the train moved. It started with getting many living animals to the same place, in the right condition, at the right time. These pens were part of the timetable.* This is interpretation, not quoted historical speech.

The museum's depot photograph **F 00220** shows an early-1940s welcome crowd associated with Sun Valley tourism. Placed alongside the sheep image, it shows contrasting uses of rail transport without assuming one industry simply vanished when another appeared. [6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Core: one museum visit, approximately 45–75 minutes, an editorial allowance rather than a scheduled tour.** No driving leg is needed to make the interpretive story work.

1. Enter the museum through its designated public entrance during confirmed hours. Begin with the museum's orientation rather than jumping straight to an industrial “before” picture. [1]
2. Read the short Breckenridge excerpt on your own device, or consult related material if actually available. Identify its uncertainty about the early-1930s date. A recollection can be valuable without being exact. [4]
3. Compare the stockyard photograph to the depot crowd photograph through the source links. Distinguish visible evidence from captions and inference. Neither photo is guaranteed on display. [5,6]
4. **Strongest story moment:** return to Breckenridge's truck-to-pen-to-rail sequence. Ask what had to happen before the visible train journey. End with the people and coordination behind shipping, rather than an unsupported “second largest in the world” superlative.

**Optional research extension:** the separate reading room, using ordinary public streets and its own hours. Walking distance, safe crossing sequence and snow clearance are unmeasured; no mapped route is released. [8]

**Optional future outdoor extension:** a short Wood River Trail segment only after an exact, permanent public access point and present condition are checked. No historic depot pin, yard boundary or temporary event access substitutes for that gate. Summer improves neither ownership certainty nor archival-photo alignment. November's earlier darkness and possible ice make an indoor-first version useful, but no specific weather observation is claimed.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The oral-history cover says **1979**, while its introduction says **March 4, 1978**. Preserve both; do not assign a single interview date without the repository's clarification. The transcript was edited by multiple named people and is not an unmediated audio recording. Original audio was not listened to. [4]

Breckenridge's account describes different multi-day hauling durations within the same passage and tentative capacities. These are recollections, not engineering specifications. His statement about a livestock-rest regulation is not adopted as a verified legal-history claim.

A museum essay gives **1976** for the last passenger train and **1987** for track removal. A City packet's historical land-use summary instead broadly says service ended about **1965** and abandonment in the early 1980s. These can refer to different service categories or contain errors. Without operating timetables and abandonment records, no exact final-service chronology is asserted. [6,10]

Repeated claims that Ketchum ranked second worldwide in sheep shipping were found, but the underlying comparative freight dataset was not. The dossier does not repeat the ranking as fact. Annual stockyard volumes, shipment destinations by year, labor rosters and site parcel boundaries remain open. No present livestock operation is treated as an attraction. No cemetery, tree carving or culturally sensitive site is a hunt target.
