# Auburn and Duncan Park — a builder’s letter and the house it describes

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP02 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Use the public Duncan Avenue approach to Duncan Park at Auburn, 400 Duncan Avenue. The National Park Service identifies the house on its public driving tour. Treat this as an exterior stop. The city’s management agreement puts tour hours in the Historic Natchez Foundation’s hands. A year-round listing supplies no dated public departure verified for this visit. Do not count on seeing the stairs inside, or enter the museum grounds or outbuildings on the strength of a park visit. Weather and temporary park closures govern the outdoor stop. [1–3]

## 2. Story

On September 27, 1812, builder Levi Weeks wrote from Natchez to Epaphras Hoyt about the brick house he was building for Lyman Harding. Weeks called the stair a “geometrical staircase,” a phrase preserved in the published transcription. He was explaining how a new house in the Mississippi Territory would look to a correspondent who could not see it. The letter survives in the Mississippi archives as part of a twenty-page account. It gives a builder’s explanation of his design and establishes that Auburn was already under construction in 1812. [4–5]

Stephen and Catherine Duncan bought Auburn in 1827. Wealth from cotton plantations worked by enslaved people supported the Duncan household. A two-story service building put the kitchen below living quarters for enslaved workers. Surviving Duncan family papers include an 1858 provisioning list and an 1860 clothing memorandum. The archive cannot place the earlier list on a specific Duncan plantation. It documents how the family organized forced labor across its properties; it cannot give an individual Auburn worker a name. [5–6]

## 3. Then and now

Later work enlarged Weeks’s design. About 1840 the Duncans added wings and a rear colonnade. In 1911 their heirs conveyed the property to the city for public park use. The April 1936 survey photograph already shows a central front with flanking wings. It lets a traveler distinguish what is visible from the avenue from what can be learned only from interior records. [5,7–8]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Public exterior — the house seen from the Duncan Avenue stop identified by NPS. Documentary interior — the curving stair described by Weeks. Other surviving fabric identified by architectural historians includes kitchen/quarters, billiard hall, dairy and carriage building. No separate stop or interior access is established for these structures. [1–2,5]

## 5. Public progression

From the public approach, identify the tall central portico and the lower wings. Use the survey photograph to read the broad front before turning to Weeks’s letter. **Strongest story moment:** the builder described the stair before the Duncans owned the house or added its wings. A traveler can place his words in the older central block without crossing the threshold. [1,4–5,7–8]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The National Trust advertises year-round tours. The city’s signed management framework gives the foundation control of hours and requires public access during pilgrimage seasons. Neither supplies a day-specific museum opening for this visit. A letter quotation was checked in published transcription against MDAH’s description; the manuscript page was not inspected. The family’s plantation records do not establish which individual worked at Auburn. [2–6]
