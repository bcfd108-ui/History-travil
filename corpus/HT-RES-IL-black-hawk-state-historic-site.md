# Black Hawk State Historic Site — Saukenuk, the CCC lodge, and the bluff above the Rock River
HT-RES-IL-black-hawk-state-historic-site · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The state site's own account begins long before any park. Native people occupied this bluff country from about 12,000 years ago in the state tourism telling, and for nearly a century beginning about 1730 the Sauk and Meskwaki (Fox) made their home here. The Illinois Department of Natural Resources page states the central fact plainly: the two cultures thrived in the region from their arrival in 1735 to their forced removal in 1832, and the Sauk city of Saukenuk, in present-day Rock Island, was the largest city in all of Illinois in that period, with a population of over 5,000. The Hauberg Museum inside the lodge is where the site tells that story — through interpretive maps, artifacts and seasonal dioramas, a real Sauk dugout canoe found at the mouth of the Rock River, a recreated full-sized winter home (wickiup), and a smaller representation of a summer home (long house). Population figures for Saukenuk vary across the sources checked this run — over 5,000 on the state page, an estimated 4,800 in and around the village by 1826 in the wider reference record, and 6,000 to 7,000 in the state tourism telling — and the dossier prints that variance in Gaps rather than choosing one number. What does not vary is the scale: this was a major Indigenous city, not a camp, at the meeting of the Rock and Mississippi rivers. [1, 3, 6]

Black Hawk — Ma-ka-tai-me-she-kia-kiak — was born at Saukenuk about 1767, on the University of Illinois American Indian project's account, which draws on his 1833 autobiography as perhaps the only reliable source for his early life. The same university account states the legal wound at the center of the story without dressing it: as a result of a very questionable treaty signed by a few Sauk and Meskwaki leaders in 1804, the United States claimed ownership of all Sauk territory east of the Mississippi, including the main village at Saukenuk; Black Hawk considered the 1804 treaty invalid, and the tense relations on Illinois' northwest frontier erupted into open hostilities in 1832. The state pages carry the same sequence in shorter form — arrival in 1735, forced removal in 1832 — and the Apple River Fort dossier in this same seeding run (HT-RES-IL-apple-river-fort) carries the war itself. This dossier does not re-fight it. The point of this ground is what stood here before the war and what the war ended: a people's principal city, in their own homeland, emptied. When Black Hawk returned to Rock Island in 1833, on the university account, Saukenuk had been settled by Americans in the meantime, its dwellings in part occupied by the newcomers, and it was then that he asked for help recording the story of his life — the autobiography that remains a primary voice for this ground. [1, 6]

The ground's next life was an amusement park, and the record keeps it because the park is why the bluff survived to become a historic site. In 1882 the site became the Watch Tower Amusement Park — concerts, operas, fireworks, bowling, outdoor movies and rides, a figure-eight roller coaster, and the "Shoot the Chutes" toboggan slide invented in Rock Island, whose flatboats slid down the bluff on a greased track at speeds the state tourism page gives as up to 80 miles per hour before bouncing across the Rock River. The National Register nomination adds the commercial frame: the Tri-Cities Railways Company developed the site at the terminal of a city streetcar system, with the Watch Tower restaurant named for the vantage point from which, by the tradition the nomination records, Black Hawk surveyed his people's ancestral home. In 1927 the Illinois legislature appropriated funds to purchase Watch Tower Park, the amusement park was demolished, and the ground became Black Hawk State Park — one of three parks acquired in 1927 under the state's 1925 park statute, on the nomination's account. [3, 5]

The lodge that anchors the visit today is Depression work. The state site records that the Civilian Conservation Corps stationed Company 1674 at what was then Black Hawk State Park, and that the camp created the trails, built two-thirds of Watch Tower Lodge, planted thousands of trees, and did much more; an exhibit at the lodge's east end covers the CCC's creation of the park. The state tourism account fills in the men's terms: more than 200 veterans of World War I recruited between 1933 and 1935, working for clothing, room, board, food and a dollar a day, building six miles of hiking trails, trail structures, parking lots and picnic shelters, and planting thousands of trees and wildflowers. The lodge itself — a one-story limestone structure with rough-hewn timber, designed under state architect Joseph F. Booten — was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on March 4, 1985, as the Black Hawk Museum and Lodge, under the Illinois State Parks Lodges and Cabins thematic nomination, significant for architecture in the period 1925–1949. The nomination describes a limestone complex atop the bluff overlooking the Rock River in three connected elements — museum, lounge and dining room joined by an enclosed gallery — with a statue of Black Hawk on the terrace, and it records Booten's own design note: working on the Fort de Chartres restoration at the same time, the designers thought stone, heavy timbers and iron appropriate to both. Two murals painted in 1936 by Works Progress Administration artist Otto Hake, depicting the seasonal activities of the Sauk and Fox people, hang in the lodge. In 1979 the state changed the designation from Park to Historic Site in recognition of the Native presence at Rock Island — a second source in this run gives 1987 for the historic-site designation, printed as a conflict in Gaps — and in 1984 the north section was declared a nature preserve. [1, 3, 4, 5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Saukenuk stood at the foot of the Rock River rapids, on the point of land between the Rock and Mississippi rivers, in present-day Rock Island. The modern state site — 213 acres on the state page, 208 acres in the state tourism telling, printed as a variance in Gaps — occupies the bluff and much of the historic village ground east of the village's southern portion, part of which now lies under the last blocks of South 11th Street (U.S. Route 67) in the wider reference record. The dossier routes the visit to the state site only; it does not route any visit onto 11th Street village ground, which is ordinary city street and private frontage. [1, 3, 6]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Black Hawk State Historic Site, 1510 46th Ave., Rock Island, IL 61201 — a staffed state historic site (IDNR). Enter on 46th Avenue, park in the site's paved lots, and begin at Watch Tower Lodge (the Hauberg Museum is in its west end; the CCC exhibit is at its east end). Grounds and trails are open year-round sunrise to 10 p.m. on the site's own description; the museum keeps the shorter Wednesday–Sunday split hours in the gate. Stay on designated trails — the site rules require it — keep pets leashed, and keep vehicles on paved roadways and lots. No camping and no ground fires; fires only in designated areas. [1, 2]

## 5. Public progression

Start at Watch Tower Lodge, in museum hours if the museum is the purpose of the trip. Take the Hauberg Museum first and take it slowly: the seasonal dioramas, the maps, and the dugout canoe are the site's own ordering of the story — a working city and a working river culture, season by season, before any soldier or settler enters it. Read the wickiup and the long house as what they are, recreations made for teaching, and the canoe as what it is, a real Sauk artifact recovered at the river's mouth. Then cross the lodge to the CCC exhibit at the east end and change centuries deliberately: the men of Company 1674, veterans of one war working for a dollar a day in the Depression, built two-thirds of the building you are standing in and cut the trails you are about to walk, on ground from which the people in the west-end exhibits had been removed a century before. The lodge holds both facts without reconciling them, and the visit should too. Take the terrace and the bluff view — the Watch Tower vantage the amusement-park restaurant took its name from — then walk a trail down toward the Rock River, through the nature preserve's oak-hickory forest and the prairie restoration. **Strongest story moment · The largest city in Illinois.** On the state's own telling, the city that stood here held more than 5,000 people and was the largest in Illinois — and its ending was not decline but a disputed 1804 signature and a forced removal in 1832, after which the village's own houses were occupied by the people who replaced it. Stand on the bluff, read the ground as a city site first and a park second, and leave the scoring out of it. Outside grounds hours there is no lawful visit here beyond the public street. [1, 3, 6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Museum-hours conflict, printed in full and not merged: the site's current description gives the Hauberg Museum as open Wednesday–Sunday 9:00 a.m.–12:00 p.m. and 1:00 p.m.–4:00 p.m., and the site's lodge notice gives regular lodge hours Wednesday–Sunday 9 a.m.–4 p.m. An older Quad Cities tourism listing gives museum hours March–October 9–noon and 1–5, November–February 9–noon and 1–4, closed Monday and Tuesday and holidays. The gate uses the current site description (split day ending at 4) and directs a phone confirmation; the seasonal 5 p.m. close is not relied on. [2, 3]
- Acreage variance: the state site page gives 213 acres; the state tourism page gives a 208-acre tract, and older listings give 208 acres of public use areas. Both stand as printed; no survey was retrieved this run. [1, 3]
- Trail-length variance: the state site page gives over seven miles of hiking trails; the state tourism page credits the CCC with building six miles of trails. Seven-plus today against six built in the 1930s is consistent with later additions, but no source checked this run states that; both figures stand. [1, 3]
- Saukenuk population variance, printed in full: over 5,000 (state site page, "largest city in all of Illinois"); an estimated 4,800 in and around Saukenuk by 1826 (wider reference record); 6,000 to 7,000, "largest Native American settlement in North America" (state tourism page). The dossier carries the state page's figure in the Story with the variance here; no contemporary census was retrieved this run — Thomas Forsyth's 1824 agent's report is named in secondary accounts but was not itself read this run. [1, 3]
- Historic-site designation-date conflict: the state tourism page states the designation changed from Park to Historic Site in 1979; a second account in this run gives 1987. Both printed; the IDNR page checked this run does not date the change. The dossier asserts neither year. [3]
- Sauk and Meskwaki arrival-date framing: the state page dates the cultures' thriving in the region "from the time of their arrival in 1735 to their forced removal in 1832"; the tourism page says the tribes made their home at the site for nearly a century beginning about 1730, with occupation of the area by Native people from some 12,000 years ago. The dossier follows the state page's 1735 and prints the about-1730 framing here. [1, 3]
- Village-footprint precision: the wider reference record places part of Saukenuk under the last blocks of South 11th Street, with the state site and museum slightly east. No archaeological boundary map was retrieved this run; the dossier routes no visit to the street ground and asserts no exact village boundary. Burial ground is not discussed, located or routed anywhere in this dossier; any burial features on this ground are outside the visit, full stop.
- Admission: no page checked this run states an admission charge or states that admission is free. The field stays blank rather than assumed.
- The 1804 treaty dispute is carried on the University of Illinois project's characterization ("a very questionable treaty signed by a few leaders"; Black Hawk considered it invalid). The treaty text and the delegation-authority record were not themselves read this run and are left as a stated gap, consistent with the Apple River Fort dossier's treatment.
