## 2. The story

**Named firsthand anchor: Eloisa Osuna**, a former San Diego cannery worker interviewed for Leonel Sanchez’s September 26, 2009 Union-Tribune report. The original newspaper URL failed, but a reproduction in a city-hosted historical submission was downloaded and its article text read. Osuna said she had worked for 35 years; the reporter places her start in the 1940s, reports her mother also worked cleaning tuna, and describes her earnings helping support nine children while her husband worked elsewhere. Her recollection connects paid work to friendship: **“We used to have picnics after work with our families.”** This is a journalist-selected firsthand quotation, not a complete recorded oral history. [4]

The Chicano Park Museum’s community-history page explicitly incorporates this account. It also preserves a son’s recollection of his father’s cannery employment, but the spelling of those names and original interview provenance require checking before using that second family as a principal character. Do not treat the museum’s link labeled “Full Interview” as proof that the linked newspaper feature is a full transcript. [3]

**Community testimony with a different perspective:** the city’s four-page *Barrio Logan Historical Resources Survey—Interview Summaries* records Norene and Natalia Riveroll remembering the harm caused by cannery closures. Evelyn Mitchell recalls women’s laundry/cannery employment and a neighborhood nursery. These are edited interview summaries, not independently checked verbatim transcripts. They establish community memory of care and job loss, not proof that Osuna used that nursery. Interview dates and recordings were not identified in the document. [5]

**Original interpretive voice:** A can of tuna fits on a shelf. The work behind it does not: a wage carried home, relationships that survived a shift, children whose lives depended on an industrial paycheck. Read pride without making it a claim that every worker was content. Read loss without reducing the neighborhood to a vanished industry. The strongest question is not how a factory worked, but what its work made possible for a family, and what disappeared when the pay stopped.
