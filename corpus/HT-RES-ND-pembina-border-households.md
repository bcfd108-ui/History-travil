## 2. The story
**Start with the Nation's own framing:** the Manitoba Métis Federation's May 26, 2022 presidential message explains that “Red River Métis” names a history and collective identity rather than simply a present location. This is a named government's published self-description, not a blanket determination of every Pembina family's affiliation or present jurisdiction. Métis and Ojibwe identities must not be treated as interchangeable. [2]

**Named household:** Ruth Swan and Edward A. Jerome's *A Mother and Father of Pembina* reconstructs **Betsy Henry and Jean Baptiste Collin** through documentary records. Their paper identifies Betsy's mother as an Ojibwe woman and the later Collin household as Métis. It places the family in Pembina's 1850 and 1860 censuses. These are authors' reconstructions, not an interview with Betsy. [3]

**Actually read primary excerpt, through a scholarly reproduction:** the paper quotes the mother's name from Betsy's scrip affidavit as **“Madeline Sauteaux”**. Its footnote specifies Canadian Department of Interior affidavits, **RG15, microfilm C-14928**, indexed under Betsy Henry, not Collin. The printed excerpt and reference were read; the original affidavit image was not. The same paper reproduces portions of Alexander Henry's will. This reveals how a woman's name can survive in a daughter's formal statement even when a trader's narrative offers little household detail. No missing first-person speech has been invented. [3]

**Original interpretation:** A border map sorts places into jurisdictions. A household record sorts lives into names and relationships. Neither document is neutral or complete. The useful experience is to hold both limits in view: a government category cannot tell the entire story of a family, while kinship alone does not authorize an outsider to decide a person's citizenship or identity. Pembina is a place to learn how such records were made, not to chase descendants or locate a private home.

## 3. Then / now
The household emerges from a mixture of affidavits, wills and census reconstruction. Those document types answer different administrative questions and do not necessarily capture how a family described itself in ordinary life. [3]

Today the museum offers public regional interpretation, while the MMF speaks for its own contemporary national identity. A North Dakota museum is not a substitute for Métis self-representation, and the MMF message is not an entrance permit or border-crossing rule. [1,2]

Keep historic jurisdictions in the period concerned. Present-day Pembina is in North Dakota; that modern label should not be projected backward as if the current state existed throughout the household's history. No Canadian destination is activated.
