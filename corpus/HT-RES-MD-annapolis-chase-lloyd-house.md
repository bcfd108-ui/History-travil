# HT-RES-MD-annapolis-chase-lloyd-house — Annapolis: the mansion a signer began

Edward Lloyd IV had William Buckland complete it by about 1774, and Buckland’s carved woodwork made it one of the finest Georgian town houses in the colonies. 22 Maryland Avenue, Annapolis, Anne Arundel County, Maryland

**Public access:** The house stands in the Annapolis Historic District. Exterior views are available from the public sidewalk. Interior access is typically limited; the property has long served as a residence for elderly women under Episcopal Church auspices and is not a conventional open museum. Confirm any tour or viewing arrangements with the current stewards or Historic Annapolis before visiting. Combine with a walk of the colonial capital.

## The story

Construction of this three-story brick Georgian mansion began in 1769 for Samuel Chase, a young Annapolis lawyer who later signed the Declaration of Independence and served as an Associate Justice of the U.S. Supreme Court. Chase sold the unfinished house in 1771 to Edward Lloyd IV, a wealthy Maryland planter and politician. Lloyd engaged the English-trained architect and master builder William Buckland (with assistance from William Noke) to complete the work by about 1774. The result is one of the earliest full three-story brick Georgian town houses in the colonies, distinguished by its Flemish-bond brickwork, belt courses, projecting central pavilion, and especially Buckland's carved woodwork, cornices, doorways, and interior detailing. The center hall features a grand stair with scrolled soffits.

The house remained in the Lloyd family until the mid-19th century, then passed to Chase relatives. In the late 19th century it was bequeathed to St. Anne's Episcopal Church as a home for elderly women, a use that continued for decades. It is a National Historic Landmark and a key element of the Annapolis Historic District.

## What's still there

The three-story brick house with its hip roof, central pavilion, and classical detailing stands on Maryland Avenue. Significant interior woodwork attributed to Buckland survives. The surrounding colonial streetscape of Annapolis remains.

## The stop, step by step

Arrive in historic Annapolis and walk Maryland Avenue. View the front elevation and massing from the sidewalk. Note the brickwork and pavilion. If special access is arranged, enter to see the center hall and stair. Continue on a walking tour of the Annapolis Historic District, including the State House and other 18th-century buildings. Return via Maryland Avenue or nearby streets.

Exact completion date and the precise division of labor between Buckland and Noke are not fully resolved in the sources examined. Primary construction accounts and early ownership deeds were not consulted for this entry. Current interior access arrangements vary.
