## 2. Story

There are two histories inside the name Rosemont. One is the family’s early settlement near Woodville. The other is the surviving house. Samuel and Jane Davis came to Wilkinson County in the early nineteenth century. The Mississippi Department of Archives and History records an 1817 patent application by Samuel Davis for the tract that includes the later house site. The earlier family home was called Poplar Grove. This establishes a family presence and a tract of land. It leaves open which house stood there when Jefferson Davis was a boy. [3–4]

The 1974 nomination, prepared by architectural historian Elizabeth Reynolds, records that the first known appearance of the name Rosemont was in an “1847 family letter.” It also cites an 1822 conveyance from Samuel to Joseph Davis. Jane lived at Poplar Grove until her death in 1845, the nomination says. Lucinda Davis Stamps, Jefferson’s sister, and her husband William enter the paper trail as later holders. Read those transactions beside the fabric. The family name might have moved from one house to another on the same tract. The 1847 letter is identified through the nomination. Its original has yet to be inspected. [3–4]

The house surveys give the workers’ hands a presence even where the workers’ names are missing. Reynolds recorded original carpenter locks, fittings and two generous front rooms. Fragments found under the house suggested a matching mantel to the one in the south front room. Later evidence gathered by Mimi Miller puts the doors, mantels, proportions and continuous roof slope in the 1830s or 1840s. The nomination and subsequent analysis do not supply the hands behind that joinery, or a household roll from which an individual enslaved worker can securely be assigned to this site. Household names remain an archival task. [3–4]

## 3. Then and now

The historical property was a Davis family plantation. An earlier Poplar Grove building and the surviving Rosemont house may have been separate structures. The nominated house is a one-and-a-half-story building with a gallery under the roof slope. Reynolds documented the house during preservation work. The current operator advertises exhibits by scheduled tour and presents the relationship of the property to the Davis family as its principal narrative. [1,3–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The 1974 nomination associates the house with Jefferson Davis’s childhood and assigns an early date. The state inventory retains 1810 in its date field while printing Mimi Miller’s later assessment that the current house was probably built by Lucinda and William Stamps decades later. Rice’s papers project puts the move to Woodville at about 1810 and calls Rosemont the boyhood home. Miller cites an 1811 arrival tradition and the 1817 application. The operator dates the family acquisition to 1810. These sources answer different parts of the settlement and construction question. The exact first house, its builder, the original 1847 letter and a full labor record remain open. [1,3–6]

Mississippi’s listing prints a west highway reference while the operator and the federal listing put the property east of Woodville. The older corridor plan printed ordinary seasonal access. The current operator directs readers to arrange an appointment, and current state tourism explicitly says appointment only. The site gets no visitor pin. [1–2,5,7]
