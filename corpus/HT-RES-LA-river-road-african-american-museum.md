## 2. Story

The July 31, 1880 Donaldsonville Chief prints a short line in its Ascension Parish official directory: “Representative — Pierre Landry.” The text is the paper’s listing as preserved in the Library of Congress page extraction. The line is useful evidence of Landry’s place in local public life. It cannot give his whole term or tell the reader that the paper revised the roster promptly. In a town where the public record had once treated him as property, his appearance in a list of officeholders belongs beside what he had built after slavery. [3–5]

The Louisiana Historical Association identifies Landry as born enslaved in Ascension Parish in 1841. A local historian working from Landry’s unpublished autobiography describes his early care and schooling with free people of color and his sale after the Prevost estate was settled. In that published summary, his schooling gave him knowledge that he later put to use organizing education for others. After emancipation he moved to Donaldsonville. The accounts describe schools, commerce, public office and long service as a minister. They also report that he was elected mayor in 1868. The claim is presented as the historians’ reconstruction, pending an original municipal election return and Landry’s full written account. [4–5]

The museum opened in 1994 to tell Black history in the River Parishes from the perspective of the people who lived it. Its present focus reaches beyond enslavement into education, culture, entrepreneurship and civic life. The Census Bureau’s museum profile uses Landry to illustrate the history of the region’s Black residents and the role of census research in rebuilding family histories. A 2026 reopening report describes the main Charles Street building’s return after storm repairs. A visitor should understand what the museum itself adds: a setting where a newspaper job title can become a question about the work that made office possible. [1–2, 6–9]

## 3. Then and now

Then — Landry lived through enslavement, emancipation, Reconstruction and the erosion of Black political power. The newspaper and his reported autobiography show different evidence. One names an officeholder. The other, through a published summary, points to a person learning and organizing in his community. [3–6]

Now — The museum’s Charles Street gallery is the public anchor. The operator describes a multi-building campus including a historic school project. Treat the school, grounds and gallery as distinct spaces with separate access set by the museum on the day. This draft does not identify the museum as Landry’s residence. [1–2, 7–9]
