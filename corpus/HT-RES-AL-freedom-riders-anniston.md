## 2. Story
**Freedom Riders National Monument, Anniston Greyhound Depot · Research draft · Fred**

Bruce Boynton's interstate bus trip had taken him to a Richmond terminal restaurant where he was convicted of trespass after refusing to leave a section reserved for white patrons. In December 1960 the U.S. Supreme Court reversed under the Interstate Commerce Act. That statutory ground mattered. Federal protection for an interstate passenger was about what could happen between boarding and arrival, including a service operating as part of the passenger's journey. The Congress of Racial Equality drew on earlier organizing and sent two buses south from Washington in May 1961 to test everyday compliance. [4–5]

On Mother's Day, May 14, the Greyhound group reached downtown Anniston. Moses Newson, a Black reporter traveling with the riders, remembered “the station that actually was closed.” In the American Archive of Public Broadcasting transcript of his 2009 interview, he describes observing police who did not intervene while the mob attacked the bus. Tires were damaged, and the vehicle was attacked again and burned outside town. Newson remembered watching the windows and trying to comfort another passenger. His account then follows the people trying to breathe after leaving the bus. The city distinguishes the downtown Greyhound encounter from the Trailways station and the later attack on the other bus. [5–8]

Rider Genevieve Hughes Houghton's transcript contributes a different window on the same emergency. She recalls leaving through a window, dropping the book she had been reading and a woman offering a place to wash. The two witnesses' accounts show different parts of the scene, especially where people went as they got off. Their recollections can be heard alongside the site where the first encounter occurred, without traveling to the separate place where the bus burned. [5–9]

## 3. Then and now
The former commercial depot survives. NPS describes traces of the earlier layout in the current interior, restoration work and an outdoor mural accompanied by interpretation. The outside audio exhibit presents rider Hank Thomas's testimony. It is a recorded account, heard from the historic depot boundary. [1–3]
