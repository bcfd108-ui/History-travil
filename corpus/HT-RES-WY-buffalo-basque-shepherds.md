## 2. The story

A sheep wagon can look picturesque from a museum aisle. The harder question is what it meant to work beyond ordinary conversation, far from home.

In Jennifer Burden's article reprinted in the **2011 NABO Buffalo festival program**, **Domingo Martirena** recalls helping establish a Basque radio hour for local herders: **“They had radios, and we wanted to talk to them.”** The program carried music and community news, reaching workers for whom English was not yet familiar. **Jean Cinquambre**, a longtime announcer, recalled preparing the broadcast before its Sunday airing. This is published participant testimony, not an original interview recording heard here. [3]

A family-published account in Wyoming's **2011 Centennial Farm and Ranch Yearbook** supplies a different scale of work. The **Kuhn family's account** identifies **Santiago Michelena**, arriving in Buffalo on **May 16, 1917**, working as a sheepherder and camp tender, then entering a partnership and purchasing a sheep wagon in 1923. It also identifies **Ethel Lorah Michelena** in the family's later ranch enterprise. This is attributed family history, not a payroll ledger or a universal path from employee to proprietor. [4]

Interpretive synthesis: wage work, tending camps, household enterprise and communication all belong in a sheep-labor story. The radio was not a quaint accessory. It was a connection between isolated work and a social world.

## 3. Then / now

**Alberta Escoz's community history**, in the same NABO program, dates the local Basque radio program **1956–1996** and describes the organizing that led to a local Basque club. The history makes continuity something people worked at, not an automatic inheritance. [3]

Do not equate Basque identity with a single nationality or occupation. The sources concern a particular Wyoming community; they do not establish that every Basque immigrant herded sheep or that all herders had the same language, contract or experience.

The present anchor is a regional museum rather than an operating sheep camp. The Wyoming Roving Archivist directory expressly lists **Basque culture and history** among its galleries. This supports the thematic fit, but not the live presence of a specific radio, wagon, photograph or label on a particular visit. [5]

The institution says it is county-supported and governed by a county-appointed board. It is not presented here as a Basque-owned cultural center or as permission to enter community spaces. [6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Begin with a real public door.** Arrive only during a verified open window and use the operator's public entrance. In closed periods, make this archive-only rather than adding an improvised outdoor stop.
2. **Read the objects as work.** In any currently accessible Basque interpretation, examine what the labels actually identify. If a wagon or work object is absent, say so; do not pretend the visitor has seen it.
3. **Change scale from livelihood to connection.** Introduce Michelena's attributed work history, then ask what a job far from town required beyond equipment. Avoid presenting successful ownership as the inevitable outcome for every worker.
4. **End with the radio voice.** Read Martirena's short statement in a quiet permitted space. The strongest story moment is a familiar language arriving where companionship was scarce. A visitor does not need to trespass into a mountain camp to understand why that mattered.
