# HT-RES-RI-nki-gilbert-stuart — Saunderstown: the snuff-mill house that produced Washington's portraitist
Division RI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Gilbert Stuart was born on December 3, 1755, in a gambrel-roofed house above a working snuff mill in Saunderstown, a village of North Kingstown, Rhode Island. His father ground tobacco into snuff on the ground floor, powered by the stream outside; the family lived in the stories above. The boy spent only his first six years there, but he grew up to paint nearly 1,000 portraits — including the unfinished 1796 head of George Washington that he kept as the model for the copies Washington's admirers commissioned, and that still looks out from the United States one-dollar bill. The house stands restored on its millpond, with a working snuff mill in the ground floor and a working 1757 gristmill across the stream. It is a seasonal museum: it closed for the winter on October 26, 2026, and this entry promises no off-season access.

## 2. The story
Gilbert Stuart — born Stewart — was the third child of Gilbert Stuart Sr., a Scottish immigrant working in the snuff-making trade, and Elizabeth Anthony Stuart, of a prominent land-owning family from Middletown, Rhode Island. He was baptized at Old Narragansett Church on April 11, 1756.

The house was built by 1751 at the junction of the Mattatuxet stream and the Pettaquamscott tidal river, beside a waterfall that drove a waterwheel on its east side. In 1751, on the ground floor, the elder Stuart began the snuff business: a water-powered mill grinding dried tobacco leaves into the fine powder widely used in the colonial era. The National Park Service nomination calls it "possibly the first snuff mill in America"; the museum tradition states it plainly as the first such mill in America. The venture was not a success. In 1761, when Gilbert was six, his father sold his share and moved the family to Newport to pursue work in the merchant field. Young Gilbert had spent his first six years within earshot of the mill — the working waterwheel, the grindstones and the stream outside the door were the soundscape of his early childhood.

Stuart became the defining portraitist of the early republic. Of his nearly 1,000 portraits, the most famous is the unfinished head of George Washington painted in 1796, known as the Athenaeum Portrait. Commissioned by Martha Washington, it was Stuart's favorite image of Washington; he purposely left the original unfinished so he could use it as the model for the numerous copies — sold at $100 each — that admirers commissioned for decades. After his death, his daughter Jane sold the original to the Boston Athenaeum in 1831; in 1980 the Smithsonian's National Portrait Gallery and the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston jointly purchased it. Washington's likeness from this portrait is the image on the one-dollar bill. Stuart also painted the full-length Lansdowne Portrait of Washington in 1796, and his sitters included the first six U.S. presidents.

Back in Saunderstown, the house passed into private residence use and the original snuff mill and water wheel were lost. In 1930 the building was restored to its colonial state by architect Norman Isham, and it opened to the public as a museum in 1931. Much of the original fabric survived — original wooden walls and beams, and the four original corner fireplaces, one in each room. During the restoration, an English snuff mill built in the early 1730s was transported to the property and installed, faithful in design to the original, and a new water wheel was built so the mill could again run by water power from the pond. The site was designated a National Historic Landmark on December 21, 1965, and listed on the National Register in 1966.

## 3. Then / now
In 1755 the property was a working industrial homestead: house, snuff mill under the family's rooms, gristmill across the stream, pond and falls. Today the same elements stand in the same arrangement, restored and running — the house furnished in period colonial pieces, the snuff mill working in its ground floor, the 1757 gristmill operational across the stream since its waterwheel was reconnected to the gears in 2007. The 23 acres around them carry nature trails past the millpond, stone walls, historic cemeteries, marsh overlooks and a view of the 57-acre Carr Pond, and a fish ladder built in the 1960s still passes river herring over the dam each spring. The difference a visitor should hold in mind: the machinery that works today is faithful, not original — the snuff mill is an early-1730s English mill installed in 1930, and the furnishings, though original colonial pieces, include nothing that belonged to the house or the family.

## 4. What's still there
- The birthplace house (built by 1751) — gambrel-roofed and clapboarded, restored and furnished with period colonial furnishings; no piece of furniture is original to the house. Rooms display reproductions of Stuart's best-known works, including the Athenaeum Portrait, The Skater and John Jay. Its original four corner fireplaces survive.
- The snuff mill — an operational water-powered snuff mill on the ground floor, on the footprint of the elder Stuart's 1751 mill; the machinery is an early-1730s English mill installed in the 1930 restoration, driven by a rebuilt waterwheel.
- The gristmill (1757) — Benjamin Hammond's one-room colonial gristmill across the stream, operational since its waterwheel was reconnected in 2007; museum tours include the operation of the mills.
- The millpond and stream — Pausacaco Pond (also called Carr Pond) and the Mattatuxet, the power source for both mills, remain the centerpiece of the grounds.
- The fish ladder — installed by the Rhode Island Division of Fish and Wildlife in the 1960s; river herring pass over the dam to spawn in the upper pond each spring.
- Nature trails — 23 acres of marked trails past the millpond, streams, stone walls, the Burlingame and Hammond cemeteries, marsh overlooks and a view of Carr Pond; over 85 species of birds have been sighted on the property.
- The herb garden — a colonial-period herb garden near the kitchen door, maintained by the Plum Beach Garden Club.
- The Bell Art Gallery (built 2014) — the welcome center and gallery, holding original works by Gilbert Stuart and portraits of Gilbert by his daughter Jane Stuart, with rotating exhibitions of Rhode Island artists.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The house's exact construction year is not pinned down: the NPS nomination says "built sometime prior to 1751," and the museum styles it both the "1750 home" and the "1751 home" on different pages. This entry uses "built by 1751."

"First snuff mill in America" is hedged at the primary federal source: the NPS nomination says "possibly the first snuff mill in America." The flat claim comes from the museum tradition, repeated by secondary sources. The claim is printed here with the hedge visible.

The working snuff mill is not the original machinery. The original mill and waterwheel were lost in the 18th–19th centuries; the mill that runs today is an early-1730s English snuff mill installed during the 1930 restoration, considered faithful in design to the original. It should never be described as Stuart-era equipment.

The gristmill's date conflicts in secondary material: the NPS nomination dates it to 1757 under Benjamin Hammond's ownership; a photo caption in secondary coverage labels it "c. 1662," which no primary source checked corroborates. The 1757 date from the nomination is used here. The furnishings are period colonial pieces; none is original to the house.
