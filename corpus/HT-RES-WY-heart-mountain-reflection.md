## 2. The story
Begin with a home, not a barrack.

In a published interview with **Shelly C. Lowe**, **Sam Mihara** recalls his family's San Francisco home: multiple generations living together, his father working as a bilingual newspaper editor, children speaking English with friends and Japanese with their parents and grandparents. He describes a settled childhood before the government's forced removal. He was nine when incarcerated at Heart Mountain. [3]

His remembered uncertainty is the center of this stop: **“We didn’t know where we were going.”** He recalls packing without knowing the destination, including clothing inadequate for the Wyoming winter. This is his firsthand recollection, published retrospectively, not a recovered transport manifest or a universal rule for every family's baggage. The NEH account dates his family's imprisonment at Heart Mountain from August 1942 to June 1945. [3]

The historic government's “relocation” vocabulary must not make the experience sound voluntary. Here it means forced removal and incarceration. This dossier uses *incarceration* in its own prose while preserving historical titles as source labels. A child remembering an ordinary home before confinement supplies a necessary corrective to treating the site as a collection of unusual buildings.

The Foundation's public history also preserves differences among those confined. It describes draft resistance alongside military service, rather than treating Japanese Americans as a single voice or requiring one form of patriotism to justify civil rights. Those distinctions deserve a separate reading, not a visitor debate that asks whether the incarceration was deserved. [7]

**Interpretive conclusion:** the strongest moment is realizing that the suitcase is not the beginning of Mihara's life. It marks the interruption of a life already rich with relationships. Do not turn packing, barrack occupancy or restricted movement into a simulation. Listening is the activity.

## 3. Then / now
- **Before forced removal:** Mihara's account begins with family life and belonging. It is his perspective, not a statistical portrait of all Japanese Americans. [3]
- **1942–1945:** his family chronology describes one family's confinement, not necessarily the site's exact opening/closing dates. [3]
- **Present interpretation:** the Foundation publishes survivor and descendant material and interprets both military service and draft resistance. A modern interpretive center is not an intact wartime camp. [7]
- **Present access:** the public museum, appointment-only archives and historic landscape are different access classes. Admission does not confer permission across every former camp parcel. [2]

## 4. What's still there
**Public interior:** the operator identifies the Interpretive Center as wheelchair accessible, with a manual wheelchair available first-come, first-served. Audio guides are offered in English, Japanese, Spanish and German; videos are subtitled and transcripts can be requested. These published provisions do not guarantee a specific device's availability on arrival. [4]

**Historic landscape:** the operator describes surviving structures and a quarter-mile paved interpretive trail. It also identifies remains in former residential fields. A descriptive page or filmed visit is not permission to enter those fields. This draft does not route visitors to them. [5]

**Root cellar:** its operator page describes restoration and prospective public opening, including older work phases. No routine interior admission is verified. It remains excluded from this proposed stop, regardless of visible paths or past special tours. [6]

**Excluded:** graves and cemetery visits, archaeological ground, private parcels, fields, off-path remnants, closed buildings, restoration areas and any unverified hospital/root-cellar interior. The mountain hiking destination and Heart Mountain Ranch are separate from the interpretive center; neither is part of this visit. [2]

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

## Connected ground — Four of the Ten Camps, 1942–1946

Heart Mountain was one of the ten War Relocation Authority camps, and in June 1944 sixty-three of its young men were convicted together in Cheyenne, in the largest mass trial in Wyoming history, after refusing the draft from inside confinement.
