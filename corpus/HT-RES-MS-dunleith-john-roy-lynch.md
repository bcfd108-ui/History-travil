# Dunleith — John Roy Lynch and the family a sale brought to Natchez

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP03 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

At 84 Homochitto Street the verified public destination is The Castle Restaurant and Pub, in the former carriage house and stables. Use the restaurant’s designated entrance during service if visiting as a customer. Its dedicated page lists Monday–Saturday pub hours of 4–9 p.m. and dinner 6–9 p.m., with Sunday closed. The inn’s general page advertises daily dinner and weekend brunch, so verify the service you want. The main house contains guest rooms and sitting areas. No independently verified general-admission tour or free grounds access is promised here. [1–3]

## 2. Story

In his autobiography John Roy Lynch recounts the moment Alfred Vidal Davis told his mother Catherine that he had purchased her and her children. Lynch renders her response as “can that be possible?” It is a son’s later account of what his mother had been told. It records what a sale did to a family that had expected freedom, even though the transaction took place in Louisiana and the conversation was never recorded verbatim at the time. [4–5]

The Historic Natchez Foundation traces the family from Tacony Plantation in Louisiana to Davis’s Natchez household. It dates Davis’s acquisition of Dunleith to 1859 and identifies Catherine White and her two surviving children as enslaved domestic workers there. After emancipation Lynch took paid work at Hughes and Lakin’s photographic business in Natchez. His memoir specifies his first wage as ten dollars a month to deliver messages. He learned other tasks in the studio and described studying the trade while doing the job he had been hired for. In 1873 he entered the United States House of Representatives at age 25. The distances between a household serving room, a photographer’s premises and a seat in Congress were crossed in one working life. [4–6]

## 3. Then and now

The present colonnaded house replaced Routhland after the 1855 fire. Charles and Mary Routh Dahlgren commissioned the new residence. A National Park Service architectural study identifies John Crothers as builder. Subsequent owner Davis gave the estate its present name. The building now used by diners was a carriage house and stable. It is a separate surviving part of the estate and should be identified as such when telling Lynch’s story. [1,5,7–8]

## 4. Surviving features and access

Public customer space — the Castle dining venue during service. Hotel space — main-house rooms, sitting areas and porches as described for inn guests. Documentary location — the domestic household in which Lynch worked. No record examined here puts his particular task in the present restaurant. [1–2,4–5]

## 5. Public progression

At the restaurant’s public entrance, identify the earlier function of the building. Read Lynch’s family account from this surviving service complex. **Strongest story moment:** his memoir can give an exact figure for his first wage after emancipation. Put those ten dollars beside his account of his mother being told that her own family had been bought. [1,4–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The foundation gives 1859 for the acquisition. The short congressional biographical entry instead associates the family’s move with 1863. Property purchase, a household move and emancipation are separate events; the exact move date warrants further evidence. The site history calls the carriage house eighteenth-century; leave its precise date open pending a fabric or documentary study. The shared name Catherine White also appears in Stanton Hall research. No cross-site identity has been established. [1,5–7]
