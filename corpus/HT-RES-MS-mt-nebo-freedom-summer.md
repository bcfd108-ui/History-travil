# Mount Nebo Missionary Baptist Church and the march to Philadelphia's courthouse

*On Old Ground · Regional March · Mississippi OP12 · Labeled research draft · Reflection only*

## 2. Story

The congregation at Mount Nebo gave local civil rights organizers a room in which people could assemble and talk about registering to vote. Mississippi's historical-marker documentation names Rev. A. D. Adkins as the church's founder in 1947 and describes it as the sole Philadelphia congregation willing to host Congress of Racial Equality mass meetings during the early 1960s. That is the state's documented account, and its geographical scope matters. It describes Philadelphia, leaving open which congregations and families elsewhere in Neshoba County offered other kinds of help. The room was a local choice made by a congregation and the people who kept it open. [1, 3]

In 1964 James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner went to Neshoba County in connection with an attack on a different congregation, Mount Zion Methodist Church in the Longdale community. Federal investigative documents and the NPS study place the murders in the context of the organizing work of Freedom Summer. The FBI file is an investigative record containing statements and leads of varying reliability. It should be read alongside the NPS study. [3, 5]

Two years later, Martin Luther King Jr. held a memorial at Mount Nebo and led a march to the courthouse, according to MDAH. The marker committee's account describes why the church had earlier opened its doors, “to encourage voter registration.” Those are the committee record's words, from 2023. They are neither a statement by Adkins nor a transcript of King's 1966 remarks. The archive's contemporary television-news log identifies Sheriff Lawrence Rainey outside during the march and records singing, out-of-state arrivals and spectators. That log remains the evidence for what the reel contains. [1, 2]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Mount Nebo Missionary Baptist Church in Philadelphia is the historic church and memorial-service site. Its present operating congregation and its memorial belong to their own ground. The courthouse square is linked as the public endpoint of the 1966 march. The two places remain distinct. [1–3]

**Present public anchor.** Neshoba County Courthouse Square at the courthouse currently listed as 401 E. Beacon Street. Use the public perimeter sidewalks of the courthouse block. NPS calls the square's surrounding edges Byrd or Range Avenue, Main, Center and Beacon. Use the building and marked sidewalks as the scope given the directional discrepancy below. [3, 4]

## 5. Public progression

Use the courthouse-block public sidewalk for a short quiet stop. Read the church's account before looking across the square. Hold the distinct choices represented by the two places together, from people meeting indoors to people demanding recognition in a county seat. The story lands at **From the meeting room to the county square**, a reflection on the connection between holding a meeting and insisting that the work of citizenship be heard in public. No march reenactment, route or interior stop is assigned. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- MDAH's WLBT log gives courthouse steps as King's speaking location. The Alabama archive's Peppler still record identifies the front of the county library. Those records may describe different moments or use different geographic descriptions. The sequence and precise position are open. The photograph aggregator also supplies Montgomery coordinates despite the title identifying Philadelphia, Mississippi. Those coordinates are excluded. [2, 6]
- NPS uses West Beacon for the square boundary. The county's contact listing uses 401 E. Beacon for the courthouse. Neither label supplies the precise speaking location. [3, 4]
- The packet identifies the congregation and Adkins. A roster of organizers and local participants, a transcript of King's church remarks, and an independently confirmed church visitor policy were not retrieved. [1]
- The WLBT record restricts collection use to the State Archives. The linked June 25–26, 1966 issue of *The Southern Courier* returned a timed-out HTTP 400 on the attempted remote open. The newsfilm footage and original issue were therefore unavailable for viewing. [2, 7]
