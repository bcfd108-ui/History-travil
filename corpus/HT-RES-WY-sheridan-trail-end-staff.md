## 2. The story
**Named worker evidence:** the site's 2019 exhibition publication *Everyday Living, Everyday Lives* reproduces a recollection attributed to **Rose Sobotka Hill**, who worked at Trail End in the late 1940s. It places her account beside Manville Kendrick's later memory that the whole family always ate in the dining room. Hill's account describes different breakfast arrangements. [3]

Her short published phrase is **“My first duty every morning ... was to set the dining room table”**. The ellipsis is in the institutional publication. This is worker testimony as excerpted by a museum, not an original recording independently heard or a new interview. [3]

**Original interpretive angle:** a house can look complete and still tell an incomplete story. The person who owned the table and the person who prepared it may remember different things because they performed different roles. Neither a beautiful room nor a famous surname tells you, by itself, what happened there each morning.

The purpose is not to stage a courtroom verdict about a breakfast. It is to show why household workers belong in the evidence, not merely in a sentence about the owners' comfort. A visitor can learn to ask who supplied the account, what period it covers, and what daily work made someone else's routine possible.

The exhibit's language treats the recollections as corrective evidence. This draft is more cautious: the accounts differ, but their original interview dates, full contexts and exact periods have not been recovered. Do not assume both speakers were describing the same year or every breakfast of their lives.

The site also explains that domestic work included both day workers and live-in employees, with room and board forming part of some arrangements. That is institutional context, not Hill's own employment contract. [4] No claim about her exact pay, citizenship, ethnicity, household or feelings is invented.

## 3. Then / now
**Historical household:** domestic service was part of the functioning home, rather than an optional extra added to its architecture. The worker perspective changes how a room is read.

**2019 interpretation:** the exhibition deliberately published a staff member's recollection alongside a family member's memory. This was an April–December 2019 exhibition, not proof of a current installation. [3]

**Present museum:** the building is a public historic site, but only designated tour areas are public. The house's former domestic circulation does not define today's permitted visitor circulation. [1]

Original comparison prompt: *If a room could be described only by the people who worked in it, what would its label emphasize?* The exercise must remain a question, not a fabricated first-person staff monologue.

## 4. What's still there
The public historic house supplies the physical anchor, while the published exhibition supplies the named testimony. No current display of Hill's recollection, personal belongings, uniform or portrait has been confirmed.

A separate site publication, *From Dream Home to Historic House*, explicitly says the servants' bedrooms do **not** retain their original furnishings. Its furnishing interpretation draws on other houses' photographs and recollections. This is a crucial distinction: a room setting is not automatically a surviving employee's own possession or an exact reconstruction of her life. [5]

That publication also leaves the original connection of bedroom bells uncertain. Do not turn a plausible interpretation into a verified technical account. No equipment demonstration, staff-room entry or operational mechanism is part of this dossier. [5]

Where a public view is unavailable, use the document rather than stepping past a barrier to complete a narrative. A story can be complete in meaning without a photograph of every space it mentions.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Public-museum version, editorial allowance 40–60 minutes, not an operator tour:**
1. Verify opening and enter through designated admission. Ask only for the ordinary public tour boundary; no special access is assumed or requested here.
2. Begin with the visible public presentation of the household, using whatever admitted viewpoint is available. No particular room or object is promised.
3. Read the attributed worker excerpt, keeping her name and the publication's date with it.
4. Compare the two recollections as differently situated evidence. Ask what additional record would help determine their scope.
5. If public interpretation includes a furnished staff room, distinguish interpretive furnishing from original possession. If not, discuss that distinction from the publication.
6. **End at the strongest story moment:** the house's history is not finished when the owners have been named. The person who set the table changes what the table means.

**Closed-season / unavailable-room version, editorial allowance 20–30 minutes:** read the two institutional documents and examine their distinctions between testimony, later interpretation and original fabric. No exterior walk, neighboring address or restricted-room substitute is required.

Suggested durations are editorial estimates. This is not a cooking demonstration, servant-role game or invitation to imitate work with historic machinery. No Route Group is activated.
