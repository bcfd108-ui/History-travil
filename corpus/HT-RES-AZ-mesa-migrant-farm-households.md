## 2. The story
**Marshall Trimble's published family oral history** supplies a specific Mesa agricultural-household connection. Interviewed by **Pam Stevenson on November 13, 2013**, he recounts his paternal family's 1918 arrival and a labor offer for Mesa cotton fields that also provided tent accommodation. He remembers the family account of his grandmother picking cotton and his grandfather supervising workers. [4, printed pp. 1–3]

His short summary is **“They picked cotton.”** This is **a grandson recounting inherited family history**, not an eyewitness to 1918. His own stated birth year is 1939. Selected transcript pages were directly read after downloading the publisher's PDF; the recording and entire interview were not reviewed. [4]

The story's power is the connection between employment and shelter. A new arrival was not merely choosing a crop to harvest; accepting work also shaped where the household could sleep. That is an interpretive reading of the account, not a verified lease, wage record or universal description of Mesa farm labor.

## 3. Then / now
Trimble distinguishes the earlier Mesa cotton-camp episode from his parents' later livestock household near Tempe. The latter must not be mapped into Mesa just because he was born in a Mesa hospital. The transcript provides a remembered family geography, not a site survey. [4, printed pp. 1–3]

The public museum is an independent place for historical learning. **No evidence establishes that the family lived or worked on the museum property**, that its collection contains their personal objects, or that a current exhibition tells this episode. This is a museum-supported reading framework, not a claim of an intact camp or a surviving family home.

The distinction matters: treating every agricultural artifact as this family's property would make a pleasing story at the cost of truth. An authentic account can remain valuable even when no associated object has been found.

**Regional comparison, not Mesa proof:** Geta LeSeur's published Arizona PBS interview discusses research with Black agricultural families centered on Randolph. It is useful for resisting a single-family, single-race account of Arizona farm labor, but neither the interview nor the families discussed are silently reassigned to Mesa. [6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Before arrival, identify who is speaking.** Read the narrator, interview date and descendant-versus-eyewitness distinction. The family account stands on its own as an archive-only experience.
2. **Enter only the museum's authorized visitor space during confirmed opening.** Do not use an old place-name as a destination or follow a source's reference toward working property.
3. **Start with the relationship between work and shelter.** Hold the brief account in mind while reading any relevant public interpretation actually available. If nothing directly supports this topic, do not invent a display connection.
4. **Separate object from attribution.** A generic agricultural object, if displayed, may illustrate a kind of work but cannot establish this family's ownership, use or experience without an item record.
5. **Land on the household, not the harvest total.** The strongest moment is recognizing that a short work offer could reorganize family life. Leave room for what the grandmother herself might have said; her grandson's story is not her recorded voice.

This is a reflection progression rather than a physical hunt. No crop picking, labor reenactment, tent reconstruction, water-system visit or poverty simulation is proposed. It does not require a visit to any historically named property.
