# HT-RES-KY-waverly-hills-sanatorium — A tuberculosis sanatorium on a Louisville hill, built in two campaigns against an epidemic
Division prisons-asylums-tours-001 • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Waverly Hills was Louisville and Jefferson County's answer to tuberculosis: a public sanatorium opened in 1910 in wooden pavilions on a wooded hill, replaced in 1926 by a large Tudor Gothic building of brick and stone that still stands. For fifty-one years people were sent up this hill to rest, to breathe open air, and — in large numbers, though not in the numbers legend claims — to die. The sanatorium closed in 1961, served a second life as a geriatric center, and is now held by a historical society that runs tours of the building. This entry records what the documents support; the inflated figures that circulate about this place are carried in §6, labeled as unverified.

## 2. The story
Tuberculosis was the disease that organized public health in American cities in the early twentieth century, and Louisville was no exception. Jefferson County's response began in 1906, when a Board of Tuberculosis Hospital was appointed; a tax levied by the city and county gave the board its building money. In 1908 the board acquired much of the sanatorium site from Major Thomas H. Hays, whose country estate on the hill was called "Waverly Hill" — the source of the sanatorium's name, per the Kentucky Historic Resources Inventory form filed for its National Register listing. The Major's brick country house no longer exists.

The first sanatorium was built quickly and cheaply. The inventory form records a notice in the trade journal American Architect of July 21, 1909, that bids would be received for construction of buildings at Waverly Hill, signed by J. J. Gaffney, architect — a prominent Louisville practitioner. What rose was a small wooden plant: a two-story administration building with two open-air pavilions of twenty patients each. The operator's history and local accounts date the opening to July 26, 1910; the inventory form, working from the surviving paper, dates the first buildings only to about 1909–1911. The treatment was the era's standard prescription — rest, food, and fresh air, taken outdoors in all seasons — and the forty beds filled at once and stayed filled.

The wooden plant was overwhelmed within the decade. In 1922 the hospital board pressed Louisville and Jefferson County for a bond issue to build anew; the inventory form preserves the campaign in the Louisville press, from the Courier-Journal's January 1, 1922 headline "One Million Dollars for Hospital Urged" onward. Arthur Loomis was chosen as architect in 1922, and in 1924 the firm of D. X. Murphy and Brother was selected to assist. The result, dedicated in October 1926, was the building that stands today: a large brick and stone sanatorium on a curved plan, Tudor Gothic, with a square tower above the entrance and a three-bay Gothic-arched entrance porch — the inventory form's description. The Filson Historical Society, which has reconstructed the building in 3D from the original blueprints, gives its capacity as 400 beds and its dedication date as October 20, 1926. Near the main building stood a two-story frame structure with half-timber work, apparently one of the c.1911 buildings, and a two-and-a-half-story brick annex in matching style, completed about 1928.

Inside, the regime remained the rest cure, supplemented over time by the surgical interventions of pre-antibiotic tuberculosis medicine — procedures to collapse a diseased lung, up to removal of ribs — which the memorial literature compiled from staff and patient accounts describes, and which many patients did not survive. A tunnel runs from the building down the hill to the railroad tracks at its foot. It was built as the sanatorium's service passage — supplies came up it — and it acquired its later function from the arithmetic of the wards: the dead were taken out through the tunnel, on the accounts preserved by the sanatorium's memorial record, so that living patients would not watch hearses come and go. It is a service tunnel. The name later attached to it is not used in this entry.

How many people died at Waverly Hills is the question this record cannot answer exactly, and this entry does not pretend otherwise. What the documented anchors support is stated here; the rest is in §6. Dr. J. Frank W. Stewart, a former assistant medical director (1945–1955), wrote in his autobiography that the highest number of deaths in a single year was 152, at the end of the Second World War, when soldiers were returning with advanced tuberculosis — men who, he wrote, often arrived too ill to live more than a week. A later check against filed death certificates put the 1945 figure at 162. Estimates built from the certificates filed since 1911 come to roughly 6,000 deaths across the sanatorium's life. By 1955 the yearly figure had fallen to 42. Those are the proportions of the documented record: several thousand deaths over fifty-one years, in a building of 400 beds.

The reason for the fall was antibiotics. Streptomycin, the first effective drug against tuberculosis, reached clinical use in the mid-1940s, and care moved steadily out of the sanatorium. Control of Waverly Hills had already passed, in 1942, to the new Louisville and Jefferson County board of health. In 1960 the Commonwealth assumed the care of tuberculosis patients, and the Waverly Hills patients were transferred to state institutions, per the inventory form. The owners' history and Kentucky Historic Institutions date the closure to June 1961, with the last patients sent to Hazelwood Sanatorium in Louisville.

The buildings were leased in 1962 to the Kentucky Geriatrics Foundation, a private nonprofit, and reopened as a geriatric center — Woodhaven. That era lasted about twenty years and ended badly: the state closed the facility in the early 1980s, after findings of patient neglect and abuse documented in the reporting of the closure (the exact years are disputed; see §6). The complex then stood empty for two decades, deteriorating and vandalized. It had been listed on the National Register of Historic Places on July 12, 1983, as the Waverly Hills Tuberculosis Sanatorium Historic Buildings. In 2001 the property was bought by Charles and Tina Mattingly, who formed the Waverly Hills Historical Society to fund its restoration; the tours described in §1 are the society's program, and tour income is the building's maintenance fund.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the first sanatorium of 1910 — a two-story wooden administration building and two open-air pavilions of twenty beds each on a hilltop, built for a county whose tuberculosis wards were already full. **Now:** the wooden plant is gone; the inventory form notes that the two-story frame, half-timbered building near the main block may have been one of the c.1911 structures, and the 1926 building carries the whole visit.

**Then:** October 1926 — a new 400-bed sanatorium dedicated, Tudor Gothic, curved to its hill, its porches built for the fresh-air treatment of a disease with no drug cure. **Now:** the same building toured in small groups, its wards empty since 1981, held against weather and vandalism by tour receipts.

**Then:** the tunnel as working infrastructure — supplies in, and the dead out, below the sight of patients taking the air cure on the porches above. **Now:** the tunnel survives as part of the toured property, the most asked-about feature of a building whose ordinary work it was.


## 4. What's still there
- The 1926 main building: brick and stone, curved plan, square tower over the entrance, three-bay Gothic-arched entrance porch, Tudor Gothic detailing — the inventory form's description, verifiable against this entry's photograph

- The open-air porch bays along the elevations — the architectural form of the fresh-air treatment itself
- The two-and-a-half-story brick annex building, completed about 1928, in matching Tudor Gothic style (inventory form)
- The two-story frame, half-timbered staff building near the main block, identified by the inventory form as possibly a survivor of the c.1911 first plant
- The service tunnel from the building down the hill toward the railroad tracks at its foot
- The hilltop site itself — a wooded hill in southwestern Jefferson County, about 16 acres of it inside the National Register boundary with three buildings, per the inventory form
- The gate and approach the operator now uses — Gate A, from East Pages Lane — the current visitor boundary, not the historic Dixie Highway frontage the Register record carries

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the entrance porch:** Start where the inventory form starts — the square tower, the three-bay Gothic-arched porch, the curved plan opening to either side — and with the two campaigns in mind: Gaffney's wooden plant of 1909–1911 stood here first; this front is Loomis and Murphy's answer of 1922–1926.
2. **On the porch elevations:** The treatment, built. Open-air bays floor over floor are the rest cure in architectural form; read them against the 400-bed capacity and the death figures in §2.
3. **Inside the wards:** The sanatorium's working floors — and the floor-count question in §6, testable directly against the inventory form's four-story description.
4. **At the tunnel:** The service passage to the railroad tracks, described as the record describes it — supplies up, the dead out of the patients' sight.
5. **At the annex and the frame staff building:** The rest of the nominated group — the c.1928 annex and the possible c.1911 survivor — and then the second institution: Woodhaven's twenty years in the same rooms.


## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The death toll is this entry's largest gap, and it is carried here in full. Documented anchors: Dr. Stewart's autobiography gives 152 deaths in the worst single year; a death-certificate check gives 162 deaths in 1945 specifically — the two figures do not agree, and both print. Estimates compiled from filed death certificates since 1911 come to roughly 6,000 deaths in total; arithmetic from the 1945 certificate count gives a maximum possible total of about 8,212 over the sanatorium's life. Kentucky Historic Institutions is compiling an index of recorded patient deaths from 1911 to 1960 from the original certificates at the state archives; that work is in progress and is the only path toward a firmer count. Against those anchors, figures of 36,000 and 63,000 deaths circulate widely in print and broadcast accounts. **They are unverified claims.** No source checked documents them, and they exceed what the documented yearly figures can support by an order of magnitude. A 2019 television report put the operators' own estimate at 20,000 to 60,000 while noting that many records are said to be lost; a further single-source claim holds that records before 1935 were destroyed in the 1937 Ohio River flood. Both are carried here as claims, not findings. Stories attaching deaths of named staff to a specific fifth-floor room circulate with the site's tours; no death certificate, newspaper report, or hospital record documenting them was found in the sources checked, and they are not repeated here.

The architect credit is compressed differently by different records. The National Register's summary record credits "Gaffney, J. J." and "Murphy, D. X." The inventory form behind the listing is more precise, and this entry follows it: Gaffney was architect of the first buildings (bids advertised 1909); Arthur Loomis was chosen as architect of the new buildings in 1922; D. X. Murphy and Brother were brought in to assist in 1924. The Filson Historical Society credits the 1926 building to "architect Arthur Loomis and the D.X. Murphy architectural firm," agreeing with the inventory. Accounts naming Gaffney as architect of the 1926 main building are using the summary credit, not the inventory.

The dedication day in October 1926 varies: the Filson gives October 20; other current accounts give October 17; the inventory form, citing the Louisville Times of October 7, 1926 ("Waverly Hills Hospital to be Dedicated"), confirms the month and year only. The month prints; the day is disputed.

The building's height is counted two ways. The inventory form describes "a large four story brick and stone building." The operator walks visitors through "all five floors." The difference is presumably the ground level, but neither source says so, and both counts print as their sources give them.

The Woodhaven years are given differently across accounts. The inventory form records the 1962 lease to the Kentucky Geriatrics Foundation for a center to care for elderly residents. The center's opening is elsewhere dated 1963, and its closure 1981 in some accounts and 1982 in others — the closure following a grand-jury finding of patient abuse in the fullest of those accounts. This entry prints the lease date from the inventory and carries the opening and closing years as disputed.

Two address records stand side by side. The Register documentation gives 8101 Dixie Highway, the historic frontage. The operator's current address is 4400 Paralee Drive, and its arrival instructions route visitors to Gate A from 4301 E. Pages Lane. All three print in §1 and here so the discrepancy is visible rather than silently resolved.

Finally, two absences. No Library of Congress photographic record of this building was found when the Library's catalog was checked on November 22, 2026 — no HABS survey and no other LOC item — so this entry's photograph comes from Wikimedia Commons under the license stated in §7. And the tunnel's length is given variously in published accounts (about 500 feet is most common); no surveyed measurement was found in the records checked, and no length prints in §2.

## 8. Standing on the ground
What a visitor can check against the record, on site:

- The entrance composition against the inventory form's description: a square tower above the entrance and a three-bay Gothic-arched porch, in brick and stone — the same elements as this entry's photograph.
- The curved plan: the ward wings' sweep is readable from the grounds and is the form the inventory form records, not a later alteration.
- The porch bays as treatment architecture: floor over floor of open-air bays, built for the rest-and-fresh-air regime the first pavilions of 1910 already embodied.
- The floor count as an open question: the inventory form's "four story" description against the operator's five-floor count, checked level by level inside the building.
- The annex and the frame staff building against the nomination group: three buildings on about 16 acres, with the half-timbered frame building standing as the possible survivor of the 1909–1911 plant the inventory form describes.
- The tunnel's course: from the building down the hill toward the railroad tracks at its foot — the service route the memorial record describes — checked against the slope and the tracks, not against any later name for it.- The boundary of the visit itself: Gate A and the society's tour route are the whole of the public ground; the Dixie Highway address in the Register record is the historic frontage, not the way in.
