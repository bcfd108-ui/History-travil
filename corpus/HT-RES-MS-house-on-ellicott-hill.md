# House on Ellicott Hill — a flag over a camp, then a merchant’s house

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March · OP03 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

Plan for a scheduled public pilgrimage tour. The Natchez Garden Club identifies the property as 211 North Canal Street and directs cars to the top of the hill at Jefferson and Wall. Enter only during a confirmed public opening. The club also accepts private group appointments. Those do not establish a regular walk-in visit. Check the dated event listing before departure, including holiday and weather changes. [1–2]

## 2. Story

The man whose name the house carries set up a camp here. In a June 1797 report to the president, the U.S. secretary of state described Andrew Ellicott arriving in Natchez on February 24 to help determine the boundary with Spanish territory. The report places his camp at the upper end of town on February 27. It says that “two days after” he raised the United States flag. Spanish governor Manuel Gayoso de Lemos asked through an aide that he take it down. Ellicott declined. His astronomical measurements and his flag occupied the same hillside. The first helped determine a line, while the second asserted authority that was still disputed locally. This is the American government’s account of the exchange. [3]

Between 1798 and 1801 merchant James Moore built the house now shown here. Its raised brick lower story made use of the slope: it opens toward the lower street but lies below grade at the back. The site could present two stories on one side and a different approach on the other. In the 1930s Samuel Wilson Jr. and Richard Koch worked on its restoration for the Natchez Garden Club. The result preserves both early house construction and a later generation’s decisions about its appearance. [4–5]

## 3. Then and now

The camp accounts explain the name on the sign. The standing fabric belongs to the subsequent merchant’s house. Inside, the principal room preserves a carved mantel, while its matching partner has a different history. A small dome in the ceiling allowed a light fixture additional height. The proposed reflective tin lining is an interpretation. [4]

## 4. Surviving features and access

On seasonal public tours, the guide determines which rooms open. Exterior features include the sloping grade, the raised lower story and roof extensions. The national historic park’s architectural study records early Federal millwork. There is no verified spot for Ellicott’s tent or flagstaff within the visitor area. [1,3–5]

## 5. Public progression

Approach by the club’s upper entrance. Let the grade explain the lower story before viewing the principal room. **Strongest story moment:** read the 1797 report beside a house built after the dispute. The report describes a tented survey camp. Moore’s house tells what people built on the hill once that encounter had passed. [1,3–5]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The tourism account assigns the flag to February 27. The June government report assigns that date to the encampment and the raising two days later, which falls on March 1. A later Library of Congress photograph’s supplied title uses 1798. The contemporary report is the basis for the chronology here. The club and architectural history also give different Canal Street numbers (211 and 215). Use the club’s specified visitor entrance, and leave pin coordinates empty. [1,3–4,6–7]

## 7. Historic photograph

LOC photographic item 2017879581 remains a discovery lead. Its catalog title’s event date conflicts with the 1797 report. No individual image adopted. A later photo of a surviving house cannot establish where a flagstaff stood. [3,6]

## 8. Optional observations

During a confirmed seasonal public opening, visitors may voluntarily report the date and whether the Jefferson and Wall entrance was in use. Reports are pending review. No repeat photograph or game element.
