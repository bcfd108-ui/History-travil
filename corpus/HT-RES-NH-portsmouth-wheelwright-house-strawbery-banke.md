# HT-RES-NH-portsmouth-wheelwright-house-strawbery-banke — Portsmouth: the house with two names
Division NH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

The records disagree about this house, and the disagreement is printed rather than settled: the 1961 federal survey called it the John Clark House, dated about 1750, while Strawbery Banke interprets it as Wheelwright House, built 1784 for a Portsmouth mariner who died owing money. What is not in dispute is the ground — a Puddle Dock lot where a captain’s household, a widow’s third, and three enslaved people named Cato, Nero, and Jane all belong to the record.

## 1. Trip gate

The lawful anchor is the staffed Strawbery Banke visitor entrance and the ticketed or program-specific museum route. ## 2. Story

Strawbery Banke preserves part of the Portsmouth neighborhood long known as Puddle Dock. The museum’s archaeology and current interpretation begin before European settlement: Abenaki people used the seacoast seasonally for thousands of years, leaving material evidence of hunting, fishing, food preparation, and temporary structures. English colonists established a settlement on the Piscataqua in the seventeenth century. The sheltered tidal inlet later called Puddle Dock supported houses, wharves, shops, maritime trades, and a neighborhood whose people changed repeatedly over more than three centuries.

The building now interpreted as Wheelwright House occupies the northwest corner of Jefferson Street and Horse Lane. Its name and date are not simple. A 1961 federal Historic American Buildings Survey called it the John Clark House, dated it about 1750, and associated the Clark family with maritime work. Strawbery Banke’s current interpretation calls it Wheelwright House, gives a 1784 construction date, and centers John Wheelwright, a Portsmouth mariner, his wife Martha, and their two children. The difference is not a minor label error; it reflects changing documentary and architectural research. This dossier uses the operator’s current name while retaining the HABS name on the adopted photograph.

The current museum account places John Wheelwright in Portsmouth’s Atlantic maritime economy. He commanded merchant vessels and, when the Revolution began, served aboard the Portsmouth-built frigate *Raleigh* and on privateering ships. War at sea was both national service and dangerous commerce. Privateers operated under government authority but depended on captured ships and cargo; sailors and their families carried the financial and bodily risk.

Wheelwright died owing money to creditors. The house was sold at auction, but his widow Martha retained use of one-third under inheritance provisions. That detail makes the house more than a captain’s monument. It holds a legal and domestic story about debt, widowhood, property, and the limited protection available to a woman whose household economy had depended on seafaring.

Research surrounding the property also records slavery in the earlier Wheelwright household connected with this ground. Jeremiah and Demaris Wheelwright lived on the lot with their children and three enslaved people named Cato, Nero, and Jane. The surviving free records used for this draft do not securely establish which present structural fabric those people occupied or how the current 1784 date relates to the earlier dwelling on the lot. Their names belong in the record, but the building must not be made to “witness” rooms or events that the evidence does not place.

By the twentieth century, Puddle Dock had become a dense working neighborhood. Urban-renewal plans threatened demolition. In 1958 residents and preservation advocates formed Strawbery Banke, Inc. to save a nearly ten-acre area. The museum opened on a limited basis in 1965. A 1961 HABS campaign photographed the house before restoration. The National Register nomination reports that Strawbery Banke employed Philip W. Baker in 1966 to restore the building then called the Capt. John Clark House, with architectural consultation by William G. Perry. HABS records restoration and remodeling between 1961 and 1968.

Today the house supports a historic foodways demonstration. Interpreters use the open hearth to show how eighteenth-century households gathered, stored, preserved, and cooked seasonal ingredients. The demonstration is interpretive work, not a claim that a specific pie, chutney, tool arrangement, or daily routine has continued unchanged in this exact room.

## 3. Then and now

Jack E. Boucher’s December 1961 HABS photograph shows the south front before museum restoration. The building appears as an occupied or recently occupied neighborhood house rather than a finished colonial exhibit: utility lines cross above it, an enclosed porch projects at the west, additions sit behind the main block, and surrounding streets and buildings press close. The entrance surround and sash pattern are visible, but the fabric looks worn and altered.

The HABS form described a roughly 30-by-28-foot, two-and-a-half-story timber-frame house with a gable roof, clapboards on the south and west, shingles on the east, a western enclosed porch, and a rear projection. The current museum description emphasizes a simple Georgian house with a triangular door pediment, repeated window-head forms, and fluted entrance pilasters. The contrast between the 1961 image and current presentation is itself part of the subject: restoration selected and clarified an eighteenth-century appearance from a building that had continued to change.

The wider museum now interprets Puddle Dock across many periods rather than treating every house as “colonial.” Wheelwright House’s late-eighteenth-century foodways program is one layer within a neighborhood that includes Indigenous history, maritime trade, enslavement, immigration, working-class life, naval-yard labor, urban renewal, and preservation.

## 5. Public progression

Enter through the museum’s official visitor route and begin with the neighborhood rather than the hearth. Read Jefferson Street as working ground where houses, shops, wharves, and later naval-yard employment supported households over centuries. At Wheelwright House, first compare the present facade with Boucher’s 1961 image. Utility lines, additions, worn cladding, and street conditions in the photograph expose the twentieth-century neighborhood that preservation advocates encountered.

Inside, if the building and demonstration are open, separate three stories: the eighteenth-century maritime household; the specific Wheelwright family record of war, debt, widowhood, and property; and the twentieth-century restoration and present-day foodways program. Ask what evidence supports a demonstrated technique and what is a careful reconstruction. Do not turn open-hearth work, privateering, or the lives of enslaved people into a game.

Continue into the broader Strawbery Banke site to test any impression that this was a uniform wealthy district. Other houses interpret merchants, laborers, immigrants, Black residents, Jewish families, naval-yard workers, shopkeepers, and changing domestic arrangements. Wheelwright House is a focused doorway into the neighborhood, not a substitute for it.

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

- HABS identifies the resource as John Clark House, dates initial construction to about 1750, and says its name came from a maritime Clark family. Strawbery Banke’s current record identifies Wheelwright House, gives 1784, and attributes it to mariner John Wheelwright. This draft adopts the current operator name but does not erase the archival conflict.
- The property may include fabric or a building sequence older than the current 1784 interpretation. A full dendrochronological or title-chain resolution was not available in the free records used here.
- The 1961 photograph predates restoration. It should not be used to claim that every visible addition or finish survives today.
- The National Register nomination names a 1966 restoration campaign. HABS gives the broader 1961–68 alteration window. Both are retained.
- Cato, Nero, and Jane are named in historical research connected to an earlier Wheelwright household on this ground. The sources used here do not support assigning them to a specific surviving room or to the 1784 building fabric.
- Foodways demonstrations reconstruct documented practices. They are not uninterrupted household traditions and may change with staff availability.
- The operator’s season and house openings change. The current closure notice is a trip gate, not a permanent status.
- Coordinates remain blank until two independent authoritative sources agree within the project tolerance.
