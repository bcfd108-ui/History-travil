# Old Warren County Courthouse and the hands behind its white walls

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March OP07 · November 20, 2026 Pacific

## 1. Trip gate

**Drive-up and short walk to the downtown museum.** The Historical Society lists November hours of Monday–Saturday 8:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m. and Sunday 1:30–4:30 p.m. Daylight-saving weekdays and Saturdays extend to 5 p.m. Admission applies to the museum. Public exterior viewing is from ordinary sidewalks during daylight and safe weather. Check holiday exceptions and floor access before departure. [1]

## 2. Story

A county could order a new courthouse. Building one required people to turn local clay into brick and raise an entire civic building on a hill. The federal landmark nomination identifies George and Thomas Weldon as the contractors and William Weldon as the designer. It records the contractors’ use of enslaved workers trained in the building trades and bricks burned at the site. The museum and HABS put that workforce at about 100. At present, the recorded count and the contractors’ names are far easier to recover than the names of the people who made the bricks and erected the walls. [2–4]

The name of a manufacturer survived too. The HABS survey credits Cincinnati’s Baker Iron Company with architectural iron brought downriver. The stair, doors and hardware connect work along the river to work on the courthouse hill. HABS records a tradition about sugar in the mortar. It calls it a legend. The workers’ skill and the firing of brick at the site are supported independently of that story. [3–4]

The building became a symbol of the siege and surrender of Vicksburg. Its later civilian life is visible through a second period of work. In 1939 county offices moved across Cherry Street. Eva Whitaker Davis led local efforts to find a new use for the old building. The museum operator dates its public opening to June 3, 1948 and describes volunteers clearing spaces and gathering collections. That work is part of why it is possible to read the building today. [1–3]

## 3. Then and now

The old building served as Warren County’s courthouse and offices. Today it contains a museum, a gallery sequence and the surviving upstairs courtroom. The nearby newer courthouse and the historical society’s archive have their own functions. The McCardle Research Library takes its name from a Vicksburg newspaper editor. In the 1869 U.S. Supreme Court opinion bearing his name the appeal was “dismissed for want of jurisdiction.” The contemporary federal case record identifies the federal Circuit Court, and does not place the litigation in this county courtroom. The library is a research resource and stays outside the visitor progression. [1–3,5–7]

## 5. Public progression

Begin on the public perimeter sidewalk of the courthouse square. From the approved museum entrance, follow the gallery sequence and the permitted stairs or staff-directed accessible option. **Strongest story moment — Brick below, iron above.** Compare the masonry the enslaved builders made on the hill with the cast iron that traveled to them by boat. Let this contrast in documented work carry the stop. Reflection at the siege exhibits. [1–4]

## 6. Gaps and disagreements

The museum records a start in summer 1858 and completion in 1860. The landmark nomination says the Weldons began in 1859 and finished in 1861. The LOC/HABS catalog describes June 16, 1858 as a dedication. Original county payment warrants and a dated dedication notice are still needed to distinguish design, commencement, completion and dedication. The earliest date cannot safely be made to describe all four. [2–4]

The museum calls its founder Eva Whitaker Davis. The typed federal nomination uses Eve W. Davis in its account of the preservation drive, placing an occupancy step in 1942. The operator dates her presidency to 1947 and the public opening to 1948. These latter dates reflect different stages. Names of the enslaved workers, the underlying contracts and an item-level historic-photo rights check remain open. [2–4]

## Connected ground — The Vicksburg Campaign, 1863

This courthouse, built by the Weldon brothers with enslaved workers, became the symbol of the siege of Vicksburg, and on July 4, 1863, after the surrender that ended the campaign fought at Grand Gulf, Raymond, Champion Hill, and the Big Black River, the Union flag replaced the Confederate flag on its cupola.
