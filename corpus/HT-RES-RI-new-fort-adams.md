# HT-RES-RI-new-fort-adams — Newport: America's largest coastal fortress, named for John Adams
Division RI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Fort Adams guards the East Passage into Newport Harbor from a point at the southwest end of Aquidneck Island: an irregular hollow pentagon of granite and brick vaulting, about 1,000 by 1,200 feet, with a perimeter of more than 1,750 yards and two levels of casemates in places. It was built between 1824 and 1857 to a design by Simon Bernard, a French engineer who had served under Napoleon, and it never fired a shot in conflict. Two corrections belong at the head of the record: the fort was named for President John Adams, in office when the first fort on the site was dedicated in 1799 — not John Quincy Adams — and Dwight D. Eisenhower never commanded it; his tie is presidential, the summer White House summers of 1958 and 1960 in the former commandant's quarters.

## 1. Trip gate
Checked November 26, 2026, against the Fort Adams Trust's and Rhode Island State Parks' own pages. The fort stands in Fort Adams State Park, 90 Fort Adams Drive, Newport, RI 02840 (phone 401-841-0707), and is operated as a historic site by the nonprofit Fort Adams Trust. The fort is open daily April through November; private and group tours can be arranged year-round by request. The Visitor Center is open daily 9:00 A.M. – 4:00 P.M. Guided tours run from $15 (age 6+; allow 90 minutes) and include the underground tunnels and other guided-only areas; departure times vary by date and are held in the booking system, so specific times are not established here. Self-guided tours run from $10, 9:30 A.M. – 4:00 P.M. with entry before 3:00 P.M. (allow 60–75 minutes), covering the parade field, select casemates and scenic overlooks; tunnels are a guided-tour feature. Discounts apply for youth, students, active-duty military, veterans and seniors, and Trust members tour free; final pricing is confirmed during booking. Parking is free on site near the park Visitor Center. The surrounding state park — grounds, playing fields, fishing pier, picnic areas, saltwater beach — is a separate, wider visit; the state page checked states no park grounds hours and no park entry fee, and neither is established here. Tour schedules can change for weather and events.

## 2. The story
The first Fort Adams was established on July 4, 1799, as a First System coastal fortification, designed by Major Louis de Tousard of the Army Corps of Engineers and built of brick. The National Register nomination records that the fortifications "received [their] name when certain fortifications on this site… were completed and dedicated in 1799; it was named in honour of the second President of the United States." The site had been used for harbor defence from earliest times.

After the War of 1812, the United States reviewed its coastal fortification needs — the program that became the Third System — and decided to replace the old fort with a much larger one. The new fort was designed by Brigadier General Simon Bernard, a French military engineer who had served under Napoleon. Construction began in 1824 with demolition of the 1799 brick fort and continued at irregular intervals until 1857. From 1825 to 1838 the work was overseen by Lieutenant Colonel Joseph Gilbert Totten, described in the standard record as the foremost American military engineer of his day. A local history of the fort records 438 embrasures in the design.

The new fort was first garrisoned in August 1841 and served as an active Army post through the middle of the twentieth century; secondary accounts record garrisons during the Mexican-American War, the Civil War, the Spanish-American War and both World Wars. Despite its size and long service, it never fired a shot in conflict. The property passed through Navy hands and was deeded to the State of Rhode Island in 1965 on the state's own account, through conveyances administered by the National Park Service (the end-of-service dates conflict between sources; see §6).

The commanding officer's quarters tell the fort's most famous later story. The two-story clapboard house with a high mansard roof, designed by George C. Mason and Son and built in the winter of 1872–73 for General Henry Jackson Hunt, then commandant, was the first dwelling on post built outside the walls. In 1958 and again in 1960, President Dwight D. Eisenhower used it as his summer residence — the summer White House — having initially stayed at the Naval War College in 1958 before moving to the house, closer to the Newport Country Club golf links. It is now the Eisenhower House, an event venue within the state park.

The Fort Adams Trust was formed in 1994 to stabilize, restore and operate the fort as a historic site, following an environmental remediation program in the early 1990s that made the fort safe for public access. The Trust now runs the guided tours. Fort Adams was added to the National Register on July 28, 1970, and designated a National Historic Landmark District on December 8, 1976.

## 3. Then / now
The working fort was a small city behind granite: the main work, its outworks and redoubts, Endicott-period batteries added for a later century's guns, officers' quarters, warehouses, and the commandant's house on its bluff. The district survives substantially whole — the National Register historic district covers approximately 80 acres and includes the masonry fort, its outworks and 2 redoubts, 6 Endicott-period batteries, 10 brick or frame officers' quarters and 2 brick warehouses. What changed is the ground around it: the point is now a state park of playing fields, picnic ground, fishing pier and sailing center, with the fort at its heart and the Eisenhower House working as an event venue on the bluff south of the walls.

## 4. What's still there
- The main work — the granite-and-brick Third System fort itself: ramparts, two levels of casemates in places, powder magazines, guns and cannons, and the underground tunnels (guided tours only), around a large central parade.
- The wider district — outworks, 2 redoubts including the southern redoubt, 6 Endicott-period batteries, officers' quarters and 2 brick warehouses.
- The Eisenhower House — the 1873 commandant's residence on a 4.7-acre bluff site south of the fort, now an event venue; whether it is open for regular public tours is not established from current official pages.
- The setting — the park offers, in the state parks' words, an exceptional panoramic view of both Newport Harbor and the East Passage of Narragansett Bay; the park also holds playing fields, a fishing pier, picnic areas, a saltwater bathing beach and Sail Newport's sailing center.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Arrive at the park Visitor Center at the north end of the state park, where the parking is free. Take the guided tour if the day allows it — it is the only route into the tunnels and the guided-only areas, runs about 90 minutes, and goes, in the Trust's phrase, from the ramparts to the underground tunnels; book ahead and arrive 15 minutes before departure. The self-guided tour (entry before 3:00 P.M.) covers the parade field, select casemates and the scenic overlooks in 60–75 minutes. Read the fort in its layers: the 1799 name and First System origin, Bernard's Third System granite, the Endicott batteries of a later era, and the officers' row and commandant's house outside the walls. Finish at the water: the harbor mouth and the East Passage are the reason all of it is here. Seasonal water shuttles connect the fort with downtown Newport and Jamestown in season.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The fort's name is corrected in this entry: it honors President John Adams (1799), not John Quincy Adams, on the National Register nomination's own statement. Eisenhower's connection is likewise corrected: no source found places him in command of Fort Adams; the documented tie is the summer White House use of the commandant's quarters in 1958 and 1960. A further Newport summer in 1957, mentioned by one secondary source, is not established from primary sources checked.

The end of military service does not reconcile cleanly across sources: secondary accounts give an active Army post until 1950; the National Park Service's 2015 environmental assessment states the Army used the fort until 1953, when the Navy took it over; Rhode Island State Parks says the fort served the Navy for ten years before the 1965 deed to the state — while a secondary record for the Eisenhower House gives a Navy transfer in 1964. Each source's statement is reported here rather than smoothed into one date; the deed year is stated as 1965 on the state's own page.

Acreage figures conflict: the National Register district is approximately 80 acres; local history describes the fort at 105 acres; the state park's acreage is not established from the current state page checked. Civil War service is supported at the level of garrisons in secondary accounts; unit-level or event-level Civil War detail was not verified from NPS or Trust sources and none is asserted. Park grounds hours and any park entry or parking fee are not stated on the state page checked and are not established; older third-party claims of sunrise-to-sunset hours were not used. Guided-tour departure times and the full ticket price schedule live in the Trust's booking flow and vary by date; only the published "from" prices and the Visitor Center and self-guided hours are established here.
