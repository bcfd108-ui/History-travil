# Spring Hill ranch work: the showplace and the work behind it
Local research draft • KS OP02 • researched 20 November 2026 • not a published route or accepted field test

## 1. Trip gate
**Visitable anchor:** Spring Hill ranch headquarters at Tallgrass Prairie National Preserve. Drive to designated visitor parking and take the short public walk to the house and barn when open. This is the historic headquarters, not an invitation onto neighboring ranch land. [7–9] NPS's seasonal schedule lists the visitor center November–March, Wednesday–Sunday, 9:00–4:30; closed Monday–Tuesday. Historic buildings have a separate daily winter schedule, 9:00–4:30, except designated holidays. Thanksgiving November 26 and Christmas December 25, 2026 are closed. These are published operating hours, not confirmation of today's unlocked doors. [1–2]

Winter weather can prevent safe building access; the operator maintains entrance/parking as conditions permit, but does not maintain winter hiking trails. ## 2. The story
**An impressive house was also a system of daily work.** Stephen F. Jones, Louisa Jones and their daughter Loutie arrived in Kansas in 1878. NPS interprets their 1881 house through its service spaces: food moved from a basement kitchen toward the dining room through a pantry, short stairs and a dumbwaiter. Later occupants changed that arrangement. This is modern institutional interpretation, not a recovered servant's diary. It supports a household-work story but does not identify who performed each task. [4]

The dated primary object is L. H. Everts & Co.'s **1887 atlas lithograph**, inspected directly. It names S. F. Jones as proprietor and presents a lavish house, large barn, windmill, smaller buildings, walls, planted grounds and a horse-drawn vehicle. Its emphasis is ownership and order; its small human figures do not supply employee identities. Read it as a public image of a prosperous enterprise, not a measured plan or a neutral account of working conditions. [5]

NPS dates the barn to early 1882, rather than simply assigning the house's 1881 date to everything. Its levels accommodated animals, equipment and stored hay/grain. The household and barn together make the stop about repeated labor, not just an owner's architecture. [6]

A later household voice makes that labor visible. The 2004 NPS Cultural Landscape Report records Erma Slabaugh’s recollection that ranch hands tended gardens east of the barn, near the parking lot described in the report. Hazel Slabaugh worked on the ranch, and their family lived in the headquarters house. This is testimony mediated by the report, not an original interview recording. [10]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** the 1887 lithograph integrates residence and working buildings in one composed view. **Now:** NPS interprets the surviving house and barn separately as parts of the ranch complex. The artwork permits a broad building-form comparison, not a matched-camera claim. No present-day comparison photograph, exact sightline, measured overlay or safe image-recreation position was verified. The windmill's depiction is historical evidence, not a claim that it survives. [4–6]

## 4. What's still there
The historic house and barn are interpreted public buildings. NPS identifies an accessible main-level house entrance using a sidewalk/ramp and barn main-floor ramps; that does not establish access to every floor. Do not use ladders, closed stairs or work areas. The concrete parking area west of the barn has gates that close at 4:30. Weather may change the usable approach. [7]

Ownership is not uniform: NPS's partner record lists 35 federal acres around headquarters/school and The Nature Conservancy as the primary landowner of the wider preserve. The 2002 transfer record describes a 32.5-acre donation. These are differently dated figures, not a boundary survey or permission to enter all privately owned preserve land. [8–9]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Start at the visitor center when open to establish that day's admitted buildings and safe approach. Visit the publicly open house main level, using interpretation of food movement rather than seeking closed service spaces. Continue only to the admitted barn main floor and compare the scale of domestic service with animal and storage work. [2,4,7]

**Strongest story moment:** reread the 1887 owner-centered image after seeing the work buildings. Its immaculate estate required effort the image barely names. Set the owner's self-portrait beside Slabaugh’s recollection of land worked by ranch hands. If winter closes the buildings, use the digitized original from home instead; no substitute private-property route.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The Slabaugh recollection was read in the published report; no underlying audio, payroll, household diary or measured original building plan was inspected. The named household comes from NPS interpretation; the directly read period evidence is the 1887 image. No invented worker quotation. House 1881 and barn early 1882 are separate construction dates.
