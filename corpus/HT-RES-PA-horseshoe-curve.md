# HT-RES-PA-horseshoe-curve — Four tracks bending around a point of rock that would not move
Division PA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A working main line that doubles back on itself in a 220-degree bend — built in 1854 so a locomotive could climb the Allegheny Front on a grade it could actually pull, instead of being winched up a staircase of inclined planes.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a look, not a ride: you stand on designated visitor ground at the apex of the Curve and watch trains work a curve that is still in daily service. The railroad itself is an active Norfolk Southern main line — the Pittsburgh Line — and the boundary is explicit: the Curve is viewed from the visitor center and trackside observation area only; the tracks, roadbed, and right-of-way are a working railroad, not a trail. The visitor facilities are operated by the Railroaders Memorial Museum of Altoona, the railroad-heritage nonprofit that also collaborated with the National Park Service on the early-1990s renovation of the site and the visitor center dedicated in April 1992. The primary records consulted for this draft — the National Historic Landmark nomination and the Pennsylvania Historical and Museum Commission account — name no hours or fees, and none are printed here. The site was designated a National Historic Landmark and listed on the National Register of Historic Places on November 13, 1966 (NRHP 66000647).

## 2. The story
Pennsylvania's first answer to the Allegheny Mountains was the state-owned Main Line of Public Works: canals east and west, joined across the mountains from 1834 by the Allegheny Portage Railroad's ten inclined planes. It worked, but slowly and dangerously — the PHMC account calls the mountain crossing in boats on flatbed railcars, pulled first by mules and then by stationary steam engines, "lengthy and hazardous" — and through the 1840s passengers and freight crossing the mountains still depended on it.

The Pennsylvania Railroad was chartered to replace that chain with one continuous railroad. Its chief engineer, John Edgar Thomson — the nomination and PHMC both carry him as J. Edgar Thomson, chief engineer and later president of the company — faced the grade problem west of Altoona: the Allegheny Front rises too steeply for a locomotive of the period to climb head-on. Thomson's solution was to gain the height gradually, on a grade of less than 2 percent that, in the PHMC account, "a train of average length could manage with a helper locomotive," rising 122 feet through the Curve itself.

The ground fought back. The NHL nomination describes the engineers locating their line through Logan's Narrows, five miles southwest of Altoona, and meeting a cul-de-sac at the foot of Kittanning Point. Building the Curve required three works at once: fill Kittanning Run, which formed a deep ravine on the north side of the valley; blast away the face of Kittanning Point; and fill the second ravine on the south side, formed by Glenwhite Run (the nomination also spells it Burgeon's Run). Irish laborers from Counties Cork, Mayo, and Antrim, living in camps along the line, did the work with picks and shovels; the earth and rock they cut away went by mule-drawn cart into the two ravine fills.

The result is the geometry the nomination records: a curve 2,375 feet long with a central angle of 220 degrees, its northern end at an altitude of about 1,594 feet and its southern end at about 1,716 feet — the 122-foot rise in the PHMC account. The line ran up one side of the valley, turned left across the fills to Kittanning Point, and turned left again, in the shape that gave it its name. It was first laid with two tracks; the nomination, written when four were in place, records that it had grown to four.

The Horseshoe Curve opened on February 15, 1854 — the date carried by both the PHMC account and the National Park Service's Allegheny Portage Railroad history, which states plainly that on that day the Pennsylvania Railroad "completed the Horseshoe Curve and, thus, its all rail line from Philadelphia to Pittsburgh." Travel time between Philadelphia and Pittsburgh fell from four days on the State Works to approximately fifteen hours. The all-rail line took the mountain traffic away from the inclined planes it had been built to replace: within three years the Commonwealth sold the Main Line system to the Pennsylvania Railroad (purchase June 25, 1857; possession August 1, 1857), and the Portage Railroad era ended. The Curve, by contrast, never stopped working — it passed from the Pennsylvania Railroad to Penn Central (the owner named in the NHL nomination), then to Conrail, and it is operated today by Norfolk Southern.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a two-track ledge cut into Kittanning Point in 1852–54, the ravines of Kittanning Run and Glenwhite Run filled with spoil hauled in mule carts, Irish camp labor working with picks and shovels. **Now:** the same alignment, widened over time — four tracks when the landmark nomination was written, three tracks in current operation — carrying Norfolk Southern freight over the same 220-degree bend, watched from a visitor observation area at its apex.

**Then:** the second half of a four-day Philadelphia-to-Pittsburgh journey that began on a canal and crossed the mountains by stationary-engine winch. **Now:** one curve on a continuous railroad — the fifteen-hour journey the Curve made possible in 1854 has only shortened since, and the Curve itself is the part of that railroad people drive out of their way to stand beside.

## 4. What's still there
- The Curve itself — 2,375 feet of active main-line track bending 220 degrees around Kittanning Point, in continuous railroad use since February 15, 1854
- The engineered ground the nomination describes: the blasted face of Kittanning Point and the fills across the Kittanning Run and Glenwhite Run ravines
- The observation park at the apex of the Curve, between the tracks and the road below — a spectator ground the nomination already records, with a steam locomotive and caboose on permanent display at the time it was written
- The visitor center at the Curve, dedicated in April 1992 after the early-1990s renovation and operated by the Railroaders Memorial Museum
- The reservoirs east of the Curve flowing into Lake Altoona, recorded in the nomination's description of the terrain
- The heavily wooded, mountainous terrain the nomination notes was "still as heavily wooded and as mountainous as it was in 1853"

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the visitor center:** Start with the exhibits on why the Curve had to exist — the grade problem, the Portage Railroad it replaced, and the Altoona–Johnstown line it completes.
2. **At the trackside observation area at the apex:** Watch a westbound train take the full 220 degrees — the front and rear of a long train visible at once, on two different headings, gaining the 122 feet the whole work was built to gain.
3. **Reading the ground from the apex:** Find the three works the nomination names — the Kittanning Run fill, the cut face of Kittanning Point, and the Glenwhite Run fill — in the one view.
4. **The paired day, as the records shape it:** Horseshoe Curve → Allegheny Portage Railroad National Historic Site. The Portage is the system the Curve replaced; the two grounds are twelve miles and twenty years apart, and each is its own record.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The track count does not hold still, and this draft does not pretend it does: the Curve was laid with two tracks, the NHL nomination records four, and current descriptions of the Norfolk Southern line record three. Each figure belongs to its own date; none is printed here as timeless.

The grade is carried two ways in the sources: the PHMC account says the route rose "at a practical grade of less than 2%," and secondary summaries of railroad figures variously give the Curve's own grade as 1.45 percent (Pennsylvania Railroad) and 1.34 percent (Norfolk Southern). The primary records consulted for this draft do not settle a single figure for the Curve itself, so none is asserted.

The PHMC account states that "in 1967, the National Park Service named the Horseshoe Curve a National Historic Site." The NPS nomination record itself carries the designation as a National Historic Landmark on November 13, 1966, with listing on the National Register the same day. The two statements conflict; the nomination record is the one printed in the locator below, and the PHMC wording is preserved here as a dispute, not adopted.

No Historic American Engineering Record survey of Horseshoe Curve was found in the Library of Congress Pictures collection in this check — the HAER documentation located for this pair of entries covers the Allegheny Portage Railroad works (see the companion entry), not the Curve. The first photo below is therefore a Library of Congress panoramic photograph, not a HAER frame, and is labeled as such. No coordinates are printed: the nomination marks its location "not for publication" and carries no latitude or longitude.
