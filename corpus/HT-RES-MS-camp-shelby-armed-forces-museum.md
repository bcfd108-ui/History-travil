# Camp Shelby through the experience of the people sent there

*On Old Ground · Mississippi OP25 · Labeled research draft · NO-ANCHOR · Quiet reflection.*

## 2. Story
The Army’s World War I order of battle gives Camp Shelby’s establishment date as July 18, 1917. It was built as a cantonment for the 38th Division, which occupied the camp into 1918. Men arrived in large groups, notably from Indiana and Kentucky. The Army’s roster records Maj. Gen. William H. Sage among the commanders. They were part of the effort to turn a new camp into a place where soldiers could be housed and trained at scale. The records show the administrative work, although they tell less about the people building or keeping the camp running. [2]

The post took on several roles in World War II, serving as a training place, hospital and prisoner-of-war site. The Mississippi state Blue Book records the presence of the 442nd Regimental Combat Team and Women’s Army Corps units. For an individual trained here, what the camp meant could depend on where he entered the ranks. Ted Tsukiyama recalled in a Densho interview, “And yet when I went to Shelby, I’m back to buck private again.” He compared his position to the responsibility he had held in ROTC and spoke about tensions between arriving Hawaiʻi volunteers and mainland cadre. The remark is a veteran’s account of his own experience. It does not describe all soldiers’ experiences. [3, 4, 5]

A wartime booklet preserved by Densho tells another version of the same ground: it introduces the training of the Hawaiʻi recruits as it was presented at the time. The booklet was a publication for an audience, not the equivalent of an informal conversation decades later. A company portrait archived separately documents men at the camp in 1943. Read together, these records preserve a public organization, faces and an individual memory of rank. [4–6]

The state history describes periodic training uses after World War II, National Guard activity in 1954 and designation as a permanent training site in 1956. The museum tells the history of Mississippi service members inside the modern installation. The present maintenance notice closes that public option for this dossier. The separate East Sixth Street USO documents the history of Hattiesburg’s segregated recreation facility, which already has its own dossier and entrance. [1, 3, 7]
