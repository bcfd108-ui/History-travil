# Landmark Park and the Wiregrass buildings that were carried forward

*On Old Ground · Alabama OP01 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
The Waddell house was a working family’s home before it became the center of a museum farm. The park’s research follows Leska H. Waddell from his parents’ family in Dale County to the house he bought near Dothan in 1906. It reproduces a federal census occupational description of his father Sidney as a “saw mill man”. The park finds his sons listed as lumber cutters or haulers. The line about Sidney’s occupation is a reported census transcription. The inference that he owned the mill remains the park researchers’ interpretation. [2]

Leska married Mattie Gypsy Hall in 1909. The operator’s account describes grain, peanut and cotton growing, rented acreage and money lent on credit. It also records family traditions about Gypsy’s butter beans and Leska’s resistance to modernizing the house. That account identifies more kinds of work than can be seen in a single piece of machinery. The name of a household owner survives in records more readily than the names of the tenant families who farmed its land. [2]

The building worked with the weather. Raised masonry supports admitted air underneath; opposing openings and a large central hall could move air through the house. A visitor following that hall can see how the building was meant to be used. It stood on another property and was moved to the park after the family donated it in 1978. Landmark’s mission brought rural structures into a new arrangement for teaching and preservation. [2, 3]

Brown’s Crossroads School came from a separate community in Dale County. The park traces it from rural one-room teaching to church use and later restoration. The school closed in 1929 during consolidation; Richard Douglas Brown donated the building on the family’s behalf in 1991. Its presence beside a farmstead in the museum does not put the Waddells’ farm and the school at one original site. [3, 4]

## 3. Then and now
The present museum landscape deliberately places working-farm interpretation, relocated buildings and artifacts together. The Waddell house and Brown’s Crossroads schoolhouse retain separate histories. Period construction, later repairs, a moved structure and new museum interpretation are all visible in a single visit. Alabama’s published government manual identifies the Alabama Agricultural Museum development as based at Landmark Park. [1–5]

## 5. Public progression
Enter Landmark Park through admissions. Follow the signed farmstead walk and begin at the Waddell house’s raised base. If the door is open, see how the long hall connects the two ends. Continue to the public schoolhouse area when open. End at **One Room, Several Ages**, considering Brown’s teacher’s work dividing one room among children of different ages and how a rural house and a school shaped two different kinds of daily work. [1–4]
