## 2. The story
Four lightbulbs are an unusually modest object to carry a major industrial turning point. On December 20, 1951, EBR-I demonstrated usable electricity from nuclear energy; the following day it supplied the building and parking-lot lights. The visitor story is peaceful power becoming visible in familiar objects, not a technical recipe. INL's published museum account distinguishes this experiment from a modern commercial power station. [6]

The strongest first-person source found is **Leonard Koch**, associate project engineer, interviewed by **Rick Michal** in *Nuclear News*, November 2001, pp. 30–35, hosted by Argonne. Koch described himself as: **“I'm not a theoretical engineer. I'm a designer/builder.”** This retrospective interview describes ten Argonne technical staff relocating to Idaho, local technicians and builders, and punishing travel/work days. He identifies **Walter Zinn** as project director, **Harold Lichtenberger** as project engineer and **Mike Novick** as senior engineer. It is a published first-hand recollection, not a contemporaneous diary; we have not interviewed Koch or heard an original unedited recording. [7]

The museum's interpretation also opens a question about whose work receives recognition. Its guide says the men signed the wall after the milestone, while a plaque recognizing the women was added in 1995. It identifies an original bulb within the display case, distinct from the demonstration string. That distinction gives visitors both a real artifact and a reason to look beyond the celebrated image. [8]

Land context must not begin with the laboratory. The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes' own account describes their continuing people, government and Fort Hall homeland. Use that published voice as regional context, not as a fabricated EBR-I-specific oral tradition or implied Tribal endorsement of this interpretation. A site-specific Tribal account is an open research gap. [9]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** A remote experimental workplace, assembling a team and a new kind of demonstration. The point was proving an outcome; it was not an ordinary household electricity service. Koch's account locates the achievement in design, construction and sustained human effort rather than a single heroic instant. [7]

**Now:** A preserved, decommissioned museum. INL's cultural-resource account says the building dates to 1949–1951 and public access followed decontamination in 1975. It identifies the landmark boundary as reactor, building and guardhouse, and notes a 2023 preservation plan. Preservation and public interpretation are not evidence that the surrounding laboratory campus is open. [10]

The building's physical survival lets a future visit move from a broad technological claim to small, checkable details: who did the work, what object is original, and how the event was recorded. That is the proposed interpretive comparison, not a claim that any display was personally inspected this season.
