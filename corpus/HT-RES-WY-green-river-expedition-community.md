## 2. The story

**Original narration:** A departure can become a heroic portrait so quickly that the people doing the daily work disappear. Green River offers a better opening: a crew assembled, equipment had to be supplied, and someone other than the leader kept a record. Read that person's doubts beside the triumphant image. The expedition becomes a human undertaking rather than a single man's destiny.

**George Y. Bradley** is the named firsthand voice. PBS's *American Experience* reproduces his **June 13, 1869** journal complaint: **“We remain in camp today for we are tired out from the effects of constant hard labor.”** He also questioned provisions and judgment. This is a later downstream entry, not a statement uttered at the Wyoming departure site. It is used here as the crew's perspective on an expedition that began at Green River, not as a reason to reenact its journey. The published extract was read; no original diary page or audio was inspected. [3]

The **New York Public Library** independently catalogs Bradley's diary under **MssCol 364**, one volume, and identifies the originals as held by the Library of Congress. Its record points to publication in *Utah Historical Quarterly* XV (1947), pages 31–72. The repository chain is therefore more specific than an unattributed internet quotation, while still falling short of collation against the autograph. [4]

The **Utah Geological Survey** dates the 1869 expedition's Green River Station departure to **May 24**. That is the historical embarkation community, not the location of today's museum. [5]

The **Eastern Shoshone Tribe's published history** starts from continuing traditional homelands and discusses reservation reduction in the era of railroad expansion. It provides a necessary regional corrective to the idea that federal exploration entered an empty place. This is the Tribe's own institutional framing, not a declaration that one Nation represents every community along the river. Nor does that broad account prove a particular camp, encounter, or local territorial boundary. [6]

**Interpretive conclusion:** Bradley's fatigue does not cancel scientific work; Indigenous continuity does not need an explorer's permission to exist. A useful visit holds both truths without making either a decorative footnote. The question is not merely who received public credit, but whose work and knowledge the surviving record allows us to hear.

## 3. Then / now

- **1869 expedition versus 1871–72 expedition:** they must not be merged. USGS explicitly notes the absence of artists and photographers on the first journey and distinguishes the later expedition's visual record. A photograph labeled 1871 is not a photograph of Bradley's 1869 departure. [7]
- **Departure community versus interpretive building:** today's museum occupies a renovated **1931 post office**; the institution was established in **1967**. Neither date belongs to the expedition's original embarkation. [8]
- **Journal versus commemorative story:** the diary offers one participant's viewpoint, not a complete account of every crew member or affected Nation. The present museum can place that viewpoint within a wider community history.
