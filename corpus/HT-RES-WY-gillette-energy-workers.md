## 2. The story
**Named worker testimony:** a CNN field-reporting/opinion article republished by KVIA on **March 16, 2021** quotes **Steve Gray**, identified there as a Gillette resident who had worked in oil and coal and experienced layoffs. The reporter describes visiting after Gray contacted CNN. Gray says, **“People are getting left behind”**. This is a short first-person statement published within journalism, not a cataloged oral-history interview or a current assessment of his circumstances. [3]

The same report quotes retired coal miner **Doug Wood** and community figures, showing that residents did not express one uniform view. Its author explicitly argues for a particular approach to energy transition. Use the named recollections as dated testimony, not the article's advocacy as a neutral consensus or a forecast proved true. [3]

**Original interpretive angle:** the history of an energy town is not exhausted by the amount of fuel it produced. A worker also has a household, a sense of belonging and a future that may depend on decisions made elsewhere. The visitor's question is not “Where can I watch this industry operate?” but “What did building a life around this work mean?”

A museum encounter can hold achievement and uncertainty together. Respect for people's work does not require endorsing every company decision, and discussion of environmental change does not require making workers into villains. Neither position is a substitute for listening carefully to a particular person's words in their historical setting.

This draft does not repeat private family detail from the report or infer anyone's present employment, finances or political beliefs. It does not generalize one person into all of Gillette. The aim is a historically dated human entry, not a current economic forecast.

## 4. What's still there
The county describes permanent and changing local-history exhibitions at the museum. That supports the independent public interpretation anchor. This dossier does not require industrial footage, machinery explanation or access beyond admitted public galleries. [5]

The museum's **Past Exhibits** page documents **Women Coal Miners of the Powder River Basin, July–December 2013**, featuring photographs by **Annalise Shingler**. This is an important lead toward a broader worker history, but not proof of a current exhibit, a currently available photograph or permission to reproduce the portraits. Its historical workforce percentage is not carried forward as a current statistic. [6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Museum version, editorial allowance 35–50 minutes:**
1. Confirm actual opening and enter the public museum door. Stay within admitted interior galleries.
2. Begin with any available local-history interpretation of the community, without promising a particular object or current energy exhibit.
3. Read Gray's short attributed statement and identify the 2021 reporting context. Ask what a worker's testimony adds that a production figure does not.
4. Introduce the earlier archive as an explicitly unopened evidence lead. Discuss what questions a future transcript review might answer, without claiming answers already found.
5. Note the museum's documented past women-miners exhibition and the still-missing named firsthand material.
6. **End at the strongest story moment:** the history of energy is also the history of people trying to sustain a community, and those people deserve names rather than being reduced to a boom-or-bust label.

**Documentary version, editorial allowance 20–30 minutes:** compare the dated article, archive description and past-exhibition record from their public pages. No drive to a mine, worker's home, company gate or reporting location is necessary.

Times are editorial allowances, not scheduled museum programs. No staff interview, industrial tour, technical explanation or route activation is included. If a gallery is unavailable, change the reading sequence, not the access boundary.
