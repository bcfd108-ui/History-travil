# Beauregard-Keyes House — a raised Creole-American villa, its enslaved quarters, a Sicilian family's stand, and a novelist's rescue on Chartres Street
HT-RES-LA-beauregard-keyes-house · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The house at 1113 Chartres Street was built in 1826 on land the Ursuline nuns sold off in parcels in 1825, and almost every generation of New Orleans since has left a named layer in it. Auctioneer Joseph LeCarpentier commissioned the design from François Correjolles and the building from James Lambert. The National Register nomination — filed under the house's full historic name, LeCarpentier-Beauregard-Keyes House — and the Society of Architectural Historians' Archipedia entry describe the same deliberate mixture: a raised house of plastered brick combining the American central-hall plan with the Creole open gallery, or loggia (now enclosed), flanked by cabinets at the rear, fronted by a raised, pedimented portico on four Tuscan columns reached by twin curved granite staircases. The nomination dates the front gallery to 1827 and the curved granite stairs to about 1850. The operator calls the result an outstanding example of a raised center-hall villa, with both Creole and American features in an elaborately detailed residence. Correjolles, the operator notes, designed at least seventeen other structures in the Quarter. LeCarpentier's family tie outlasted his ownership: his daughter Louise married Michel Alonzo Morphy in 1829, and their son, born June 22, 1837 — Charles Paul Morphy, known as Paul Morphy — became world chess champion; the house's street marker identifies LeCarpentier as the champion's grandfather. Nothing in the record inspected shows the adult Morphy living in this house, a point that matters to the lore section below. [1–5, 7]

In 1833 LeCarpentier sold the house to John A. Merle, who became Swiss consul to New Orleans; his wife Anaïs Merle laid out the house's first parterre garden beside it. In 1841 Josephine Laveau Trudeau, widow of Manuel Andry, bought house and garden from Merle's creditors, and she and her daughter's family — Adonai Andry and L. Armand Garidel, in the house next door — maintained and improved the garden until the end of the Civil War. The garden visible today is a restoration, not a survival: the nomination records that the original garden's plans survived, that a later building on the garden site was bought and demolished in 1952, and that Koch and Wilson restored the garden for the Keyes Foundation from an 1865 drawing in the city's Notarial Archives — the Archipedia entry dates that drawing's design to the period it depicts and notes the walled garden can be viewed from Chartres Street. [2, 4, 5]

Behind the main house stands the dependency, and the operator's slavery history for this address is direct. Enslaved people of African descent lived at BK House from the time it was built until, most likely, 1864, when Louisiana's state constitution first abolished slavery following the Emancipation Proclamation of 1863. They lived in the second story of the quarters behind the house — a building that still stands — and worked in the kitchen and courtyard below. The operator states that it is very likely the laborers who built the house in 1826 were themselves enslaved men, and prints the archive's limit plainly: because enslaved people were rarely taught to read or write, far too little is known of their personal thoughts and experiences here, and what is known comes through the official documents that recorded their ownership. That framing — a standing quarters building, a documented span of 1826 to about 1864, and an admitted silence where individual voices should be — is the house's own, and this dossier keeps it intact rather than decorating it. [2]

The name the house carries first belongs to a tenant who never owned it. Dominique Lanata, a Genoese businessman, bought the house from Madame Garidel on October 28, 1865, as an investment and rental. Returning from the Civil War in late 1865, General Pierre Gustave Toutant Beauregard rented the entire house from Lanata and lived here with his two sons — his second wife, Caroline Deslonde, had died during the war, and her family's Esplanade Avenue mansion, which the couple had shared, had been sold at auction by her heirs. Beauregard's tenancy ran, on the operator's account, from late 1865 into 1868 (the Archipedia entry gives eighteen months within 1866–1868), and it was this postwar residence — a defeated general rebuilding a civilian life as a railroad executive in a rented Creole-American villa — that later preservationists took as the house's memorial subject. [2, 3, 5]

The layer the house's name leaves out is the one that nearly fills the next sixty years. A Lanata relative was living here by 1873; the Lanata heirs sold in 1904 to Corrado Giacona, whose Sicilian family had likely been tenants since the late 1890s. Corrado, his father Pietro and his mother Maria Crocefissa turned the ground-level basement into a wine cellar for their wholesale wine and liquor business, Giacona & Co.; Corrado and his wife Rosena Punzo raised eight children born in this house, and the family worshipped at St. Mary's Italian Church across the street, then the center of Italian life in the French Quarter. Their success drew extortion: letters demanding money arrived from men the sensational press of the day called the “Black Hand.” The operator's Giacona history, drawn in part from neighbor testimony, recounts that Pietro finally admitted four of the men to dinner; that they drank generously and refused to leave; and that at two o'clock in the morning Pietro and Corrado shot the four men on the back gallery — three killed, the fourth fleeing and later found by police and taken to hospital. Father and son were charged, and the case was dismissed on grounds of self-defense in 1910, with prominent Italians publicly supporting them. Contemporary newspaper retellings place the confrontation on the night of June 17, 1908, with times and details varying between accounts — the operator's page gives no calendar date, and the discrepancy in the hour (an evening arrival in the newspaper version, a 2 a.m. escalation in the operator's) is printed in §6. The Giaconas stayed, living and working in the house, until they sold in 1925 and moved to Esplanade Avenue. A documented immigrant family's home, business, and armed refusal of extortion — in the same rooms the tours present under a general's and a novelist's names — is part of this ground's record, not a footnote to it. [2, 3]

What followed was the familiar Quarter sequence of threat, rescue and partial use. The operator's timeline records severe disrepair in the early twentieth century and a rescue purchase on July 8, 1926, by architect General Allison Owen, which gave Beauregard House, Incorporated — formed to preserve the house as a memorial to Beauregard — time to stabilize it; other accounts of the 1925 sale describe a new owner intending demolition for factories, and the two accounts are not reconciled in the sources inspected (§6). The memorial plan stalled; for years the house was partly occupied by Warrington House, a home for homeless men, and by Alcoholics Anonymous, with only enough repair to keep it from ruin. It had already been documented, in March and April 1934, by Richard Koch's camera for the Historic American Buildings Survey — the survey record that still anchors its photo file (§7). [2]

The second name is the rescuer who finished the job. Novelist Frances Parkinson Keyes — born in Charlottesville in 1885, author of more than fifty books including the 1948 New Orleans murder mystery *Dinner at Antoine's* — first visited New Orleans for Carnival in 1940 and took up this house in the mid-1940s. The state encyclopedia's entry on Keyes says she rented and later purchased the house in 1945; the Archipedia entry gives a lease running 1944 to 1955, restoration begun with architect Richard Koch in 1945, and the Keyes Foundation created in 1955, acquiring ownership at her death; the operator's own timeline credits architect Sam Wilson with helping her restoration and dates her era 1945–1970. The three accounts conflict on the purchase date and on the restoration architect, and §6 prints them side by side without choosing. What is consistent is the work and the ending: Keyes restored house and garden, wrote here — including *Madame Castel's Lodger* (1962), her novel of Beauregard in this very house — established the Keyes Foundation to hold the property, and died here on July 3, 1970. The Foundation has operated the house as a museum since 1970, presenting it restored to its Victorian character, with Beauregard family material, Keyes's studio, and her collections of dolls and rare porcelain veilleuses, and the house now also hosts the Nous Foundation's French and Creole cultural programming. The National Register listed the house on November 20, 1975. [2, 4–6]

**Lore, kept separate.** Ghost-tour and popular retellings market this house as haunted, most elaborately with a claim that the Battle of Shiloh is re-enacted in the main hall — soldiers, cannon fire and the smell of blood — and with a story that Paul Morphy went mad here and attacked passersby. Both tellings are lore: they are sourced here only to the popular accounts that circulate them, the Morphy story is contradicted by the record inspected, which shows no adult Morphy residence in this house at all, and no documented event in the operator, nomination or encyclopedia record supports either telling. The documented violence of this address — the 1908 gallery shootings — is in the story above, with its court outcome, and needs no embellishment. [8]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** 1113 Chartres Street, between Ursulines and Governor Nicholls Streets, directly opposite the Old Ursuline Convent in the French Quarter, New Orleans, Orleans Parish — listed in the National Register as the LeCarpentier-Beauregard-Keyes House and a contributing ground of the Vieux Carré. The operator's home page calls the house a National Historic Landmark; the federal record inspected lists it in the National Register without an individual landmark designation, a conflict printed in §6. [1, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** BK Historic House & Gardens, 1113 Chartres St., New Orleans, LA 70116, 504-523-7257 — guided-tour museum entry only, at the published tour times. The walled parterre garden can also be viewed from the Chartres Street sidewalk without entry, per the Archipedia entry; the quarters, courtyard and interiors are tour ground only. [1, 5]

## 5. Public progression

Arrive for an on-the-hour tour — 10, 11, 1, 2 or 3 on the operator's open days; check the day itself, because the current schedule closes Wednesdays and Sundays — and take the house in the order its names omit as well as the order they proclaim. Begin at the sidewalk marker and the portico with Correjolles's 1826 hybrid, a Creole gallery carried on an American hall plan. **Strongest story moment · The quarters above the kitchen.** From the courtyard, the house's first forty years read in section: work below, sleep above, and a record that preserves the arrangement precisely because it preserves almost nothing of the people who lived it — the operator's own admission, printed on its history page, that far too little is known of their thoughts because they were kept from reading and writing. Then walk the names in sequence: Beauregard the tenant, rebuilding a life he did not own the walls of; the Giaconas' basement wine cellar and back gallery, where an immigrant family defended the same house with rifles and were cleared by a court; and Keyes's studio, where the novelist who saved the building wrote its most famous tenant back into fiction. End in the restored parterre, reading it as what it is — a 1950s act of documentary recovery from an 1865 drawing, which is this house's whole method in one garden. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **“National Historic Landmark” — claimed by the operator, not shown in the federal record inspected.** The operator's home page describes BK House as “a National Historic Landmark built in 1826.” The National Park Service record for this property (NRIS 75000853) shows a National Register listing dated November 20, 1975, with significance in architecture and literature and no individual NHL designation date; the house stands within the Vieux Carré Historic District, itself a National Historic Landmark district (66000377). This dossier follows the federal record and prints the operator's claim as a conflict for Dash review, rather than adopting it. [1, 4]
- **Keyes: purchase date and restoration architect.** The state encyclopedia says Keyes rented and later purchased the house in 1945. SAH Archipedia says she leased it from 1944 to 1955, began restoration with Richard Koch in 1945, created the Keyes Foundation in 1955, and that the Foundation acquired ownership at her death in 1970. The operator's timeline dates her era 1945–1970, says she undertook the restoration “with the help of architect Sam Wilson,” and that she established the Foundation, which has maintained the house as a museum since her death. The garden restoration is separately credited to Koch and Wilson in the nomination. No deed, lease or Foundation instrument inspected in this pass reconciles 1945 purchase vs. 1955 foundation vs. 1970 acquisition, or Koch vs. Wilson as the house restoration's architect. All three accounts are printed; the story above adopts none exclusively. [2, 4–6]
- **The 1925–1926 rescue.** The operator states the house was saved when General Allison Owen purchased it on July 8, 1926, for the Beauregard House, Incorporated stabilization. Popular preservation accounts describe the 1925 Giacona sale as bringing an owner who intended demolition for factories, prompting the preservation response. The sources inspected do not name the intermediate owner or join the two accounts into one chain of title; both are printed as found. [2]
- **The 1908 shootings — date, hour and toll.** The operator's Giacona page gives no calendar date, places the escalation at 2 a.m. after a dinner, and records three men killed and a fourth fleeing wounded, with the case dismissed as self-defense in 1910. A contemporary-press-based retelling dates the confrontation to the night of June 17, 1908, with the men arriving about 9 p.m. The operator's is the fuller institutional account and is followed in the story; the newspaper-derived date is carried here as unverified against a primary court or police record, none of which was inspected in this pass. Names of the dead also vary in spelling across retellings and are therefore not listed in this dossier. [3]
- **Beauregard's tenancy, 1865–1868 or eighteen months.** The operator has Beauregard renting from late 1865, after his return from the war, and leaving in 1868; Archipedia gives eighteen months within 1866–1868. The span is printed as 1865/66–1868 in the locator tradition (the street marker reads 1866–1868); no lease was inspected. [2, 5, 7]
- **Hours have changed across listings.** Older museum listings give guided tours Monday–Saturday, 10 a.m.–3 p.m. The operator's current home page gives Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, Friday and Saturday only, tours at 10, 11, 1, 2 and 3, with dated seasonal closures. The operator's page governs the trip gate; the older pattern is recorded here so a stale listing is not mistaken for a closure error. [1]
- **The enslaved record is an owners' record.** As at the sister Quarter houses, the people enslaved here between 1826 and about 1864 are known through documents of ownership, not through their own writing; the operator publishes no names for this house in the pages inspected, and none are supplied here. Research is stated by the operator to be ongoing. [2]
