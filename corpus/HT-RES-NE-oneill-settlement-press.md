# O’Neill — the immigrant circular and a town’s newspaper record

DRAFTED · Nebraska OP02 · Pending independent Dash QC

## 2. The story

### Before the pamphlet, a request for facts

From Sioux City on November 12, 1874, John O’Neill wrote a circular soliciting information on northern Nebraska counties. The *Central Union Agriculturist and Missouri Valley Farmer* printed it in its December issue, and the Nebraska State Historical Society reproduces the text. “I shall also be pleased to receive reliable information on the subject,” O’Neill wrote while asking for existing pamphlets, newspapers and information. He describes people selecting their claims through the Dakota City land office and returning for their families. The circular shows how an organizer tried to turn local descriptions and personal contacts into a printed recruiting tool. Claims about future migration are the organizer's expectations. [4]

### The price of getting started

O’Neill’s 1875 *Northern Nebraska As a Home for Immigrants* describes northern Nebraska as a destination. The state historical society reproduces his warning that families should not arrive entirely without means. It also reproduces his estimate that a family man needed $500 or more after reaching the West to begin operations. Set this beside the solicitation from 1874. The enticing claims and the cost warning reached readers through the same campaign. The amount is the organizer's advice. The available text gives no family budget survey or verified outcome for those who followed it. The state marker dates the beginnings of the colonizing work to 1874; the pamphlet followed in 1875. [4–6]

### The newspaper became a place to do work

O’Neill’s library genealogy page identifies the *Holt County Record*, beginning with June 16, 1879 in its microfilm list. It also identifies a photograph of Bob and Hap Miles working on the local paper where it operated in the First National Bank building from 1904 to 1955. Library of Congress metadata records *The Frontier* from 1880 to 1965. These references shift the story from promoting a place to making a regular record of its daily affairs. The issue-level leads also let a researcher test when an event first reached print. The scene of printing is part of the documentary story. The modern reading stop is the public library rather than the original press room. [3,7,8]
