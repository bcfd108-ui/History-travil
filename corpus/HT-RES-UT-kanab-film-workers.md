## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** the person who helped create a film's illusion still had a livelihood outside the frame. Kanab's movie history belongs to local labor, not only visiting stars.

**Trevor Kenneth Leach**, interviewed in Kanab by **Marsha Holland on July 17, 2002**, described combining cattle work with movie jobs. On printed pages 22–23 he recalls long working days before union membership, and says, **“Before that we didn't get much pay.”** He also names **Johnny McAllister and his son Ken** as partners in a set-cleanup contract. [6] This is his firsthand testimony in a university-hosted transcript, not independently checked payroll or contract evidence. Relevant text was read; audio was not heard.

The interpretive point is the overlap between existing skills and temporary production work. Ranching, transport, construction and cleanup are not background color when they are how a production happens. This is an original framing, not a claim that every local household had the same experience.

A second named worker, **Marve Adams**, described his local film career in Nicole A. Bonham's 1993 newspaper reporting. The article identifies him as a carpenter who spent 35 years working as a grip and records his recollections of production work. [7] That published firsthand interview material supports a labor-centered reading, although its film chronology contains errors and should not be copied wholesale.

## 3. Then / now

Kanab Museum identifies filmmaking as part of its historical remit and describes its artifact and archive holdings. [8] Its exhibits page currently says there is **no special exhibit**; it does not establish the display of a Leach transcript, Adams photograph, payroll sheet or particular grip tool. [9] Bring the published account as context rather than promise a specific object on a wall.

At Little Hollywood, the operator describes actual film-set material, including the homestead set from *The Outlaw Josey Wales*. [3] Regional tourism calls the presentation a reconstructed old-town setting assembled from brought-together sets. [4] These descriptions can coexist: an authentic set component can be relocated and arranged within a later museum landscape. Neither makes the museum yard the original filming location or an authentic nineteenth-century settlement.

Historic ranches, old sets, canyon locations and hotel rooms mentioned in film histories are not added stops. A title's location credit is evidence of past production, not current public access.

## 4. What's still there

- **Public scheduled interior:** Kanab Museum, subject to the operator's holiday and opening rules. [1]
- **Conditional public interpretive yard:** Little Hollywood, only during independently confirmed opening and within marked visitor areas. Its set structures are exhibits, not invitations to climb, enter closed rooms or test their construction. [3–5]
- **Digital primary evidence:** the SUU transcript remains the independently accessible story source used here. [6]
- **Unverified:** original film-work structures still standing at their original locations, public access to former working ranches, or the present condition of privately held film artifacts. No such field route is supplied.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Choose the open public anchor first.** Do not start with a pin for a ranch or a photograph of a ruined set. If winter opening is uncertain, use the transcript instead.
2. **At Kanab Museum, if open:** begin with town context and the actual labels available. Notice whether a film item credits a maker, employer or worker as well as an actor. Do not assume a film-specific display exists merely because the website discusses films.
3. **Read the worker's account alongside the glamour.** Consider what information an employment record could add that a poster cannot: task, time, compensation and whether work continued between productions. This is a source-reading exercise, not an instruction to seek private personnel records.
4. **Optional second setting only after its gate is cleared:** at Little Hollywood, distinguish a labeled film-used object from its modern museum placement. A relocated component can still be meaningful without pretending it stands where the camera stood.
5. **Finish on the strongest story moment:** ask who was still working when the audience's imagined story ended. The local labor behind the picture is the payoff. No stunt, destructive-work demonstration or set re-creation is part of this stop.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

Leach's view of why production declined is his retrospective interpretation, not a settled economic explanation. His pay recollection has no supplied year or inspected pay record. The transcript contains hazardous cleanup descriptions; this dossier deliberately supplies neither methods nor locations for reenactment or recovery. [6]

The 1993 article places *The Greatest Story Ever Told* filming in 1969; Kanab Museum's film list uses 1963. [7,10] These do not reconcile production versus release dates. Do not choose one silently or use this discrepancy to discredit every worker recollection. The exact film chronology needs primary production documentation.

Winter museum hours conflict as described above. No real-time door check, phone call, accessibility verification or current exhibit inventory was obtained. The **Once Upon a Time in America Museum** is another institution, not an alternate name for either anchor. Its operator page simultaneously says closed for winter and gives December 12 as the season's last day with December 13 closure and an unset 2027 reopening. That internally awkward wording is not clearance to visit. [11]

No published local worker voice stands for all residents, and movie Westerns are not documentary accounts of Native communities. Tribal perspectives require their own published sources before being added; no cultural-site directions are included.
