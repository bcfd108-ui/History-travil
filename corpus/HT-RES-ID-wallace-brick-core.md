## 2. The story
Wallace burned the first time in **1890**, when fire took its wooden downtown — one local tally counts thirteen saloons, six hotels, a bank, a theater, and better than fifty other buildings. The town rebuilt the way mining money could: in **brick, stone, and steel**, with Italian marble sinks in the barbershops, cast-iron cornices, terracotta trim — and, in 1901–02, a Chateau-style Northern Pacific depot in buff brick. That 1890s brick core is the downtown you walk today, and it is the reason there is a Wallace to walk.

On **August 20, 1910**, the Big Burn came down out of the forests. Timothy Egan's account, drawn from the town's own record: a *“warm fog so opaque”* covered Wallace that at **3 p.m. the lights in town were all turned on**; people buried their possessions in the ground. The elderly, women, and children were packed onto trains out of town — **no pets, no baggage beyond what a person could carry** — and the men were ordered to stay and fight. The fire took the town's east side: a mill, a rooming house, hotels, a brewery, and homes — about **one-third of Wallace** in the standard accounts — while the brick core, built after 1890 for this exact day, largely held. The same night, five miles of canyon away in the other direction, Ed Pulaski was holding a mine portal with wet blankets (see the tunnel entry); Wallace is where his survivors staggered in over burning logs the next morning.

The town rebuilt again, financed by the silver in the hills around it — the Coeur d'Alene district went on to produce more than **1.2 billion ounces of silver** — and Wallace kept its 1890s brick face. In the 1970s the citizens did something no other American town has done in quite the same way: they got the **entire downtown listed on the National Register of Historic Places**, a move that forced Interstate 90 onto the elevated viaduct that still runs over the town's edge instead of through its heart.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a brick mining capital that had already survived one total fire and rebuilt against the next. **Now:** the most intact 1890s brick downtown in the Inland Northwest — Wallace Corner and the Smokehouse among the first brick buildings of the 1890–91 rebuild still stand — with the freeway on stilts overhead and a population under 800 guarding ninety square blocks of listed streetscape.

## 4. What's still there
- The brick downtown itself — walk Bank Street and Cedar Street and you are walking the post-1890 rebuild that faced the 1910 fire
- The Northern Pacific Depot (1901–02), Chateau-style, now the depot museum — the railroad that evacuated the town, in the building that received it
- The Shoshone County Courthouse (1907), which stood through the fire
- The Wallace Carnegie Library (1910–11), rising the year after the burn — the rebuild made literal
- The east end of town: the ground the 1910 fire actually took, read against the brick core it couldn't

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
'One-third of Wallace' is the standard figure (Spokane Historical; Discover Wallace); Egan's narrative and the Adobe/Forest Service summary list specific losses (mill, rooming house, two hotels, depot, brewery, courthouse) that do not all square with the surviving 1901 depot and 1907 courthouse standing today — those lists appear to conflate threatened, damaged, and destroyed. This entry prints the one-third figure and the brick core's survival, and flags the itemized lists as unreliable in detail. The 1890 loss tally is a local-history count, carried as such.
