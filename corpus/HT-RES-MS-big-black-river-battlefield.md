# Big Black River and the people left at the crossing

*On Old Ground · Regional March · Mississippi OP13 · Labeled research draft · NO-ANCHOR · Reflection only*

## 2. Story

The Confederates who withdrew from Champion Hill on May 16, 1863 reached an important railroad crossing on the Big Black. Here track, river and earthworks shaped where ordinary soldiers could leave or be left behind. Two bridges served the retreat, including a steamboat put to work as a crossing. On May 17 Union forces broke through the east-bank defense. Men escaped, were taken prisoner, or had to cross a river whose bridges were being lost. NPS describes the defeat as the last major encounter before retreat into Vicksburg. [1, 3, 5]

Samuel H. Lockett, the Confederate chief engineer, wrote that the bridge was made by “swinging the steamer Dot across the stream.” His report names engineering officers D. Wintter and P. Robinson in the effort to preserve a crossing for the army, and later records orders to abandon and destroy the spans. The brief quotation is from the original printed *Official Records* compilation. The published copy identifies the officers and the vessel. The names of the boat crew, the men who handled lines, and the laborers who built the earthworks require other sources. The *Dot* belonged to a world of moving people and freight before it became a bridge. [5]

Across the river, Union commander Peter Joseph Osterhaus said his men “hastily threw a bridge over the Big Black River” during the evening and night. In that report the burned railroad span put a delay between victory and pursuit. From the Confederate side, Francis Cockrell describes seeing parts of a line break and shared equipment already out of reach, with the responsibility for moving battery horses uncertain. These are different accounts of how the same river narrowed people's choices. Read them with the casualty and prisoner totals held separately. [1, 5]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Big Black River Battlefield district in Hinds and Warren counties. The 1863 railroad crossing and the steamer bridge belong to the wartime setting. The Mississippi inventory separately records a later railroad bridge resource. The record of that structure supplies continuity of transportation in the vicinity, with neither proof of an 1863 surviving span nor access to the water. [1–3, 6]

**Present public anchor.** No separate site-specific open gallery or lawful non-battlefield and non-shore observation area was established. MDAH's Vicksburg Civil War Visitor Center is a project with a projected 2030 opening, without a present visitor gate. [4]

## 5. Public progression

Read Lockett's account first for the civilian vessel pressed into military service. Then read Osterhaus on the work needed once the bridge was gone. End with the digital stereograph at **The river after the army**, a quiet view of terrain with the people absent from the frame. It is a reading sequence, with no visit or reenactment. [5, 8, 9]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- NPS gives 1,751 Confederate losses in its estimate and approximately 1,800 people captured in its narrative. The categories and precision cannot be harmonized without checking returns and prisoner records. The figures are not added. [1]
- Lockett describes responsibility for preparing to abandon the bridges. Osterhaus measures the resulting delay at more than twelve hours and emphasizes the railroad bridge, while NPS describes two crossings and withdrawal in general terms. Their scope and perspective differ. Individual bridge builders and the *Dot*'s crew remain unidentified. [1, 5]
- The public interpretation table in the federal survey records no on-site visitor interpretation for this battlefield. Current lawful off-site interpretation remains unverified. A registered position or state inventory listing is not an access determination. [2, 4, 7]
- The catalog image landing page returned HTTP 403 on direct retrieval. The catalog's indexed item metadata and MARC companion provided item and rights details, and the Library's direct large JPEG loaded for visual inspection. Battle-day civilian testimony, material survival of the first spans and independent public viewing rights remained unverified. [6, 8–10]

## Connected ground — The Vicksburg Campaign, 1863

The army that withdrew from Champion Hill on May 16, 1863 reached this crossing that night; its defeat here on May 17, and the burning of its two bridges, sealed it inside the Vicksburg defenses it surrendered on July 4, 1863.
