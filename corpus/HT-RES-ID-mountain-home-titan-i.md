# Mountain Home AFB Titan I Missile Field (569th Strategic Missile Squadron): Complexes 569-A, 569-B, and 569-C

**Research draft · Labeled THEME SWEEP (Family 5 — Military installations, Cold War ICBM) · NO-ANCHOR (Research reading only) · Owyhee, Ada, and Elmore Counties, Idaho · November 20, 2026 Pacific**

## 1. Trip gate

**NO LAWFUL PUBLIC ANCHOR ON SITE — RESEARCH READING ONLY.** All three former launch complexes of the 569th Strategic Missile Squadron are closed to public entry. Complex **569-A** (south of Bruneau, Owyhee County) and Complex **569-B** (near Oreana/Grand View, Owyhee County) were sold after deactivation and converted into industrial hazardous-waste disposal facilities operated by Wes-Con and later Envirosafe Services / **U.S. Ecology of Idaho** (`Site A`, EPA ID `IDD000773952`, and `Site B`). Under Idaho Department of Environmental Quality (IDEQ) Post-Closure Care Permits, the complexes are capped, monitored closed landfills enclosed by chain-link perimeter fences, barbed wire, locked gates, warning signs, and monitoring wells; historic interest creates no right of entry. Complex **569-C** (northeast of Orchard, Ada County) is privately owned rural land. Parent **Mountain Home Air Force Base** (Elmore County) is an active U.S. Air Force installation closed to the general public without base sponsorship or pass. [1, 2, 3, 4]

Visitors seeking lawful public interpretation of Mountain Home Air Force Base's Cold War history can visit the **Idaho Military History Museum** at 4692 W. Harvard Street in Boise (45+ miles away; Tue–Fri 10:00 a.m.–4:00 p.m., Sat 12:00 p.m.–4:00 p.m. MT), which maintains a Mountain Home AFB exhibit separate from the restricted missile sites. [5]

## 2. The story

Between 1961 and 1965, southern Idaho's sagebrush desert briefly formed the western anchor of Strategic Air Command's first-generation **SM-68 / HGM-25A Titan I** intercontinental ballistic missile force. Under the parent **9th Bombardment Wing** (redesignated the **9th Strategic Aerospace Wing** in April 1962) at Mountain Home Air Force Base, the Air Force redesignated and organized the **569th Strategic Missile Squadron (569th SMS)** at Mountain Home on **June 1, 1961**—the final Titan I squadron activated by Strategic Air Command. [1, 6]

Unlike later solid-fuel Minuteman silos or storable-liquid Titan II sites, Titan I used a dispersed **3 × 3 configuration**: three remote underground launch complexes (**569-A** near Bruneau, **569-B** near Oreana, and **569-C** near Orchard), each housing three missiles for a total of nine launchers. Each missile measured roughly 98 feet in length and burned RP-1 kerosene and cryogenic liquid oxygen (LOX). Because liquid oxygen could not remain loaded indefinitely inside the missile tanks, crews kept each Titan I fueled in a standby state on a massive elevator 157 feet underground inside its silo, paired with a control center, powerhouse, propellant terminals, equipment terminals, guidance-antenna silos, and 40-to-60-foot-deep connecting tunnels. On launch alert, the missile had to be fueled, raised above the paired blast doors to the surface, and fired in the open while ground-based radio-inertial guidance radars tracked its flight. [1, 3]

Construction began across the three complexes in 1960; the field reached completion prior to April 1, 1962, the first missiles arrived in April 1962, and the squadron was publicly declared operational on **August 16, 1962**—just two months before the **Cuban Missile Crisis**, during which the 569th SMS stood nuclear alert. Combat crews worked 24-hour underground shifts at the remote complexes while maintenance, security, logistics, and wing administration linked them back to Mountain Home AFB. [3, 6]

Technology overtook the weapon almost immediately. In May 1963, Headquarters USAF slated Titan I for phaseout between 1965 and 1968; in 1964, Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara accelerated retirement to eliminate all Titan I squadrons before the end of fiscal year 1965 (publicly announced November 1964) in favor of quicker-reacting Titan II and Minuteman missiles. The 569th SMS began taking missiles off alert on **April 1, 1965**, and inactivated on **June 25, 1965**—barely three years after the first missiles reached Idaho. [6]

After federal salvage and disposal, the deep concrete silos found an unusual second life. Beginning in the early 1970s (**1972** at Site B / `569-B`; **c. 1973** at Site A / `569-A`), **Wes-Con** acquired the Bruneau and Oreana complexes to dispose of industrial and hazardous wastes—including ordnance/propellant and chemical wastes—inside the subterranean silos and tunnels. Envirosafe Services of Idaho acquired the Bruneau property in 1981 and continued disposal into January 1983. Under subsequent Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) and Idaho Hazardous Waste Management Act enforcement, both `569-A` and `569-B` were capped, closed, and placed under long-term Idaho DEQ post-closure care and groundwater/security monitoring under **U.S. Ecology of Idaho** (`IDD000773952` for Site A). Complex `569-C` near Orchard passed into private rural ownership. [1, 2, 3, 4]

### Haunted history / lore

No documented haunted lore is associated with the 569th SMS complexes in military, environmental-permit, or state historical sources reviewed for this entry. Modern public interest centers strictly on Cold War nuclear engineering and environmental post-closure containment. [1, 2]

## 4. What's still there

- **Subsurface & regulated sites (excluded):** At `569-A`, surface superstructures were demolished; the 82.5-acre U.S. Ecology parcel (enclosing the ~30-acre former missile base) holds engineered caps, drainage works, groundwater monitoring wells, locked gates, and chain-link/barbed-wire security fencing. Similar containment/closure infrastructure restricts `569-B`, and `569-C` is private fenced land. [1, 3, 4]
- **Off-site museum context:** Mountain Home Air Force Base history is interpreted publicly 45+ miles northwest at the **Idaho Military History Museum** in Boise (`4692 W. Harvard St.`). No preserved Titan I missile or open Titan I silo museum exists in Idaho (the nearest public Titan missile museum is the Titan II complex in Green Valley, Arizona). [5]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **County attribution error in casual sources:** Because parent Mountain Home AFB lies in Elmore County, secondary listings sometimes group all three complexes under Elmore County. In fact, `569-A` and `569-B` stand in **Owyhee County**, and `569-C` stands in **Ada County**. [1, 3]
- **Engineering drawing gaps:** Surviving as-built 569th SMS blueprints archived online (`chromehooves.net`) lack complete drawing categories for Control Centers, Entry Portals, Antenna Silos, and Civil/General plans, and no complete measured drawing set for `569-B` has been located in open repositories. [7]
- **Active vs. closed status at `569-B`:** IDEQ Site A permit documents describe Site B (`Grand View`) as the administrative/operating location where Site A records are kept, while military-history summaries note former Titan I structures at `569-B` are buried and capped; exact cell-level active vs. closed status of Site B units lies in IDEQ's separate Site B permit record (not retrieved in full here). [2, 4]
- **FUDS inventory PDF check:** Direct download of USACE's `FUDS_Inventory_Idaho.pdf` URL returned an HTML/redirect wrapper on November 20, 2026 rather than a readable PDF binary; federal property control ID `D-Idaho-457` for `569-C` is cited via The Military Standard pending direct GSA/FUDS docket retrieval. [3]
