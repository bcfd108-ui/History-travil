# HT-RES-MI-port-oneida — A whole farming neighborhood, kept
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

North of Glen Arbor, inside Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore, an entire immigrant farming community survives as a landscape: some 3,400 acres of farmsteads, orchards, sugar maples, two schoolhouses, and two cemeteries along M-22 and Port Oneida Road, listed on the National Register in 1997 as one of the largest and most complete historic agricultural landscapes in public ownership. Port Oneida began as a cordwood dock on Sleeping Bear Bay, became a neighborhood of Hanoverian and Prussian farm families, farmed marginal sand for a century — and stopped, almost all at once, when the National Lakeshore arrived in 1970. The Park Service has kept the buildings standing ever since, and the visit is a drive through the neighborhood that resulted.

## 1. Trip gate
Port Oneida is not a single site with a gate; it is a rural historic district inside a national lakeshore, and the Park Service's terms govern the whole visit. Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore requires an entrance pass for all visitors: the Park Service's fees page states that all visitors must have one of its entrance passes — a standard pass at $15.00–$25.00 depending on how a visitor arrives ($25 for a private vehicle, good 1–7 days), an annual park pass at $45.00, or an America the Beautiful pass — and that the park does not accept cash (NPS fees page, checked November 26, 2026). The district is experienced from the public roads: the Park Service publishes a self-guided Port Oneida Driving Tour booklet, and its self-guided activities page notes of the Port Oneida tour, plainly, "There are no restrooms." Farmstead grounds the Park Service owns are the stops; the district also contains private farmsteads — the nomination records ownership as mixed private, public-local, and public-Federal — and a private farmstead is someone's property, not a sight. The Kelderhouse (Port Oneida) Cemetery, at the corner of M-22 and Port Oneida Road, is privately owned and not park property, as the Park Service's place page states; it is read from the road. Building interiors open only where a program opens them — the Charles Olsen Farm hosts the Port Oneida Farms Heritage Center, run by the park's nonprofit partner Preserve Historic Sleeping Bear — and no interior hours are printed here, because no operator page checked in this pass states standing ones.

## 2. The story
The community's first European residents, in the nomination's account, were Carsten and Elizabeth Burfiend, who arrived in 1852 and took up 275 acres; Carsten Burfiend fished and ran a ferry to the Manitou Islands. The Werners followed in 1855 and the Dechows in 1857, and by 1860 the settlement counted 87 people, mostly immigrants from Hanover and Prussia; between 1860 and 1865 sixteen land claims were filed, twelve under the Preemption Act and four under the Homestead Act. The place became Port Oneida through a piece of commerce. On April 1, 1861, Thomas Kelderhouse, a lumberman from Albany, New York, struck a deal with Burfiend: a dock on Sleeping Bear Bay in exchange for 177 acres of shoreline. The dock was built by 1862 — only the second on the bay — and the community took its name from the SS Oneida, one of the first steamships to stop there. Kelderhouse's sawmill cut cordwood, and the dock served as a steamship fueling station on the Buffalo–Chicago route: the young community's economy was, at first, the forest itself, sold by the cord to passing ships.

That economy consumed its own basis. By the 1890s, the nomination records, Pyramid Point was almost completely deforested, and the steamships had switched from wood to coal — the economic base disappeared. Thomas Kelderhouse died in 1884. By 1908 the dock and the original townsite buildings stood abandoned, all but the Kelderhouse residence, and the community's center had shifted about three-quarters of a mile inland, to Port Oneida Road and M-22, where the school, the Evangelical Lutheran church, and the cemetery stood. By 1952 every built feature of the logging era had disappeared. What replaced the dock economy was farming on ground the nomination calls "marginal at best" — sandy glacial soils and unreliable water — and what shaped the surviving landscape was dairying: subsistence dairy farms, worked by families who mostly held non-farm jobs as well. Electricity arrived only in the late 1940s. The Dechow/Klett and Charles Olsen farms ran Grade A dairies, and Howard Burfiend won national recognition in 1937 in Hoard's Dairyman. The buildings the visitor sees are that second community's: upright-and-wing farmhouses with Greek Revival touches and heavy-timber barns, raised between 1870 and 1945, the district's period of significance.

The end came by designation. Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore was established in 1970 and took in all of Port Oneida; in the nomination's words, "when Port Oneida became part of the National Lakeshore in 1970, farming effectively came to a complete stop." The Park Service acquired at least ten to fifteen farmhouses and dozens of agricultural buildings, and held them with minimal upkeep — the Burfiend barn was lost to fire, and field and fencing patterns began to disappear; the Park Service's Open Field Management Plan now keeps the fields open by periodic mowing. The district was added to the National Register in 1997, on the nomination prepared by Marla J. McEnaney of the Park Service's Midwest Field Area. The cultural landscape account gives the scale: 3,400 acres, 121 contributing buildings, 5 contributing sites, and 20 contributing structures dating from 1870 to 1945 — among them the Thoreson, Olsen, Dechow/Klett, Kelderhouse, Burfiend, and Basch farmsteads, the Port Oneida and North Unity schools, and the Port Oneida and Werner family cemeteries. It is, in that account, "one of the largest and most complete historic agricultural landscapes in public ownership" — a neighborhood preserved whole because its economy ended all at once.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a dock and a sawmill on Sleeping Bear Bay, selling cordwood to Buffalo–Chicago steamships, with a shoreline townsite around them (nomination). **Now:** the dock and townsite are gone — every logging-era built feature had disappeared by 1952 — and the district's boundary still runs to the lake, taking in the Port Oneida dock ruins as a site (nomination).

**Then:** a farming neighborhood of Hanoverian and Prussian families — 87 people by 1860, then a century of dairy farming on sandy, marginal ground, its social center on Port Oneida Road with a school, a Lutheran church, and a cemetery (nomination). **Now:** the same neighborhood unpopulated as a working community but standing — 121 contributing buildings kept by the Park Service and its partners, its fields mowed open under the park's Open Field Management Plan (nomination; NPS cultural landscape account).

**Then:** the Kelderhouse farm at the M-22 and Port Oneida Road crossroads as the community's gathering place — farmhouse, grocery store, telephone exchange, and post office (NPS place page). **Now:** the same white farmhouse, built about 1910, standing alone in its field as this entry's first photograph shows it in 2006 — the district's crossroads kept as a landmark (NPS; NPGallery asset record).

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its source attached:
- The farmsteads of the driving tour, among them the Charles Olsen Farm — home of the Port Oneida Farms Heritage Center (Preserve Historic Sleeping Bear) — the Kelderhouse, Dechow, Thoreson, and Burfiend farms, and the Miller Barn, all named as stops on the Park Service's Port Oneida Fair shuttle and its driving-tour booklet (NPS; Friends of Sleeping Bear Dunes)
- The William and Charlotte Kelderhouse farmhouse, built about 1910 at the M-22 and Port Oneida Road crossroads — once the community's grocery store, telephone exchange, post office, and meeting place, held by Kelderhouse descendants from 1863 until the Park Service acquired it in 1970 (NPS place page; Preserve Historic Sleeping Bear)
- The Dechow/Klett farm, with its sugar maple row and sugar shack, and the remnant orchards at the Kelderhouse and Thoreson farms (NPS cultural landscape account)
- The Franklin Basch Centennial Farm, whose Werner family buildings of about 1855 are among the district's oldest (NPS cultural landscape account)
- The Brunson Barn on Thoreson Road — the only building left of the Joseph and Margaret Brunson farm, a two-and-a-half-story gambrel-roofed, heavy-timber barn built around 1900, restored through Park Service and volunteer workshops and named a Michigan Barn Preservation Network Barn of the Year in 2020 (nomination; MBPN)
- The Port Oneida School — school began in the community in the 1860s; the present building went up in the 1890s and is owned and maintained by the Glen Lake Community School District (Friends of Sleeping Bear Dunes driving-tour booklet) — and the North Unity School (nomination)
- The cemeteries: the Kelderhouse (Port Oneida) Cemetery at the crossroads, privately owned, with forty-six markers over at least fifty-nine graves (NPS place page; Preserve Historic Sleeping Bear), and the Werner Family Cemetery (nomination)
- The landscape itself — open fields, fence lines, orchard remnants, and the maple rows, held open by the Park Service's mowing plan across the district's 3,400 acres (nomination; NPS)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, following the order of the Park Service's own driving tour and fair shuttle, from public roads and park-owned grounds as §1 bounds them:

1. **At the Charles Olsen Farm, on M-22 about four miles north of the M-22/M-109 junction in Glen Arbor:** The tour's beginning, per the driving-tour booklet — the farmstead close to the road just past the district sign, and the seat of the Port Oneida Farms Heritage Center. Start here, with the pass displayed and the booklet in hand.
2. **At the Port Oneida School, on Port Oneida Road:** The community's second center — the 1890s schoolhouse near the crossroads where the social town rebuilt itself after the dock died. Read the move inland here: dock to crossroads, in one stop.
3. **At the Kelderhouse Farm, M-22 and Port Oneida Road:** The crossroads farm of §4 — store, telephone exchange, post office, meeting place — with the cemetery across the corner, read from the road as the private ground it is. This entry's first photograph was taken here.
4. **At the Dechow Farm and the Dechow/Klett maple row:** The district's best dairy-era ground — a Grade A dairy landscape with its sugar maples and sugar shack, the farming that replaced the cordwood economy at its most complete.
5. **At the Thoreson Farm, on Thoreson Road:** Three generations of one Norwegian family on 160 acres — and, west along the same road, the Brunson Barn standing alone in its field, the last building of its farm. End where the preservation question is plainest: one barn, kept standing on purpose.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The district's period of significance prints two ways inside the Park Service's own record: the nomination, the Park Service's cultural landscape account, and the General Management Plan's own table give 1870–1945, while one passage of the General Management Plan's text gives 1880–1945. The nomination's 1870–1945 is used in this entry, and the 1880 form is printed here unreconciled.

Scale figures vary by the frame a source chooses. The Park Service's cultural landscape account gives 3,400 acres and counts 121 contributing buildings, 5 sites, and 20 structures; the nomination's own inventory counts 121 contributing and 14 noncontributing buildings, 146 contributing resources in total. Secondary summaries describe "3,000 acres" and "16 historic farms"; those forms are not used here, and the farm count depends on how a source groups farmsteads the nomination inventories building by building. The acreage in §2 and §4 is the Park Service's 3,400.

The visitor-services picture is thin in the primary record and prints thin here. The Park Service's self-guided activities page states there are no restrooms for the Port Oneida tour; the Park Service's Port Oneida Fair page, an event page, maps restrooms at fair shuttle stops (Olsen, Kelderhouse, Dechow) during the fair. Both print; neither is generalized — the fair is a dated annual event, and its facilities are event facilities. No standing interior hours for any district building were found on an operator page in this pass, so none print; the Heritage Center at the Olsen Farm is a partner-run program space, not a posted-hours museum in the records checked.

Two record gaps are named plainly. The nomination's location line reads "Rural Route Port Oneida," Maple City vicinity, while the district is presented to visitors — and titled in this entry — as Glen Arbor vicinity; both are Leelanau County postal framings of the same ground, and the entry keeps both rather than silently choosing. And the district's National Register reference number was not carried in any record read in this pass, so §10 leaves the register line to the nomination itself rather than supplying a number from a secondary catalog.
