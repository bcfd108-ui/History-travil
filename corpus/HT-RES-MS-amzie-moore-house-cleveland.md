# The house Amzie Moore opened to voter-registration organizers

*On Old Ground · Labeled research draft · Mississippi OP21*

## 2. Story

Amzie Moore’s home gave visiting organizers in the Delta a place to sleep, talk and work. In an oral history with John Jones and John Dittmer, he describes Bob Moses’s arrival: “he lived at my house and we started to work together.” The quote is from Mississippi Department of Archives and History’s transcript. Moore adds that he could not remember exactly how Moses first got there. The limit in the same answer matters. The recollection verifies the working relationship and hospitality without settling the initial itinerary. [3]

The property file describes a brick house, dates construction to 1941, and points to voter-registration classes associated with the home. The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee’s historical project adds Moore’s wartime experience and gives Moses’s recollection that Moore offered a sustained idea for registration work when they met during his 1960 journey. Local organizers were already confronting discriminatory registration practices. Giving a visitor a place to stay served a wider strategy developed with other residents, including people who made meetings possible, prepared food and looked after children. Moore himself names work through the Head Start program as one way people were fed. [1][3][4]

MDAH links Moore to leading the Cleveland NAACP branch and establishing the Bolivar County Voters League alongside Herman Perry. The National Park Service context study names Moore, Aaron Henry and T. R. M. Howard together in forming the Regional Council of Negro Leadership. These civic relationships carried the work beyond one house. A later restoration has interpreted the house as one of several places where movement work happened in everyday rooms. [1][2][4][5]

## 3. Then and now

The state file identifies the 1941 Chrisman Avenue brick house. The 1980 oral-history introduction names Moore’s house on Kennedy Cove, and he describes having moved. The interview therefore remains personal evidence about his history. It does not prove where the recording was made relative to the older house or which room housed a particular event. The Freedom Houses project describes restoration and appointment-based use of the historic house. [1–3]

Compare the state property context with Moore’s memory of working with Moses. **Strongest story moment — “We started to work together.”** The phrase changes the scale of the story from a national movement’s chronology to the decision made inside a house to put time and shelter behind local organizing. ## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- MDAH assigns the Regional Council founding to 1950 and singles out Moore. NPS and the SNCC historical project place the founding in 1951 and credit several organizers. The original organizing minutes were not retrieved. Keep both the date discrepancy and shared work visible. [1][4][5]
- The house recorded as a resource and the Kennedy Cove interview location are not verified as the same street address. The original loan and Moore–Moses correspondence were not retrieved for this pass. The Freedom Houses project narrows the first-FHA-loan claim to Bolivar County while the MDAH description uses broader language. [1–4]
