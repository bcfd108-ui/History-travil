## 2. The story
**Original interpretive angle:** a public-works project can be studied through time off as well as time on the job. A shared day tells us something that an aerial photograph cannot: how workers tried to remain brothers, friends and people with lives outside their employment.

**Named primary voice:** Ray Nelson was interviewed by Gladys Peterson on April 10, 1988. University of Montana's catalog identifies the record as **OH 221-002**, Nelson Family Oral History Project. The transcript was downloaded and the relevant page visually checked; audio was not played. [5][6]

On printed page 10, Nelson recalls moving from farm work to Fort Peck, followed later by his younger brother. Their days off differed but overlapped on Sunday. His short statement is **“We had two days off a week.”** He recalls outings together in his Model A. [6]

This is a remembered personal schedule, not a verified rule for every worker or a union contract. The historical outings are not modern visitor directions. No excavation, fishing or driving destination mentioned in his recollection becomes a place to visit through this dossier.

**Original visitor narration:** Look for the person inside the phrase “dam worker.” The job gives him a category. His brother and their shared day give him a relationship. A large project could reorganize a family's week without making that family appear in its official silhouette. Ask what a photograph of the structure would need beside it to tell that story honestly.

The point is not that a day off resolves hardship. Nor does one person's memory prove that everyone benefited equally. This deliberately narrow account makes one relationship visible while leaving unsupported claims about wages, accommodation, recruitment and household budgets open.

The state's Voices of Labor collection identifies New Deal in Montana/Fort Peck Dam as one of its digitized oral-history projects. This provides a substantial expansion path, not evidence that every interview has been listened to here. [7] Ray's independently held university interview remains this draft's actual primary text.

No operational or construction instructions are reproduced. Discussion of injuries or deaths belongs in reflection and contextual research, never a find, reenactment or approach to a hazard. Tribal histories and displacement require Tribal-published sources before this account is expanded into a comprehensive social history; they are not supplied by Nelson's testimony or inferred from a shared place name.
