# Fort Robinson Northern Cheyenne witness stop

Nebraska OP04 · Research draft for Dash · November 19, 2026

## 2. The story

### Captivity after forced removal

The Northern Cheyenne had been forced to move to Indian Territory in 1877. The Nebraska State Historical Society records the return north the next year, the separation of the parties led by Morning Star (Dull Knife) and Little Wolf, and the Army’s arrival at Fort Robinson with 149 men, women and children on October 24, 1878. Their movements and choices are also interpreted in *Coming Home: The Northern Cheyenne Odyssey*, created by Northern Cheyenne historians, artists and tribal members under Francine D. Spang-Willis’s direction. The exhibition includes pages drawn by the Northern Cheyenne artist Little Finger about the return. Read the federal post account alongside that authored account of people going home. [3–5]

### A barracks became a place of confinement

The same building had housed cavalry companies. The state history describes people initially able to spend part of the day outside, with evening roll call. That changed in late December. Federal authorities insisted on return south, and the commanding officer withheld food and fuel to force submission. On the night of January 9, 1879, the prisoners broke out. The ensuing pursuit ended in deaths and recaptures. The state timeline calls its last day, January 22, the end of the massacre. [3,4]

### What made it into print

A Fort Robinson dispatch reported in the January 26, 1879 *Daily Astorian* recorded the returned military dead and gave a different kind of information about the captive people: “The Indians now here are under guard”. This short quotation was checked against page one of the original newspaper scan. It is a contemporary news dispatch, with its attention trained on the Army’s movements. The Western Heritage Center account of the Northern Cheyenne–created exhibition follows the consequences past that military news cycle and through the effort to establish a permanent home in Montana. [5,6]

## 3. Then / now

The detention was in a cavalry barracks in 1878–79. The History Center today occupies the 1905 post headquarters building. The public exhibit is the witness venue. The barracks location, escape path and places of recapture or death remain historical subjects for reading. [1,3,4]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- On the group that began the return, the state marker says some 300, the Society’s artifact article says 350, and its feature article says more than 350. All three report a Fort Robinson group of 149. The marker says more than 70 recaptured and the artifact article says 80 women and children. The feature article says some 80 in its account of the first night. The sources agree on 64 Cheyenne and 11 soldiers killed over the escape and pursuit. The January 24 dispatch counts one return of prisoners and dead. Its scope differs from the totals. [3,6–8]
- The direct Montana Memory Project record for Lucille Spear returned an internal error. The oral history was neither heard nor quoted. The exhibition credit and description were retrieved at the partner museum. The original Little Finger ledger pages remain unread for this dossier. [5,9]
- The June wildfire reopening notice is dated and covers its own reopening scope. Use the museum’s current hours and present road conditions. No trail or battlefield access was verified for November. [1,2,10]

## 7. Historical photo record

The Nebraska State Historical Society publishes a studio portrait captioned as Little Wolf and Morning Star (Dull Knife) in November 1873. The Society’s historic-photo gallery documents other periods of the fort. The portrait supplies no ground position at the fort. The ledger is historical artwork. No Photo Point. [3,5,11]
