# HT-RES-CT-hartford-mark-twain-house — The Mark Twain House: the Hartford house where Clemens wrote his major books
Division CT seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

Samuel Clemens built this polychrome brick house at 351 Farmington Avenue in 1873–1874 and lived in it until 1891 — the years of *Tom Sawyer*, *Huckleberry Finn*, *The Prince and the Pauper*, and *A Connecticut Yankee*, written in the third-floor billiard room he used as a study. The house is now the Mark Twain House & Museum, a National Historic Landmark with restored interiors.

## 1. Trip gate
The Mark Twain House & Museum at 351 Farmington Avenue is open to the public as a historic house museum. Tours of the restored interiors are available; exterior grounds are accessible during operating hours. Confirm current hours, ticket requirements, and any special exhibits with the museum before travel.

## 2. The story
Samuel Langhorne Clemens (Mark Twain) and his wife Olivia Langdon Clemens moved to the Nook Farm neighborhood of Hartford in the early 1870s, drawn by the city's publishing industry and a literary circle that included Harriet Beecher Stowe. They purchased land and commissioned New York architect Edward Tuckerman Potter (with Alfred H. Thorp) to design a large brick house in an eclectic Stick/Victorian Gothic style. Construction occurred in 1873–1874; the family moved in during September 1874.

The Clemenses lived here until 1891. During those years Clemens wrote major works including *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer*, *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, *The Prince and the Pauper*, and *A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court*. The third-floor billiard room served as his study. Financial difficulties forced a move to Europe in 1891; the death of daughter Susy in the house in 1896 made return impossible. The property was sold in 1903. Later uses included a boys' school and apartments before preservation efforts began. It was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1962 and operates today as a museum with restored interiors (including Tiffany decorations).

## 4. What's still there
The polychrome brick house with its gables, porches, chimneys, and distinctive silhouette remains on Farmington Avenue. Interiors have been restored to the Clemens period, including the billiard room/study and public rooms decorated by Associated Artists (Louis Comfort Tiffany). The carriage house survives nearby. Grounds and a modern museum center provide visitor orientation.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Arrive at 351 Farmington Avenue. Begin at the visitor center for tickets and orientation. Tour the main house, noting the billiard room where Clemens worked and the restored public spaces. Exterior views from the grounds show the original massing. Optional visits to the carriage house and nearby Stowe house may be available. Return via Farmington Avenue.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
Exact construction start and completion months vary slightly across accounts (spring/summer 1873 to September 1874 occupancy). Secondary sources differ on precise room uses and the extent of later alterations before restoration. Primary Clemens correspondence and museum restoration reports were not consulted for this entry.
