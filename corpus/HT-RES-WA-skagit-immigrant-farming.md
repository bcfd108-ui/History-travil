## 2. The story
The specific community lens is **Japanese immigrant and Japanese American family farming in the Skagit Valley**, centered on the Sakumas. This differs from the Yakima dossier's migrant-labor chronology; it follows a family's migration, forced removal, return and generational continuity without claiming to represent all valley farmers.

The family's own company history begins with **Takeo Sakuma's migration from Kyushu in 1907**, farming on Bainbridge Island and marriage to **Nobu** in 1914. It dates son **Atsusa's Burlington move to 1937**, followed by brothers, and describes forced removal dividing the family between camps. It credits the **Oscar Mapes family** with caring for the Burlington farm and records the family's postwar concentration there after losses on Bainbridge. These are attributed family/company accounts, not independently reconstructed deeds or a complete financial history. [3]

A named descendant gives the work texture. **Richard Sakuma**, quoted in Skagitonians to Preserve Farmland's family feature, remembers: **“The uncles did carpentry, plumbing, built things, took care of the crew, and farmed.”** [4] The published account is a community interview/profile, not an archival recording inspected here. His recollection makes farming more than a row of crops: rebuilding required practical trades, coordination and knowledge passed between generations. Do not convert his family's perspective into the voices of all hired workers.

A wartime primary record interrupts any easy success story. The **Minidoka Irrigator, May 8, 1943**, front page, reports that **Atsusa Sakuma**, formerly operating a Burlington farm, and two other Japanese American men were withdrawn from agricultural jobs at Marengo, Illinois, after local opposition. [5] This is a contemporaneous newspaper report, read through extracted text; the image fetch failed, so exact typography and garbled OCR passages remain unchecked. The document follows a Skagit farmer's exclusion elsewhere. It is evidence for this Washington history, **not an Idaho or Illinois itinerary addition**.

The story also has a present-tense voice. In a **July 16, 2026 EDASC interview**, **Ryan Sakuma** describes opposite seasonal water problems: removing excess water in winter and supplying enough in summer. He also explains that the business name persists while ownership now includes cousins. [6] This is his published account at that date, not a field-condition report or an invitation to inspect drainage. His perspective links inherited family identity to changing work; the dossier does not endorse his separate assessment of current labor relations as an independently audited conclusion.

The strongest story moment is the contrast between competence and exclusion: a person could possess agricultural skill and still be denied work because of ancestry. That is our interpretation of the source pairing, not an invented statement by any narrator. The incarceration and discrimination portions are reflection, never scored or hunted.

1. **Begin with scope.** At the public museum, distinguish the current exhibitions from the expired food-and-farming show. A visit can still be worthwhile without the specific show, but should not be sold on it.
2. **Find the work in ordinary objects.** Where a tool or household exhibit is actually present, consider how farm production depends on maintenance, domestic work and skills beyond planting. Do not assign family provenance to an unlabeled object.
