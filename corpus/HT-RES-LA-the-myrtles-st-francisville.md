## 2. The story

The documented history of The Myrtles begins in the aftermath of the **1794 Whiskey Rebellion**. **General David Bradford**, a Pennsylvania lawyer, deputy attorney general, and prominent rebellion leader, fled federal troops down the Ohio and Mississippi rivers into Spanish West Florida (receiving land-grant approval under Governor Baron de Carondelet for roughly **650 acres** near Bayou Sara). In **1796–1797** (see §6 for year & estate-name conflict), Bradford built a one-and-a-half-story timber-frame Creole-style house—now the **western six bays** of the main facade—on his plantation, recorded in the 1978 National Register nomination as **"Richland."** [3, 4]

Bradford died in **1808**. His daughter **Sarah Mathilda Bradford** married young lawyer **Clark Woodruff** on **November 19, 1817**, and Woodruff managed the plantation for Bradford's widow Elizabeth (who formally sold him the estate in **1826**). During an epidemic of **yellow fever**, **Sarah Mathilda died on July 21, 1823**, followed by their son **James on July 15, 1824**, and daughter **Cornelia two months later in September 1824**; a third child, **Octavia**, survived to adulthood. [3, 4]

On **January 1, 1834**, Woodruff sold the plantation to **Ruffin Grey Stirling** and his wife Mary Cobb Stirling, who renamed the estate **"The Myrtles"** after the abundant crape myrtles on the grounds. In the **1850s**, the Stirlings nearly doubled the house, adding an eastward/southward formal wing with a wide stair hall, ornate **pierced-plaster cornices**, **Rococo Revival marble mantels**, and the signature **107-foot ornate cast-iron front gallery** supported by coupled iron posts. [3, 4]

Following the Civil War and Ruffin Stirling's death, the estate passed to daughter **Sarah Stirling Winter** and her husband **William Drew Winter**, a local attorney. On **January 26, 1871**, **William Winter was fatally shot** on or near the front porch/gallery by an unidentified assailant and died shortly thereafter—the **only documented homicide** in the property's primary record. The Stirling/Winter heirs later sold the house in **1889** to the **Williams family**, and it was restored beginning in **1975** before entering its modern bed-and-breakfast and museum era (`NRHP 78001439`, listed 1978, 10-acre historic parcel). [3, 4]

### Haunted history / lore

The Myrtles is commercially marketed as one of America's most haunted houses, centered on the story of **"Chloe,"** an enslaved young woman supposedly forced into the Woodruff household, mutilated (ear severed) for eavesdropping, who allegedly poisoned Sarah Woodruff and two daughters with oleander in a birthday cake before being hanged—and supposedly appears in a **green turban**, alongside tales of **10 murders**, an ancient **Tunica burial ground**, and a haunted hallway mirror. [2, 4]

Primary and archival cross-checks contradict the core legend:
1. **No "Chloe" in Woodruff records:** West Feliciana Parish probate and property records enumerating the people enslaved by the Woodruff family contain **no enslaved woman named Chloe or Cleo** (documented by researchers David Wiseheart, Troy Taylor, and historian **Tiya Miles** in *Tales from the Haunted South*). [4]
2. **Yellow fever, not poison:** Sarah Woodruff died on **July 21, 1823**, and two children (one son, James, and one daughter, Cornelia—not two daughters on a single day) died over a year later in **July–September 1824** of **yellow fever**, per family and estate timelines. [4]
3. **Twentieth-century origin:** Folkloric accounts trace the unnamed "woman in a green bonnet" joke to early twentieth-century Williams descendants, expanding into the "Chloe" legend under 1950s owner Marjorie Munson (`1953`), 1970s–1980s tourism promotion (`LIFE` magazine, November 1980), Frances Kermeen's 1992 book, and a blurred 1992 photograph taken between two outbuildings. None of the ghost/poisoning claims is supported by documentary evidence. [4]

## 4. What's still there

- **Public (daytime/evening tours & dining):** Downstairs six rooms spanning Bradford's 1796–97 core and Stirling's 1850s wing, 107-foot cast-iron front gallery, central stair hall, `1796` restaurant/coffee shop, and the landscaped live-oak grounds (10-acre NRHP parcel). [1, 2, 3]
- **Private (overnight guests only):** Upstairs bedrooms and private guest cottages. [1, 2]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Turn off U.S. Highway 61** just north of St. Francisville into **7747 U.S. Hwy 61** and park in the main visitor lot. [1, 2]
2. **Begin with the complimentary grounds walk** to view the 107-foot cast-iron gallery from the lawn and separate the six western 1796–97 bays from the 1850s Stirling addition. [2, 3]
3. **Take a guided or self-guided daytime downstairs tour** to inspect the documented 1850s Rococo marble mantels, pierced plasterwork, and the front gallery associated with the 1871 William Winter shooting. [2, 3, 4]
4. **If booking an evening mystery tour,** treat the "Chloe" narrative explicitly as modern tour folklore layered over—and contradicted by—the documented 1823–24 yellow-fever and probate record. [4]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Construction year (`1796` vs. `1797`):** The 1978 NRHP nomination (`78001439`) uses both **1796** and **1797** for David Bradford's original six-bay house; the operator uses **1796** (hence the on-site `1796` restaurant). Both dates are preserved. [1, 3, 4]
- **Original estate name (`"Richland"` vs. `"Laurel Grove"`):** NRHP `78001439` gives Bradford's plantation name as **Richland**, whereas the operator's modern historical timeline calls it **Laurel Grove**. [3, 4]
- **William Winter staircase detail:** Secondary tour tellings claim Winter staggered up the main staircase and died on the seventeenth step; the primary 1871 record verifies he was shot on the porch/gallery and died January 26, 1871, while the specific stair-step detail remains unverified against an 1871 coroner's inquest. [4]
- **Tunica burial ground & "10 murders":** Unverified commercial/tour claims with zero archaeological or parish-record confirmation in sources read this run. [4]
