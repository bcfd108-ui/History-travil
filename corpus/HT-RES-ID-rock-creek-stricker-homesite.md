## 2. The story
**Original framing:** A trail is usually drawn as a line. Rock Creek makes the line stop. People needed water; animals needed grazing; passengers, mail and freight needed a place where movement could be supported. Read the store beside its storage spaces and the place becomes a small working supply system, not just a picturesque old cabin.

The National Register nomination describes an 1864 stone home station serving scheduled mail/passenger coaches and an 1865 log store. Its brief phrase **“store, post-office, lunch-room, saloon and club”** is the preservation author's description of multiple functions, not a quoted customer's recollection. The documented cellar and animal shelter show supporting structures. The original stone station is not the surviving log store. [4, description/significance]

**What moved, and what the evidence supports:** NPS identifies emigrants, freight teams and pack strings stopping for provisions, meals and repairs; Holladay's service carried passengers and mail. The creek supported stock as well as people. BLM's trail study places the Kelton freight/stage connection in the 1869 network and explains later loss of many trail traces to farming and roads. Separate overland migration, scheduled coaching, freight distribution and local trade instead of assigning every wagon to the same journey. [3,5]

**Primary commercial evidence identified, not yet read:** Idaho State Archives holds Herman Stricker's financial record book, **MS 0275**, dated **1887–1889**, one volume. The catalog verifies a surviving grocery/mercantile business record. It does not supply its entries. No pounds of flour, price of coffee, credit terms, named buyer or exact freight origin is asserted from this unseen ledger. Transcribing actual transactions is the next research step needed to turn “supplies” into demonstrated goods and quantities. [6]

**Indigenous history and published Tribal voice:** NPS describes Shoshone and Bannock use of this creek area before the emigrant campground. That is agency history, not direct Tribal testimony. The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes' own published account describes seasonal hunting, fishing and gathering and continued stewardship “for future generations.” This is the Tribe's general published statement, not a site-specific oral tradition or permission for cultural tourism. No invented story, Indigenous campsite coordinate or ceremony is supplied. [3,7]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** store, storage, stock support and family life were connected to changing transport routes. **Now:** the survivor group allows those relationships to be interpreted; it is not a complete operating stage depot. The NPS page distinguishes the vanished stone station, surviving log store/cellars, family home and a **reconstructed** summer house. Do not call the reconstruction an untouched 1860s building. [3]

**Then:** the 1979 nomination photographed buildings needing preservation. **Now:** a current comparison requires a new field photograph; neither its condition rating nor its private ownership checkbox is current. ISHS now states public ownership and cooperative management. [1,4]

**Then:** the site's transport importance changed as rail displaced long-distance wagon/coaching business. **Now:** use the store and road relationships to explain change, not a simplistic claim that a single rail arrival erased all local commerce instantly. [3,6]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Editorial 30–45-minute exterior allowance on clear ground; not a measured accessible route.

**Arrival:** locate the signed public entrance and current site rules. Read the place as a creek crossing and supply stop, not an empty land awaiting settlers.

**Store exterior:** compare the small building with the breadth of its documented functions. Open the 1979 photograph; identify changes only when visible, without guessing restoration dates.

**Storage/support relationship:** from the designated route, look toward interpreted cellar/outbuilding features without entering. Ask what could remain at the stop while travelers moved on. Use the documentary categories, not invented goods.

**Home/reconstruction view:** distinguish a household from a transport facility and a reconstructed building from an original. Do not collapse a century of occupation into one arrival date.

**Finish at the interpretation/monument near the store:** the historic commemorative marker and its corrective explanation provide the **strongest story moment**. The marker invokes “Pony Express,” but the added explanation explicitly rejects membership in the National Pony Express route. A visitor learns how history itself is corrected. Read the explanation from the public path if currently accessible; never step around a barrier to see its reverse. [9]
