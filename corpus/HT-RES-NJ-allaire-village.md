# HT-RES-NJ-allaire-village — A steam engine maker's iron town, kept at its peak year
Division NJ seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

The Howell Works: a bog-iron company town that James P. Allaire, a New York steam-engine builder, bought in 1822 to feed his own foundry — a self-contained village of furnace, mills, shops, a general store, a church, a school, and workers' houses on the Manasquan River, five miles from the bay. The furnace fires went out in 1846, the village emptied, and the empty village is what survived: the furnace stack and casting-house walls stand in ruin, and the rest of the town stands restored inside Allaire State Park, interpreted to a single year — 1836, its peak.

## 1. Trip gate
Allaire Village is a staffed museum village inside Allaire State Park, and it runs under two operators whose division of labor the New Jersey State Park Service's village page states plainly: the State Park Service maintains the buildings, while Allaire Village, Inc. administers the collections, programming, and events. The Park Service page, checked November 26, 2026, gives the practical terms by reference rather than by number: historic village hours are checked on the Allaire Village, Inc. website (allairevillage.org), grounds hours on the Allaire State Park page, an Allaire State Park entrance fee may apply, and program and event fees vary. The village's published contact is (732) 919-3500, and its address is The Historic Village at Allaire, 4263 Atlantic Avenue, Wall Township, NJ 07727. No fee or hour beyond what the Park Service itself states is printed in this entry.

The site's published rules bound the visit, as they do across the state park system: interior photography, videotaping, or audio taping are not permitted in the historic structures, visitor center, or museum except by prior written permission and for educational purposes only; commercial photography requires a special use permit, as do metal detecting and drones; the collection or removal of any object from State Park property is prohibited without the Superintendent's written permission; and the rules ask visitors to help preserve the site by not attaching anything to, climbing, or sitting on historic structures and features. That last rule is the boundary for the village's ruin: the furnace stack and casting-house walls are part of the interpreted site, read from its paths — not a climbing ground. Nothing in this entry directs a visitor off the village's paths or beyond the park's own terms.

## 2. The story
The ground here is the Manasquan River, five miles from the bay, in bog-iron country (National Register nomination, NRIS 74001174). The nomination records that the land was first known as Williamsburg Forge, and is careful about it: the forge's location is not determined. The Allaire State Park brochure dates the Williamsburg Forge to the early 1790s and records that by 1814 a furnace stood here called Monmouth Furnace.

James P. Allaire bought the property in 1822 (NJDEP; park brochure). He was a brass founder and steam-engine builder whose Allaire Works in New York City needed iron, and the nomination records the depth of that trade: Allaire had supplied marine engines for several of Robert Fulton's early steamboats between 1810 and 1815 — the nomination's own statement, printed here as its claim. The brochure gives the purchase's scale — a 5,000-acre enterprise — and its program: Allaire renamed the works the Howell Works, and rebuilt and expanded them between 1827 and 1837. The present furnace was built in 1831, replacing an earlier furnace and a still earlier forge (nomination; 1831 is one of the Register record's two significant years).

The work was a supply line with a town attached. Bog ore was smelted into pig iron and shipped to Allaire's New York foundry for further processing (NJDEP). The Park Service lists the finished castings: hollowware, stoves, and sash weights; the brochure adds water pipe, stove plates, and retail castings, and the steam-engine and boiler parts the whole enterprise existed to feed. Around the furnace stood a self-contained community — the brochure lists a church, a school, a general store, a bakery, carpenter and blacksmith shops, mills, and worker housing; the New Jersey Historic Trust's account adds a pattern-making shop, a post office, and a boarding house, and calls the place what it was: an iron-producing factory town. The nomination fixes the surviving town's dates: the buildings that stand — the brick store, the bakery, the carriage house, the carpenter's shop, the enamelling furnace, the worker cottages, the frame church — date from the Allaire period, 1822 to 1850, while the furnace stack and the casting-house walls stand in ruin.

At its peak the works were large. The Park Service's village page states that at its peak the works employed over 400 people; the park brochure counts a mid-1830s peak population of 200 to 300 residents. The two figures measure different things and print side by side in §6. The village today is interpreted to 1836, the peak year (NJDEP).

The decline came from outside. The brochure names its causes: high-grade Pennsylvania ore lying near coal, new smelting technology, and national depression. In 1846 the furnace fires were extinguished (brochure). The Allaire family held the property until 1901 — by then the empty works had taken the name it still carries in the records, Allaire Village, the "Deserted Village of Allaire" of the Historic District's formal name. Arthur Brisbane bought the property in 1907, and the Boy Scouts' Camp Burton was among its tenants (brochure). In 1941, 800 acres including the village were deeded to the State from Brisbane's estate; the park has grown to over 3,000 acres (brochure).

Preservation followed the State. Allaire Village, Inc. was founded in the late 1950s and still runs the village's collections and programming while the State Park Service maintains its buildings (NJDEP; brochure). The village was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on January 11, 1974, as a district significant for industry and architecture. The New Jersey Historic Trust has funded its fabric in recent years: a $342,410 grant in 2019, which helped fund removal of a bat colony and remediation in the General Store's attic, and a $150,000 grant in 2022 toward a new roof, HVAC system, and ADA modifications for the Enameling Building and a new roof and steeple for the Historic Chapel — and on October 1, 2026, the Trust announced a further $75,000 Historic Site Management grant recommended for Allaire Village, Inc. for this district.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a 5,000-acre works (brochure) smelting bog ore into pig iron for a New York steam-engine foundry, employing over 400 people at its peak (NJDEP). **Now:** a 330-acre listed historic district (nomination) inside a state park of over 3,000 acres (brochure), its surviving village core counted by the New Jersey Historic Trust at thirteen original structures on forty acres.

**Then:** furnace fires burning from the 1831 furnace until they were extinguished in 1846 (nomination; brochure). **Now:** the furnace stack and casting-house walls stand in ruin at the village's heart (nomination), while the shops around them work again as interpreted demonstrations under Allaire Village, Inc.

**Then:** a company town of a few hundred people with its own church, school, store, and post office. **Now:** a museum village interpreted to one year — 1836, its peak (NJDEP) — the town held at the moment before the decline its own ground records.

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its source attached:
- The interpreted village buildings named by the Park Service: a general store, blacksmith shop, carpenter's shop, owner's house, foreman's house, church, and carriage house, with a visitor center offering exhibits on the village's history (NJDEP)
- The furnace stack and the casting-house walls, standing in ruin (nomination)
- The nomination's surviving Allaire-period building list: the brick store, the bakery, the carriage house, the carpenter's shop, the enamelling furnace, the worker cottages, and the frame church, dated 1822–1850 (nomination)
- Thirteen original structures built between 1750 and 1835, on forty acres of land (New Jersey Historic Trust)
- The Enameling Building and the Historic Chapel, under Trust-funded roof, HVAC, ADA, and steeple work from the 2022 grant (New Jersey Historic Trust)
- The Historic American Buildings Survey's documentation of the village (survey NJ-28, "The Deserted Village") at the Library of Congress, including the 1936 church photograph in §7

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The peak of the works prints two ways. The Park Service's village page states that at its peak the works employed over 400 people; the park brochure counts 200 to 300 residents at the mid-1830s peak. Employees and residents are different units and the sources are different publications of the same agency; both print, unreconciled, and §2 carries them with their sources named.

Acreage prints at four scopes, each measuring a different thing: the nomination's listed district is approximately 330 acres; the New Jersey Historic Trust describes the current village as forty acres of land; the brochure records 800 acres, including the village, deeded to the State in 1941, and a park now over 3,000 acres. None of these corrects another, and none is merged here.

The village's beginning is genuinely open. The nomination gives the land's first known use as Williamsburg Forge and states that the forge's location is not determined; the Register record's periods of significance open at 1750–1799 with 1750 as a significant year, and the Historic Trust dates the thirteen original structures from 1750 — but the park brochure's narrative begins the forge only in the early 1790s, with the Monmouth Furnace by 1814. No source checked in this pass explains the 1750 date, and this entry prints it as the record carries it, unexplained, rather than building a founding story on it.

Structure counts likewise measure different things: the Historic Trust counts thirteen original structures standing today; the nomination lists the buildings of the Allaire period (1822–1850) that stood at listing. The counts are printed with their scopes and are not reconciled into one number.

The nomination's claim that Allaire supplied marine engines for Fulton's steamboats between 1810 and 1815 is the nomination's own statement. It is printed in §2 as attributed to the nomination; no independent primary source for it was checked in this pass.

Finally, coordinates: the nomination's coordinate figures are not legible enough in the scanned form as checked to transcribe verbatim, and no other primary source checked carries a verified point for the village. Secondary catalogs publish a point for Allaire; under this entry's rule it does not print, and §10's latitude and longitude stand blank.
