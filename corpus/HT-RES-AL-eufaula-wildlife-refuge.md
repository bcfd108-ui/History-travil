# Eufaula’s upland fields after the Walter F. George project

*On Old Ground · Alabama OP01 · Labeled research draft.*

## 2. Story
A river project and a refuge put two different federal purposes on the same regional landscape. The Army Corps of Engineers developed the Walter F. George Reservoir on the Chattahoochee. The Fish and Wildlife Service’s history records a December 1963 congressional appropriation for acquiring refuge lands and an October 6, 1964 permit from Army to Interior that established Eufaula National Wildlife Refuge. In its later official planning record, the Service explains, “The Service manages the refuge under a perpetual Lease Agreement with the Corps of Engineers.” Those instruments identify who acquired and manages the public landscape. [4–6]

The refuge’s subsequent work gave fields and water a different calendar. The 2009 management plan describes growing vegetation on managed moist soils and making habitat available when different birds need it. The operator’s present wildlife drive description explains fall flooding of an upland impoundment and gives late fall and winter as the best season for viewing. Managers decide when to manage water and vegetation. Cycles in what visitors see can therefore be the result of work as well as rainfall and migration. [2, 5]

The landscape had owners and workers before the federal project. The official refuge and management histories explain the public agencies and their reasons for acquiring land more fully than they preserve each farm’s experience. The original condemnation files, deed sequence and testimony of families affected by the reservoir were not established in this pass. The account does not infer consent, compensation outcomes or the exact location of individual lost parcels. [4–6]

## 3. Then and now
Today’s refuge contains upland and wetland units on two sides of the Chattahoochee. The modern viewing platform gives a public point from which to read a managed landscape in the Alabama Upland Unit. The approved scope is the platform’s landward approach only. The official map distinguishes public wildlife-drive roads, roads on levees, public platforms and areas closed to all entry. The landward platform approach can be identified on the map without using waterside segments. [1–3]
