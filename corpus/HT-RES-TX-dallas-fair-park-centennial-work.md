# Dallas Fair Park — Two halls and the work of the 1936 exposition
Research draft · Texas OP07 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

**“Texas welcomes the world.”** Dallas’s 2007 designation report quotes Secretary of Commerce Daniel Roper’s opening statement. When the Texas Centennial Exposition opened June 6, 1936, the report says, the Hall of State was unfinished. Around the main vista, architects and builders had already turned a state fairground into a centennial complex. The slogan is an invitation to see what the builders created and what the fair set apart. [3–4]

## 3 · Then / now

**Then — construction and representation.** The National Register nomination documents the materials and individual artists. Inside the Hall of State, Eugene Savage and assistants Reveau Bassett and James Buchanan Winn Jr. worked on murals. The marble flooring included inlaid San Saba stone. A city designation report credits Donald Barthelme with the facade design and describes the curved entrance recess. On another part of the grounds, the federally funded Hall of Negro Life was planned with the Negro Advisory Committee, designed by George Dahl, built by Cross Construction Company, and dedicated on Juneteenth. The African American Museum identifies its exhibits and Aaron Douglas’s murals. One hall was a state monument, and the other created a space for African American work and achievement at a fair built within a segregated society. [3–5]

**Now — a surviving axis and a lost hall.** The Esplanade still leads toward the Hall of State. The Hall of Negro Life was razed. The Dallas Historical Society’s ordinary visitor gallery offers interpretation inside the documented 1936 building. The African American Museum’s work offers a documented reading about the lost pavilion. Neither this dossier nor the 1985 photograph establishes visitor access to the lost hall’s building footprint. [2–6]

## 4 · What’s still there

- **Exterior only on open designated paths.** Esplanade, Hall of State front mass and nearby 1936 exhibition-building exteriors described by the nomination. [1,4]
- **Public interior during listed hours.** Hall of State galleries, with entry subject to event rules and room access. [2]
- **Not accessible as surviving fabric.** The demolished Hall of Negro Life. No separate physical hunt stop is assigned to this account. [5–6]

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Historic location.** Fair Park’s 1936 exposition grounds. The Hall of State survives. The Hall of Negro Life’s former exact ground is held for a map and public-access comparison. [4–6]
2. **Lawful anchor.** Enter by a designated open Fair Park gate and take the public walk alongside the Esplanade. The operator’s rules keep visitors out of fountains, closed grounds and event barriers. The Hall of State interior is optional when open. [1–2]
3. **Strongest story moment — The view and the missing hall.** End with the Hall of State’s curved stone center filling the view. Read Roper’s opening line, then read the African American Museum account of the building erected for Black achievement and subsequently razed. Take that second reading as quiet witness. [3–6]

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The city calls the Hall of State exterior Classical Modern while the federal nomination describes it as Art Deco. The labels reflect different stylistic readings. The city reports that the exhibition opened June 6 with the Hall of State unfinished. The state register’s district date spans the 1936–1937 fairs. The opening-day speech was read as quoted by the city. An official souvenir guide survives in a digitized extract of covers and foreword, not the full guide. Payroll and the original federal pavilion contract remain open in the City Secretary’s collections. The separate modern LOC image-item rights lookup returned HTTP 403. [3–4,6–9]
