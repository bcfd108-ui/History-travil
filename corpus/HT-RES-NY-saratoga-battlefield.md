# HT-RES-NY-saratoga-battlefield — Two autumn battles on the same farm clearings, and an army surrendered whole
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Rolling fields, deep ravines, and river bluffs above the Hudson — farmland in the town of Stillwater where, eighteen days apart in the fall of 1777, the same two armies fought over the same clearings, and where the second fight ended a British invasion from Canada, its army surrendered entire.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a battlefield, not a single building: Saratoga National Historical Park, the ground of the Battles of Saratoga, fought September 19 and October 7, 1777, in the town of Stillwater, Saratoga County, New York, with the park's Schuyler House, Saratoga Monument, and Victory Woods units some eight miles north in and around Schuylerville. The operator is the National Park Service. The NPS's own fees page states the terms, printed here as that page carries them: the park is free, and an entrance pass is **not required** to access Saratoga National Historical Park. The park's 2026 compendium, on its Laws & Policies page, carries the hours: battlefield grounds open sunrise to sunset; the Visitor Center open 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m. March 1 through November 30, and Friday through Sunday, 9:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m., December 1 through February 28; the Park Tour Road open to vehicles April 1 through November 30 on a seasonal schedule, closed to motor vehicles December 1 through March 31, and open to pedestrians and bicycles sunrise to sunset. Begin at the Visitor Center on the battlefield, at 648 Route 32, Stillwater. The park was added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000569, per the NPS's own National Register record).

## 2. The story
For centuries the Lake Champlain–Hudson River route had been, in the NPS Historical Handbook's words, a strategic highway through the northeast. The British plan for 1777, as the NPS park brochure carries it, was built on that highway: Gen. John Burgoyne would move south from Canada by Lake Champlain and the Hudson to Albany, a smaller force under Col. Barry St. Leger would support him eastward from Lake Ontario through the Mohawk Valley, and together with Sir William Howe's army from New York City they would sever New England from the other colonies. Burgoyne left St. Johns, Canada, on **June 17, 1777**. The brochure gives his force as 4,000 British regulars, 3,200 German auxiliaries, 250 Canadian and loyalist soldiers, 400 Iroquois and Algonquian warriors, and about 1,000 camp followers. His first major objective, Fort Ticonderoga, fell on July 6 after a four-day siege.

Then the plan came apart at its edges. Howe took his army against Philadelphia instead of north to Albany — the brochure notes he was already at sea and committed when London's conditional approval reached him, leaving few troops in New York to help Burgoyne. St. Leger stopped American militia at Oriskany on August 6 but, learning that a strong force under Gen. Benedict Arnold was approaching, raised his siege of Fort Stanwix and retreated to Canada. Worse followed on August 16, when Gen. John Stark's and Col. Seth Warner's New England troops shattered a detachment Burgoyne had sent toward Bennington, inflicting 900 casualties, per the brochure. All the while, Gen. Philip Schuyler, commanding the American Northern Department, impeded the advance with rough terrain made rougher — the delaying tactics both the brochure and the handbook credit with slowing Burgoyne's march through Skenesboro, Fort Ann, and Fort Edward. Schuyler did not keep the command he had prepared: on August 19, Gen. Horatio Gates replaced him, taking over an army growing as militia turned out. Who deserves the credit for what followed is a dispute the sources carry, printed as one in §6.

On September 13 Burgoyne crossed to the west bank of the Hudson at Saratoga (now Schuylerville) and marched south. Four miles north of Stillwater village his force came on the Americans, 9,000 strong in the brochure's figure, entrenched on Bemis Heights — where, as the brochure puts it, the road to Albany squeezed through a defile between the hills and the river, as today's U.S. 4 still does. Col. Thaddeus Kosciuszko, the Polish engineer serving with the Americans, had chosen and fortified the site; American artillery on the heights and in redoubts along the Hudson commanded river and road. Burgoyne had either to run the gauntlet under those guns or drive the Americans off the heights. He chose to fight.

On **September 19, 1777**, the British advanced on the American camp in three separate columns. American scouts detected the movement, and the armies met in a clearing known as Freeman's Farm, about a mile north of the American camp — an oblong clearing of some 15 acres, per the NPS handbook, where the heaviest fighting of both battles would fall. The fight seesawed across the clearing until German troops under Riedesel arrived and steadied the British line; at dusk the British held the field. They held it at a price the ground could not repay: the battle cost Burgoyne roughly twice the American loss (the figures themselves are disputed; see §6), and his army could not exploit what it had won. Burgoyne entrenched around Freeman's Farm, built the Balcarres and Breymann redoubts, and waited for help from New York that was not coming, while the American army in front of him grew.

On **October 7, 1777**, short of supplies and out of options, Burgoyne sent a flanking column of 1,500 soldiers against the American left. The handbook's guide places that column's first line on the southern slope of the rise north of the Middle Ravine, where the British met the full fury of the first American attack; it was in this area that Gen. Simon Fraser received his mortal wound and the British artillery and grenadier commanders, Major Williams and the badly wounded Major Acland, were taken prisoner. The American counterattack rolled west: the Balcarres Redoubt, the strongest British work — some 500 yards of log-and-earth wall, 12 to 14 feet high, mounting eight cannon, per the handbook — was assailed repeatedly in vain, but Benedict Arnold, relieved of his command by Gates after the September battle and on the field anyway, took part in the closing assault on the Breymann Redoubt at the British right, the key to the whole position. The handbook states the consequence plainly: Arnold's successful assault on the Breymann Redoubt outflanked Balcarres, forced its evacuation, and sealed the fate of the Burgoyne Campaign. Arnold was wounded in the leg in the attack — the wound the Boot Monument at the redoubt site now marks. Fraser died of his wounds and was buried at the Great Redoubt on the river bluffs, from where, the handbook records, the British began their retreat northward.

The retreat went ten miles and no farther. Surrounded at Saratoga (now Schuylerville), Burgoyne surrendered his entire army to Gates on **October 17, 1777** — a 5,700-man army, in the figure the NPS's biography of Gates carries (see §6 for the other figures). The consequence outran the ground: the NPS Gates biography states that this victory, a major turning point of the Revolution, convinced France to recognize American independence and form a military alliance with the Americans in 1778. The handbook adds the home-front half: though four more years of fighting were needed to reach Yorktown, the success brightened a desperate cause when failure would have been disastrous.

The ground was then kept, in stages. The battlefield was first authorized as a New York state park in 1927, on the battles' sesquicentennial, and Saratoga National Historical Park was authorized in 1938; the Saratoga Monument in Victory — a granite obelisk whose cornerstone was laid on the surrender's centennial, October 17, 1877, and completed in 1883 — became part of the park in 1980. The National Register listing is dated October 15, 1966 in the NPS Register record.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a defile where the Albany road squeezed between Bemis Heights and the Hudson, fortified by Kosciuszko, with American guns on the bluffs commanding river and road. **Now:** the same bluffs and corridor, U.S. 4 running where the road ran, the heights walked and driven on the park's 9.5-mile tour road, the American line's outline marked in white posts with blue tops at the Neilson farm stop, per the NPS tour text.

**Then:** farm clearings in heavy woods — Freeman's 15-acre oblong clearing, the Barber wheatfield — fought over on September 19 and again on October 7. **Now:** open, mown battlefield ground; the handbook notes the park's present open clearings stand in sharp contrast to its heavily wooded appearance in 1777, and few vestiges of either army's fortifications survive above ground.

**Then:** a small red farmhouse on the heights, built 1775 or 1776, emptied by its owners and taken as quarters by Generals Arnold and Poor. **Now:** the Neilson House, restored, at Tour Stop 2 — the only standing structure on the battlefield from the time of the battles, per the NPS.

## 4. What's still there
- The battlefield ground itself in Stillwater: Bemis Heights, Freeman's Farm, the Balcarres and Breymann redoubt sites, the Great Redoubt bluffs, and the ravines — more than 3,000 acres, per the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation's park account — read through the NPS's 9.5-mile, ten-stop tour road (NPS audio tour page)
- The Neilson House at Tour Stop 2, the battlefield's only contemporary building, quarters of Generals Arnold and Poor in 1777 (NPS Neilson House page and Historical Handbook)
- The Boot Monument at the Breymann Redoubt site (Tour Stop 7), marking where Arnold was wounded on October 7 — a monument to a leg, naming no man (NPS Historical Handbook)
- The Visitor Center at 648 Route 32, with exhibits and a film, and the Wilkinson Trail across the battlefield (NPS park pages)
- Eight miles north: the Schuyler House in Schuylerville — erected by Gen. Philip Schuyler in 1777 shortly after the surrender, replacing the house Burgoyne's troops burned on October 10, 1777 (NPS Historical Handbook) — the Saratoga Monument in Victory, and Victory Woods, Burgoyne's army's last encampment

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Visitor Center, 648 Route 32:** Start where the NPS starts the visit — exhibits, the film, and the tour map, free as the NPS fees page states, before the one-way tour road.
2. **At Stop 2, Neilson's Farm:** The American line's western anchor — the marked fortification outline, the abatis slope, and the one house that stood here in 1777, with Kosciuszko's monument at the parking lot's head (NPS tour stop text).
3. **At Stop 3, Bemis Heights:** The defile itself — the ridge where the American line's right anchored on the river bluffs and the guns commanded the road.
4. **At Stop 6, Freeman's Farm:** The clearing where the fighting of September 19 began and around which both battles turned, later enclosed inside the Balcarres Redoubt line.
5. **At Stop 7 and Stop 9:** The Breymann Redoubt, where Arnold's closing assault on October 7 unhinged the British position — the Boot Monument stands here — and the Great Redoubt, where Fraser was buried and the retreat north began; then the eight miles north to the Schuyler House, the Saratoga Monument, and Victory Woods, where the surrender story ends.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The casualty figures do not agree across the sources, and the disagreements are printed rather than resolved. For Freeman's Farm (September 19), George Washington's Mount Vernon gives American losses of 65 killed, 218 wounded, and 36 missing, and British losses of 600 killed, wounded, and captured in total — its narrative rounds the same fight to almost 600 British killed or wounded, roughly twice the American losses. For Bemis Heights (October 7), Mount Vernon gives 150 American killed and wounded and again 600 British killed, wounded, and captured. The *Dictionary of American History* account of the second battle, carried by Encyclopedia.com, gives a different anatomy: Gates's losses at about 30 killed and 100 wounded, against Burgoyne's 184 killed, 264 wounded, and 183 captured, with Riedesel's Germans separately at 94 killed, 67 wounded, and 102 prisoners — British-side totals well above Mount Vernon's round 600 for that day. Both accounts are carried as their sources state them; this draft does not reconcile them, and no single casualty figure for either battle is asserted in §2.

The surrender's size splits the same way. The NPS biography of Gates gives the surrendered force as a 5,700-man army; Mount Vernon's account describes an army of nearly 6,000; the precise figure of 5,752 circulates widely in secondary summaries. Army strengths are likewise source-dependent — the NPS brochure carries the Americans at 9,000 when Burgoyne met them, while Mount Vernon's table gives 7,000 Americans at Freeman's Farm and 11,000 at Bemis Heights. Each figure is printed with its source; none is harmonized.

The credit dispute is carried by the sources themselves, not settled here. The NPS brochure and handbook credit Schuyler's delaying tactics with slowing Burgoyne and Gates with the command that won the battles; the NPS Gates biography gives Gates the victory plain. Against that, the New York State Military Museum's account argues the victory was impossible without Schuyler's direction, calls Schuyler the architect of the victorious campaign, and states that Gates received the credit through sectional politics while Schuyler was condemned — noting his acquittal by court martial in 1778. The *Dictionary of American History* account adds that the Gates–Schuyler controversy has distorted Arnold's role ever since: Schuyler's partisans tend to exaggerate his impact, Gates's advocates to minimize it. All three positions are printed; this draft takes none of them.

The NPS National Register record for the park (66000569) carries no latitude or longitude. The one NPS point found — on the park's NPGallery asset records (42.9943008422852, −73.6440963745117) — is repeated identically across the park's assets as a park-level point, while a federal recreation listing carries a different point again (42.995226, −73.633861); neither is verified for the battlefield ground, so the locator prints no coordinates, and lead-list coordinates were not used.
