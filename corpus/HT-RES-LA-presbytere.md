# The Presbytère — Casa Curial, courthouse, and Louisiana State Museum
HT-RES-LA-presbytere · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The building was designed to complete a set. In 1791 the French-born architect Gilberto Guillemard, in the military service of Spain, designed a rectory for St. Louis Cathedral to match the Cabildo he was designing for the other side of the church. The pair, with the cathedral between them, were to give the Place d'Armes a formal Spanish colonial front: two matching two-story buildings flanking the church, each with an arcaded ground floor and a gallery above. The Presbytère's original name was Casa Curial — Ecclesiastical House — and it stood on the site of the Capuchin monks' residence, the *presbytère* from which its later French name derives. The foundations were set before the Cabildo's, sometime before the fire of 1794. [1, 2, 3]

Construction then stalled for a generation. The nomination records that work halted in 1798 with only one story standing, halted, in its account, by Don Andrés Almonester y Rojas while he was engaged on the Cabildo; the Landmarks Commission marker transcribed on HMdb gives the halt to Almonester's death in 1796. Either way the unfinished single story stood on the square through the Louisiana Purchase and the first American decade, and the wardens of St. Louis Cathedral completed the second story only in 1813, to Guillemard's design, with Gurlie and Guillot as builders in the marker's account. As completed about 1813 it was a two-story brick building with a flat balustraded roof topped by urns: the lower arcade formed by slightly elliptical arches springing from square piers with Doric pilasters, the upper story similar with Ionic pilasters between glazed arches, and a central pediment crowning the center three bays. The National Park Service and the nomination agree on the judgment that followed: together with the Cabildo, it is among the finest surviving examples of Spanish colonial formal public building architecture in the United States, and the two together make one of the most important architectural plazas in the country. [2, 3, 4]

The rectory never housed a priest. The nomination states it plainly — the building was never used for its intended purpose. The museum's own history gives the first use as commercial, until 1834. The nomination gives a more specific early tenancy the museum shorthand omits: the United States government rented the space for its lower courts, the court of appeals and the supreme court from 1813 until 1833. Both accounts then converge on a courthouse life: in 1834, in the museum's dating, it became a courthouse, and the Louisiana Supreme Court's long tenancy followed — the museum's social-media history in this pass gives 1822 to 1853 for the supreme court and judicial use, a range that does not reconcile cleanly with either the federal-court tenancy or the 1834 date, and it is printed as a conflict below rather than harmonized. In March 1831 the City of New Orleans paid the cathedral wardens $10,500 for land in the rear of the Presbytère; the building itself was bought by the city from the wardens for $55,000 in 1853. Rear wings were added in 1840. [1, 2, 4]

In 1847 the building was altered to keep its twinship. When the mansard roof was added to the Cabildo that year, the Presbytère received the same French-style mansard, with dormers and a cupola, so the pair still matched across the cathedral. The National Park Service attributes the mid-century pressure in part to Baroness Micaela Almonester Pontalba — Almonester's daughter, who had funded the original work — whose French-inspired renovations to her own Jackson Square buildings prompted the city to improve its buildings in kind. The nomination notes the exterior was otherwise little altered after 1847; the interior was restored and renovated in 1962–63 to make modern offices on the third floor, with a natural science museum then in the lower section. [2, 3]

Ownership passed to the state for museum use. In 1911, in the nomination's wording, ownership was transferred to the State of Louisiana for use as a museum, as was the Cabildo; secondary summaries divide that into a city sale to the state in 1908 and a museum opening in 1911, the same two-stage pattern recorded for the Cabildo. It has remained a Louisiana State Museum building since, administered from 751 Chartres Street, with the offices for museum operations in the upper story at the time of the nomination. [2, 4]

The roofline a visitor sees today is partly a twenty-first-century restoration. The original cupola, added with the 1847 mansard, lasted more than sixty years until the hurricane of September 29, 1915 demolished its exterior structure; for lack of funds the roof was patched without a cupola and stayed bare for almost a century. Damage from Hurricane Georges on September 28, 1998, compounded by a hailstorm, forced a major roof and exterior project, and the state chose to restore the building to its appearance when the museum acquired it in 1911. Field work found the original heavy timber ring that had carried the cupola still in place in the attic, and the new cupola — designed by Yeates & Yeates to match the Cabildo's — was raised on that ring, with completion scheduled for October 2005 and the replacement dated to 2005 in the summaries inspected. The restored cupola therefore survived Hurricane Katrina weeks after its return, though that survival is noted here only as chronology; no Katrina damage account for this building was retrieved in this pass. [5]

Today the museum's two permanent exhibitions tell, in its own framing, two sides of an ongoing Louisiana story — celebration and resilience. *Mardi Gras: It's Carnival Time in Louisiana* traces the festival from medieval roots through parade floats a visitor can climb, costumes, historical throws, the secretive social clubs from which modern krewes evolved, and Mardi Gras music in albums, records and sheet music. *Living with Hurricanes: Katrina and Beyond* documents rescue, rebuilding and renewal after Hurricane Katrina struck a badly engineered levee system, left 80 percent of the city flooded and hundreds dead, and follows southeast Louisiana's ongoing recovery through interactive exhibits and artifacts. A building raised for clergy, used by courts, and kept by a museum now holds the state's carnival and its catastrophe under one mansard roof. [1]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The northeast flank of Jackson Square (the Place d'Armes) in New Orleans' French Quarter, Orleans Parish — on the former Capuchin monastery and residence site, beside St. Louis Cathedral toward St. Ann Street. Designed 1791, foundations before the 1794 fire, one story by 1798, completed 1813. [1, 2, 3]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The Presbytère, Louisiana State Museum, 751 Chartres Street, New Orleans, LA 70116. Use the public museum entrance on Chartres Street facing the square and the museum's exhibition floors. Jackson Square in front is public ground on the city's park hours and is part of the same visit; the cathedral between the Presbytère and the Cabildo is a separate active church with its own visiting hours, covered in its own dossier. ## 5. Public progression

Start on the square and read the front as one of a pair: find the Cabildo across the cathedral and check the Presbytère against it — same arcade, same pilaster rhythm, same mansard and cupola — then remember that this twin was finished fifteen years later, one story at a time, and never did the job on its plans. Enter from Chartres Street and take the two permanent exhibitions as the building's present argument: *Mardi Gras* for the city at celebration, *Living with Hurricanes* for the city in disaster and recovery, in that order if the floors allow, so the Katrina rooms land after the carnival rooms rather than before them. On the way out, look up at the cupola and hold its dates: 1847, destroyed 1915, absent for ninety years, restored 2005. **Strongest story moment · The rectory that never was.** Every institution that actually used this building — merchants, federal courts, the state supreme court, a museum — was a substitute for the clergy residence it was designed to be. The architecture is the intention; the history is the improvisation. [1–5]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Address in the nomination.** The 1975 nomination form lists the property at 713 Chartres Street and the owner, the State of Louisiana administered by the Louisiana State Museum, at 751 Chartres Street. The museum's current address is 751 Chartres Street, which governs for the visit; the 713 numbering is printed so a reader matching the nomination is not misled. The Cabildo dossier records the same pattern in reverse for that building. [1, 2]
- **Why construction halted.** The nomination says construction was halted in 1798 by Don Almonester y Rojas, then engaged on the Cabildo. The Landmarks Commission marker transcribed on HMdb says construction was halted when Almonester died in 1796. The French Quarter cupola account gives a third variant, construction begun in 1797. All three are printed; the one-story-by-1798 state of the building is the point on which the nomination and the National Park Service agree. [2, 3, 4, 5]
- **Court tenancy dates.** The museum's short history says commercial use until 1834, then a courthouse. The nomination says the United States government rented the building for its lower courts, court of appeals and supreme court from 1813 to 1833. A Louisiana State Museum social-media history retrieved in this pass gives 1822–1853 for the Louisiana Supreme Court and judicial house and a courthouse use continuing to 1911. These ranges overlap without reconciling; no court-by-court tenancy table was retrieved in this pass, and none is constructed here. [1, 2]
- **State transfer date.** The nomination dates the transfer to the State of Louisiana to 1911. Secondary summaries divide it into a city sale to the state in 1908 and museum use from 1911. Both are printed, as for the Cabildo; no deed-level source was retrieved in this pass. [2, 4]
- **Cupola completion.** The restoration account, written during the work, schedules completion for October 2005; summaries date the replacement to 2005, one social-media account to July 2005. The year 2005 is firm in the sources inspected; the month is not printed as verified. [5]
- **National Historic Landmark date.** The National Register record gives reference number 70000257, published April 15, 1970, with significant years 1791 and 1813. Landmark designation in 1970 is stated consistently in the summaries inspected, but the designation day was not verified against the NPS Landmark record in this pass beyond the Register publication date, and no separate day is printed. [6]
- **Haunted lore.** No haunted claim for the Presbytère was retrieved from a primary or museum source in this pass. None is stated.
