# HT-RES-WI-cana-island-lighthouse — An 1869 brick tower in a steel skin, at the end of a causeway the lake covers when it pleases
Division WI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

The federal government built this light in 1869 on a small island off Baileys Harbor, between two natural harbors where ships in peril could run for shelter — and for its first half-century the island could only be reached by boat. The causeway that replaced the boat is itself conditional: the operator states plainly that high lake levels sometimes put water over it, and the crossing is made on foot over rock or in a tractor-drawn haywagon. The tower, the keeper's dwelling, and the oil house all stand, the light is still an active aid to navigation, and the island is a Door County park run by the Door County Maritime Museum.

## 1. Trip gate
This trip gate leads with the crossing, because at Cana Island the crossing is the visit's defining condition, set by the lake, not the visitor. The Door County Maritime Museum, which operates the site for Door County, states the access reality on its Cana Island page (checked November 26, 2026): "Please be aware that due to high lake levels, water sometimes flows over the causeway to Cana Island. We offer a complimentary tractor and haywagon ride to shuttle you over to the island. If you do choose to walk across, be aware that the causeway is a rocky pathway. Those with mobility challenges might want to use the tractor ride." Door County's own park page carries the same condition: "The Island is accessible by a causeway that you can walk across or enjoy a complementary wagon ride. Depending on the water levels of Lake Michigan, the causeway can be covered by water." There is no driving onto the island; the mainland side of the visit is the parking at 8800 East Cana Island Road, Baileys Harbor.

Within that condition, the operator's stated terms are these. The island is open May through October, seven days a week, 10 a.m. to 5 p.m.; the last tower climb starts at 4:30 p.m.; the Cana Tractor runs on a limited weekly schedule — Mondays and Wednesdays 12:30–3 p.m., Fridays, Saturdays, and Sundays 10 a.m.–5 p.m. — not continuously, and the operator directs visitors to its Cana Island Facebook page for daily updates and weather-related closures. The tower's gallery is reached by climbing the 97 steps of the spiral staircase; the crossing and the tower are not universally accessible on any reading, and the operator's mobility guidance above governs. The county records that admission fees exist and are designated for preservation and restoration; no amount is printed here because none was verified from the operator in this pass. Outside the operator's season the museum operation is closed, and the causeway in winter water and ice is not a stop this entry makes.

## 2. The story
The light exists because of where the island sits. The 1976 National Register nomination puts it directly: in 1869 the federal government erected a lighthouse on Cana Island "strategically situated between two good natural harbors, North and Moonlight bays, where ships in peril could readily find refuge from Lake Michigan storms," on a shoreline of "reefs and headlands" and "scores of hidden shoals and dangerous passages" (the nomination, through the older county histories), along a peninsula whose "Death's Door" passage the Sturgeon Bay–Lake Michigan Ship Canal would not bypass until 1881.

Built in 1869 for $12,792.55 (Door County), the tower of Cream City brick was the tallest building in Door County. The Coast Guard Historian's record gives the fabric: dressed-stone foundation, yellow brick, cylindrical tower, attached to the keeper's dwelling; the nomination describes the dwelling as a 1½-story brick house joined to the free-standing tower by a small brick gable-roofed passageway, with a small stone oil house, a brick outhouse, and a frame storage shed on the cleared ground southwest of the tower. The county dates the first official lighting of the beacon to January 28 — the year is one of this entry's printed conflicts (§6) — and records 4,862 ships counted passing the station in the first year of operation, daylight hours, verified by telescope. The original lens was a third-order Fresnel (Coast Guard Historian, dated there to 1870).

The nomination, quoting Roland's History of Door County, records the storm of October 16, 1880 — among the worst ever to hit the peninsula: "The waves ran so high that at the lighthouse the sea frequently broke over the house. The Lantern at a height of 88 feet was at times completely covered with spray from the huge waves."

The tower's brick was later wrapped in steel. The nomination dates the steel shell to 1901; the Coast Guard Historian dates it to 1902 — the conflict prints in §6 — but both agree on the work: the brick tower was encased in steel, the space between steel and brick filled with concrete (Coast Guard), and the tower painted white. The banded tower in the 1913 photograph in §7 is that steel skin, a dozen years on.

For its first decades the station was an island in fact. The nomination states that for many years the island could only be reached by boat, and that "a gravel causeway built from the mainland ca. 1917 now makes the island accessible by foot except in time of high water." The keepers' era ended with automation in the 1940s — the Coast Guard's own records give three different years, all printed in §6. Power lines were run to the island in 1960 (Coast Guard Historian). The island's residents were not only keepers: the McCarthy family leased a summer residence from the Coast Guard for 25 years from 1945, and Louis and Rosie Janda were caretakers from 1976 to 1995 (Door County).

The preservation era began while the light still worked — as it still does. The Door County Maritime Museum began managing the property in partnership with the U.S. Coast Guard in 1970 (Door County). The light station was listed in the National Register of Historic Places on November 21, 1976. On April 27, 2006, the Bureau of Land Management transferred the island to Door County, free of charge, for use as a park, preserving the lighthouse with public access; the county partners with the Maritime Museum to manage it, and fees are designated for preservation and restoration. In 2008 the tower was opened for the public to climb (Door County). The Coast Guard still carries the light as operational and automated — an active aid to navigation that is also a museum.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** an island of 3.6 hectares reachable only by boat (nomination). **Now:** a causeway — gravel, built about 1917 by the nomination's account — that the lake still covers at high water, crossed on foot or by the operator's tractor-drawn wagon; the island is a nine-acre county park (Door County).

**Then:** a keeper climbing 97 steps through the night with the lamp's fuel — the operator evokes him "trudg[ing] up and down the steps each night, carrying heated lard to keep the light burning." **Now:** the same 97 steps are the visitor's climb to the gallery deck, and the light itself is automated, tended without a resident keeper (Coast Guard Historian).

**Then:** the Bureau of Lighthouses' July 1913 photograph (§7) — the steel-banded tower and the brick dwelling from 200 feet southwest, twelve years after the encasement. **Now:** the same pairing stands on the same cleared ground, the dwelling kept as the keeper's home museum, the tower climbable since 2008 (Door County).

**Then:** a working federal light station, owned by the Coast Guard (nomination, 1976). **Now:** the island is Door County's — transferred by the Bureau of Land Management in 2006 — and run by the county's maritime museum (Door County).

## 4. What's still there
Only what the sources checked establish, each with its date attached:
- The light tower of 1869 — Cream City (yellow) brick on a dressed-stone foundation, encased in a steel shell (1901 per the nomination; 1902 per the Coast Guard Historian — §6) and painted white, rising about 27 meters (nomination) — 89 feet in the operator's and county's current figures; still an active, automated aid to navigation (Coast Guard Historian, checked November 26, 2026)
- The keeper's dwelling — 1½-story brick, attached to the tower by a brick passageway (nomination, 1976), kept and interpreted as the keeper's home (Door County Maritime Museum)
- The oil house — the small stone building southwest of the tower where the lamp fuel was stored (nomination; operator); the nomination calls it pentagonal and the county calls it "the only six-sided stone structure of its kind in the Great Lakes" — the disagreement is printed in §6
- The brick outhouse (privy) and frame storage shed recorded by the 1976 nomination and documented with the oil house in the Historic American Buildings Survey's measured drawings (HABS WI-376, Library of Congress)
- The causeway, built about 1917 by the nomination's account, still the only land connection, still subject to high water (nomination; operator)
- The island itself — rocky shoreline and footpaths, no designated trails (Door County)
- Offshore, the wreck of the Frank O'Connor, a 300-foot steamer built in 1892 that burned to the waterline in 1919, lying off the island's shore as a dive site (Door County) — recorded here as the county carries it; it is a water stop, not a land one

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the conditions are the operator's: season, hours, and a crossing the lake can close, as §1 bounds them:

1. **At the mainland parking, 8800 East Cana Island Road:** Read the causeway before crossing it. Whether it is dry rock, wet rock, or under water decides the crossing — foot, wagon, or not today — and the operator's daily word governs.
2. **On the causeway:** The station's first half-century was lived by boat; the gravel bar under your feet, dated by the nomination to about 1917, is the machine that ended the island's isolation — except, as the nomination already said in 1976, "in time of high water."
3. **On the cleared ground, at the dwelling and oil house:** The keeper's working yard as the nomination recorded it in 1976 — dwelling, passageway, oil house. Count the oil house's sides against the two descriptions in §6.
4. **Up the tower:** 97 spiral steps to the gallery deck (operator; county) — the keeper's nightly road, now the visit's crown, last climb at 4:30 p.m. by the operator's schedule.
5. **From the gallery deck:** North Bay and Moonlight Bay — the two refuge harbors the nomination names as the light's whole reason — with the Door Peninsula and Lake Michigan laid out between them.
6. **Along the shore footpaths:** The rocky edge of the island (county), with the Frank O'Connor lying offshore — a wreck for divers, read from land as a name and a date only.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The steel shell is dated two ways by the two best sources. The 1976 nomination states the tower "was encased in a steel shell in 1901." The Coast Guard Historian's station record states in its data block "1902 STEEL SHELL ADDED" and in its history notes, "In 1902 the brick tower was encased in a steel shell with the space between the steel and the brick filled with concrete." This entry prints both and uses neither as settled; the 1913 photograph in §7 shows the shell in place by then, which is all the photograph settles.

The first lighting is similarly unsettled. The Coast Guard's data block reads "Station Established: 1870" and "Year Current Tower(s) First Lit: 1869" — the service's own two fields point at two different years — and dates the original third-order Fresnel lens to 1870. Door County gives the day and month — "the first official lighting of the beacon was on January 28" — without a year, and secondary accounts supplying 1870 were not treated as primary in this pass. Section 2 therefore prints January 28 with its year open, and the tower's completion in 1869, which all sources support.

Automation carries three dates, all from official records: the Coast Guard Historian's history notes say "In 1941 the light was automated with power supplied by a generator"; Door County says keepers kept the beacon "until the lighthouse was automated in 1944"; the Coast Guard's own data block reads "Automated? YES 1945." No reconciliation is attempted; §2 says only "the 1940s."

The oil house's geometry conflicts in plain sight: the nomination describes "a small, single-story pentagonal stone building," while Door County calls the oil house "the only six-sided stone structure of its kind in the Great Lakes." Five sides or six is a question the building itself answers; the records do not agree, and both claims print under their sources' names.

Three lesser items print as found. The nomination, as digitized, gives the focal plane as "203.6 meters M.S.L." — inconsistent with the same form's 27-meter tower, and not carried in this entry. The National Register's own asset metadata for the property lists a significant year of 1865, which the nomination form itself does not carry and nothing in the sources checked explains. And the Coast Guard Historian's page (dated October 21, 2019) still describes the island as "currently being leased from the Bureau of Land Management by the Door County Maritime Museum" — superseded by the county's record of the April 27, 2006 ownership transfer; the page is used here for fabric and light status, not ownership. Finally, the keepers themselves: the sources checked name no keeper roster for the station's working years — the county names only later residents and caretakers — so no keeper is named in this entry, and the ca. 1917 causeway date rests on the nomination's single carried citation of Roland's History of Door County. The property point is likewise unsettled: the nomination form's UTM reference (§10, carried there as filed and not converted) falls roughly 200 meters south of the latitude secondary listings print (45.0883, −87.0483); the difference is unreconciled, and no decimal point prints in this entry.
