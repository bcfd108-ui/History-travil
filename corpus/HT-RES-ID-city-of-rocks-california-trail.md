# City of Rocks · The California Trail signatures

History Travel · Idaho · Draft for Dash QC · Desk review 18 November 2026

## 2. The story

At Register Rock, two travelers used a material they already had on their wagons. The marks in axle grease name A. Freeman and D. Tickner and give the same date, June 12, 1850. The National Park Service identifies both names at this particular rock. No travel diary has to be matched to a stranger’s handwriting for the marks to tell you that two people were there that day. [4]

The diaries describe a much less certain business than following a line on a modern map. On June 22 of the same year, Lorenzo Sawyer wrote about the difficulty of deciding which of several formations deserved to be called Steeple Rock. He used the point where another road came in to explain what he saw. Two months later James Bennett recorded halting for a midday meal in the shade of a rock already covered in names, with running water nearby. These entries describe navigation, shade and water. They do not identify Bennett’s lunch rock as the modern Register Rock. [6]

The women who kept journals did more than repeat the trail name. In 1849 Sallie Hester saw “high cliffs of rocks resembling old ruins or dilapidated buildings.” In April 1850 Lucena Parsons predicted that the stone would soon crumble away. In July, Margaret Frink preferred the name Pyramid City. Helen Carpenter in 1857 wrote about women and children going out to look around while the stock was cared for. Read them in different voices. A town in ruins, an unstable substance, a proposed name and an outing are four responses to the same country. [6]

Camp Rock still bears names and dates that the park places within 1843–82. The 1868 King Survey photograph brought yet another way of describing this country: a mounted albumen print, numbered in a survey series. The NPS landscape inventory and landmark file let a reader follow the wagon corridor at a wider scale than any one photograph. The inscriptions are unusually fragile historical documents. A person can read one from the trail without touching the rock. [5, 7, 8, 11, 12]

The trail crossed a country already known and used by Northern Shoshone and Bannock people. NPS identifies its continuing importance to descendants at Fort Hall. The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes’ Language and Cultural Preservation Department describes its own work as preserving languages and traditions and protecting sensitive information. Follow only the established emigrant waysides. No gathering areas or activities are mapped or used for a find. [15, 16]

## 3. Then / now

A route and its junctions once had to be recognized from the country itself. At present two named features have signed public interpretation. Axle grease on exposed rock, a line in a diary and a survey photograph are separately identifiable records. A date readable on a rock is evidence for the people who left that particular inscription. [4–8]

## 4. What’s still there

- **View from public ground** — Register Rock and its wayside. Read the names from the designated viewing area. [4]
- **View from public ground** — Camp Rock and the short public trail, approximately 330 feet according to NPS. [5]
- **Public interior** — Almo visitor center on its dated winter schedule. If interior roads are blocked, it is the staffed alternative. Subject to same-day operating conditions. [1–3]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Begin in Almo. Check the condition of the interior road and public walking surfaces before deciding to go farther. If held, use the NPS close-up and journal excerpts at the visitor center. [1–4, 6]
2. When reachable, walk the signed portion at Camp Rock and read Bennett’s account of a meal in the shade. Keep his unnamed rock and the modern named one distinct. [5, 6]
3. **Strongest story moment** — finish at Register Rock with the two dated names. First read them on the NPS caption, then see which strokes are legible in the day’s light from the public viewing area. Two people signed on the same day. Whether they knew each other is still an open question. The winter version ends at the NPS close-up. [4]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The same operator page carries an undated Wednesday–Sunday footer and a November 2026–mid-April 2027 notice giving Tuesday–Saturday. Use the dated notice. The agency also posts two planned Almo-area power outages for November 20, 2026, 8–9 a.m. and 4–5 p.m. These are interruption windows, not a declared all-day closure. [1]

The NPS page dates inscriptions across the Camp Rock record to 1843–82. These are not dates for every signature at Register Rock. No manuscript image of the diaries was compared here. The passages used are the NPS transcriptions. No owner, destination or relationship is assigned to Freeman or Tickner beyond what their inscriptions support. [4–6]

## 7. Photo record

**Timothy H. O’Sullivan, 1868, City of Rocks, Idaho.** The Nelson-Atkins Museum of Art catalogues its albumen print on its King Survey mount, number 81 in the series, object 2005.27.3273. The Library of Congress also indexes a print under the title. Its full item viewer returned an error. The catalogue does not justify identifying a particular wayside as O’Sullivan’s position. No Photo Point for this print. [7, 13]

**Blanche H. Schroer, September 1975, Twin Sisters**, National Register photo form. **Photo Point — APPROXIMATE**, south of the pair looking north. The form records the southern view. The two separate summits and the lower granite ridge beside them control the alignment. High confidence in general facing, low in exact distance or legal standing point. Use only an established public viewpoint with that alignment in open conditions. It is an archive comparison, outside the required two-rock walking sequence. [8, 11]
