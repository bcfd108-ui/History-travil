## 2. The story
**Original interpretation:** Florence is a story about the gap between rich ground and enough rich ground. A spectacular return could draw a crowd much larger than the deposits could support. The resulting movement of people does not make neighboring mining districts interchangeable.

ISHS's *Mining in Idaho* places the Florence rush in late 1861, the major production year in 1862, and subsequent decline alongside later Chinese mining and a renewed quartz promotion in 1896. Its separate Elk City entry describes a different district on the South Fork Clearwater. People moving between them are evidence of a connected mining economy, not a reason to give Florence Elk City's landmarks, statistics or approach roads. [5]

**Named work, not just outlaw lore:** the official *Lure of Gold* brochure reproduces storekeeper **Alonzo Brown's** account: **“I slept in the store on the floor”**. His description of defensive sleeping arrangements shows the vulnerability of ordinary commerce, although the original recollection/manuscript has not been collated here. The same publication describes **Billy Courtney** being hired to keep snow off buildings after the boom. These are attributed historical accounts, not invented firsthand speech or an assertion that either building survives. [6]

**Transport labor with specific newspaper leads:** ISHS identifies **George Shearer** carrying mail and passengers through Florence in 1886, and **Fred Riggins and James Lytle** operating a four-horse stage in 1896. Its citations include *Idaho County Free Press*, July 16 and September 24, 1886; July 3, 1896. Those newspaper issues are research leads here, not scans independently read. [7]

**Published Tribal voice:** the Nez Perce Tribe characterizes the gold rush as **“mass trespass and theft”** within its reservation and describes the forced reduction of the reservation in 1863. That published account prevents the camp's story from beginning with supposedly empty land. It is not permission to visit sensitive places or to speak on behalf of the Tribe. [8]

A primary survey-related letter survives in the university catalog: **William L. Spalding to H. K. Moore, Florence, February 10, 1901**, two pages. Its catalog establishes a named record of mining-claim work; the letter itself was not retrieved, so no quotation, survey outcome or claim coordinates are invented. [9]

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** placer work concentrated people around limited deposits. **Now:** the surviving evidence includes landscape, records and a managed interpretation site, not an intact boomtown. [1,5]

**Then:** Old Florence and the later New Florence were separate settlement phases. **Now:** an 1896 photograph explicitly labeled Old Florence must retain that identity; neither its date nor the new boom turns it into a photograph of New Florence. [5,10]

**Then:** hand methods processed gold-bearing gravel. **Now:** the agency brochure discusses rockers, long toms and pans, plus altered streams and collapsing tunnel hazards. That is a reason to interpret machinery remotely or on panels, not to enter a working or try mining. [2]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
**Current version is remote; future on-site sequence awaits access QC.** No routes, travel times or winter arrival promise.

1. **Begin with Old Florence in 1896.** Establish its date and settlement identity before discussing the rush decades earlier. [10]
2. **Follow a worker's ordinary problem:** Brown's store had to keep functioning amid insecurity. A commodity town required more than someone washing gravel. [6]
3. **Read the transport record:** compare a passenger/mail service with mining itself. Preserve the dates of Shearer's and Riggins/Lytle's work, not one timeless pioneer scene. [7]
4. **Future managed-site interpretation only:** if conditions allow, read the public panels without leaving approved visitor space to locate the photographed building or a claim. The cemetery remains optional reflection, never an achievement checkpoint.
5. **Strongest ending:** hold the rush story beside the Tribe's account of its cost. Ask who could leave an exhausted camp and who remained with the consequences. This is an interpretive question, not an invented answer from a named resident. [8]

Return by the lawful public access used to arrive. No exit through diggings, private land or an assumed historic connection to Elk City.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Discovery is consistently placed in **August 1861**, but exact days differ: ISHS gives August 19–20 while an Idaho Geological Survey presentation gives August 12. Use month/year pending primary reconciliation. [5,11]
- *Lure of Gold* places New Florence about half a mile south; *Florence Tells Her Secrets* says a quarter mile. These approximations are not usable entrance coordinates. [2,6]
- The university's survey-letter call number **MG 303** belongs to Spalding; the following **MG 5039** belongs to Summit Flat Mining District No. 2. Do not shift identifiers by one catalog entry. [9]
- The 1901 letter is catalog-level evidence only. The transport newspaper citations are also uncollated leads, not first-person testimony already retrieved. [7,9]
- Gold-output and population estimates vary in scope and period. This dossier does not pick a dramatic number as a census or audited return.
- Year-round facility use does not resolve winter road access; the June 2026 closure is expired on its own terms. [1,4]
- Florence, Idaho County, is not Florence in Oregon, Colorado, Wisconsin or a person's name in an oral-history catalog. Elk City is a different camp.
