## 2. The story

Agricultural education was not only a matter of telling people how to grow a crop. It also meant deciding what evidence should count when explaining why one farm prospered and another did not.

A named student voice is **Willard W. Cochrane**, author of the **1938** master's study *A study of the organization, operations, and practices of a selected group of successful Montana farms*. His institutional repository's indexed abstract says he selected farms using an income threshold of $1,000 and acknowledged that the selection method left some successful farmers outside the sample. It reports 314 farms and concludes there was **“no set formula for successful farming in Montana.”** This nine-word quotation is from the indexed primary abstract, not a recording. The full PDF could not be opened in this pass, so no page-level analysis is claimed. [4]

MSU's departmental thesis list independently records **June 1, 1938**, Cochrane's title and **R. R. Renne** as chair. Nearby it records **Harold Halcrow's** study of unsuccessful dry farms. Their juxtaposition is documentary evidence of questions being studied, not proof of what either student personally believed about every farmer. [5]

The institutional work also reached beyond campus. MSU Library identifies **Milburn Lincoln Wilson** as an assistant state agronomist in Bozeman, then county agent, Extension leader and agricultural economist. Its photographic collection describes both attempted improvements and methods imported from wetter regions that were poorly suited to Montana. [6] That description complicates a simple story of experts delivering universally successful answers.

The visitor's question is therefore deliberately narrower than a celebration of progress: **what did a school learn to measure, and what did its categories leave out?** This is an interpretation drawn from the sources, not a claim that either a thesis or a reconstructed farm represents all rural experience. Historical classifications are not present-day farming advice.

## 3. Then / now

Then, students and Extension personnel studied working agriculture through records, observations and demonstrations. Now, those records can be read alongside a public exhibit about the institution's development. The museum is the viewing anchor; it is not the original location of all the work described.

The Tinsley House makes the distinction unusually clear. The museum says it acquired the house in **1986**, moved it from **Willow Creek**, restored it, and dedicated it on **November 8, 1989**. Associated farm structures include recreations. [3] Its current setting is educational interpretation, not an unchanged original homestead. This dossier supplies no address or approach to the original property.

An archival campus view is a contextual comparison only. No current photograph has been aligned with it, and no historic exposure point has been established as publicly accessible today.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

1. **Prepare with the research question.** Read the short indexed thesis abstract and distinguish the author's findings from the limits of the sample. Do not present it as a complete transcription of the thesis.
2. **Begin inside the admitted museum.** Use Paugh History Hall as the public historical anchor. Find the agricultural-school institutional story if it remains displayed; if an exhibit has changed, do not improvise a campus-building tour.
3. **Compare two kinds of teaching.** Put document-based research beside the museum's published description of its seasonal farm. In November, make that comparison on the page, not by approaching a closed exhibit.
4. **End with the strongest story moment:** return to the word “successful.” Imagine how different a history looks depending on whom the researcher includes. The intellectual turn is from admiring an old institution to noticing how it created knowledge.

**Onsite question:** When a display describes educational progress, does it also make the evidence and its limits visible? This is a question for the visitor's reflection, not a request already sent to museum staff.

Keep this as a single indoor stop with optional source reading. If museum access is unavailable, the reading-only version remains useful; a closed door does not justify entering another campus facility.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The visit page's exception entries carry an “all day” calendar label even when their text says 9–1. The explicit early-closing hours are the operative planning detail. Older calendar results showed 3 p.m. for holiday eves; the current visit page says **1 p.m.**, so use the earlier closing rather than assume the stale later one. [1]

The farm page distinguishes acquisition, relocation and dedication but does not give a separately explicit move date in the read text. Do not collapse all of those events into 1989. Its season label is more precise than a generic May–September summary. [3]

The named primary voice is limited to the repository's indexed abstract: repeated direct PDF attempts failed. The departmental list corroborates identity/date/title, not the thesis's numerical analysis. Full scan review remains a research gate before expanding or reusing its conclusions. [4][5]

No recorded interview was heard. No firsthand farmer testimony is invented to fill that gap. This entry is a bounded history of agricultural education, not a complete account of regional settlement or Indigenous land relations. No Tribal perspective is attributed without a Tribal-authored source.
