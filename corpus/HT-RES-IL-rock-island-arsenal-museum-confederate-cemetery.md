# Rock Island Arsenal Museum & Confederate Cemetery — the prison barracks' last vestige on an active arsenal island
HT-RES-IL-rock-island-arsenal-museum-confederate-cemetery · Illinois · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The island came first, and the Army's own history page starts the federal story early: the island's importance was identified as early as 1809, when it was set aside as a federal military reservation by Act of Congress. In 1816 Fort Armstrong was built as part of a system of Upper Mississippi Valley forts; its most important peacekeeping role, on the Army page, was serving as military headquarters during the Black Hawk War of 1832. The fort was abandoned in 1836 and remained an ordnance depot until 1845; what stands today is a 1916 replica of one of its blockhouses, built for the fort's centennial. The island's civilian thread runs through George Davenport, the fort's sutler, who resigned in 1818 to trade, built his 1833 mansion overlooking the Mississippi, served as militia quartermaster in the Black Hawk War, and was murdered in that home on July 4, 1845 by members of the "Banditti of the Prairie." In 1856 the first railroad bridge across the Mississippi was completed here; fifteen days after opening, the steamboat *Effie Afton* struck it and burned, and the resulting litigation — Abraham Lincoln defending the railroad, a hung jury, and an eventual Supreme Court decision in the railroad's favor in December 1862 — is part of this island's record, with a monument marking the bridge location today. The Army history in this run also records what the museum itself states as its starting frame: the Sauk used Rock Island as a summer camp site in the pre-arsenal years. That Indigenous use is the island's first documented layer in these sources and is carried here as the Army states it, without elaboration beyond the record retrieved. [1, 2]

An Act of Congress established Rock Island Arsenal on July 11, 1862, re-designating the abandoned fort and the whole island as an arsenal and national armory, and ground was broken for the first building on September 1, 1863. The Army's six-part history gives the siting rationale in Brig. Gen. George D. Ramsey's 1864 words to Secretary of War Edwin Stanton: secure from an enemy advancing by lakes or river, able to ship supplies in any direction at any season, set in country teeming with coal and wood, elevated above floods, and near enough to Rock Island, Davenport and Moline for its workforce. Major Charles P. Kingsbury, the first commanding officer, designed three buildings; only the Clock Tower Building, completed in 1867, was finished to his plan, and it houses the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Rock Island District today. Brevet Brig. Gen. Thomas J. Rodman, commanding 1865–1871 and remembered as the "Father of Rock Island Arsenal," redesigned the arsenal on a grander scale: ten stone shops at the island's center — five south-row "arsenal row" buildings for general ordnance, five north-row "armory row" buildings for small arms — built out under Lt. Col. Daniel W. Flagler from 1871 to 1886 after Rodman's death, and Quarters One, an Italianate villa completed in 1871 with 20,000 square feet in more than 50 rooms, described on the Army page as the second-largest single-family government residence next to the White House. The arsenal was placed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1969 (NRIS 69000057, listed September 30, 1969) and its original buildings were designated a National Historic Landmark District — the Army page dates the designation 1989 while the Register record in wider circulation dates it June 7, 1988; the conflict is printed in Gaps and neither year is asserted. [1, 2]

The prison is the reason this dossier pairs the museum with a cemetery. From 1863 to 1865 the arsenal held two Civil War roles at once: the shipping and storage supply center Congress mandated, and a prisoner-of-war camp it was never intended to be. The island was chosen because it was far from the fighting, government-owned, sparsely occupied and secure. In August 1863 Capt. Charles A. Reynolds, under Quartermaster General Montgomery C. Meigs, began surveying and building a camp for 10,080 prisoners — 84 barracks, each 100 by 22 by 12 feet, inside a stockade 1,300 by 900 feet and 12 feet high, on the National Park Service's Civil War series account. Union victories and overcrowding elsewhere forced the Rock Island Prison Barracks to open before it was ready. The first 468 prisoners, captured at Lookout Mountain and Chattanooga the month before, arrived in December 1863; by the end of that month nearly 5,600 were held. They arrived into an unfinished camp in one of the worst western Illinois winters on record, short of blankets, clothing and stove fuel, many already sick. The Army history states plainly that conditions were "primitive at best" at the start, and that they generally improved over the following months as medical attention, food and clothing caught up — prisoners received the same rations as their guards at first, made and sold trinkets, earned scrip building the camp reservoir and sewer system, read newspapers, and corresponded with home. Disease was the constant enemy on both sides of the stockade: by early 1864 nearly 700 prisoners and guards had died in a smallpox outbreak, answered by Col. Adolphus Johnson with quarantine, a hospital and a pest house, after which the epidemic subsided within months. The VA's cemetery history adds the ground conditions the Army narrative passes over: the prison sat in a low-lying area with poor drainage, which contributed to the unhealthy living conditions, and smallpox and pneumonia accounted for most of the deaths. In total, on the Army's figures, 12,192 Confederate prisoners were held at Rock Island between December 1863 and July 1865; 5,581 volunteered to join the Union as galvanized Yankees; and 1,964 died — the VA history gives interments at the Confederate Cemetery as approximately 1,950 when burials were discontinued on July 11, 1865, a small numerical variance printed in Gaps. The NPS series account gives 1,960 prisoner deaths and 171 Union guard deaths for the camp's twenty months. Each Confederate grave marker, on the Army page, identifies the individual soldier, his company and his unit. [1, 2, 3, 7]

The camp's reputation needs the same care as its death toll, and the Army history itself does that work. Rock Island was called the "Andersonville of the North" as early as January 1864 in circulating criticism, on the VA account, and the Army history attributes much of the camp's later notoriety to J. B. Danforth Jr., editor of the *Rock Island Argus*, whose antiwar campaign published what the Army calls undocumented stories of deliberate murder by starvation and disease, and to the postwar Lost Cause retelling — capped, in 1936, by *Gone with the Wind*, which sent Ashley Wilkes to Rock Island and claimed three-fourths of the men sent there never came out alive. The Army history states directly that the historical records do not support that figure: 1,964 dead of 12,192 held is about sixteen percent — a grievous toll by disease in an unfinished camp in a hard winter, and a different fact from the novel's. The dossier prints both the documented toll and the reputation, labeled as reputation, because visitors will arrive carrying the novel's version and the ground itself — 1,964 named graves — is the correction. Andersonville's own documented rate, on the same Army account, was 27 percent; comparisons are left there, unscored, as the sources leave them. [2, 3, 7]

After the camp closed in July 1865 the barracks were torn down and arsenal construction resumed. The VA history states the ending exactly: the federal government transferred all extant prison structures to the Arsenal, all buildings associated with the prison have since been destroyed, and Rock Island Confederate Cemetery is the last vestige of the Rock Island Confederate prison. The Army history adds the one other survivor: a historical plaque at Blunt Drive and East Street. Two cemeteries came out of the prison years — the Confederate Cemetery on Rodman Avenue, overseen by Rock Island National Cemetery, and the guard cemetery that became Rock Island National Cemetery itself, established in 1863 as the post cemetery for Union prison guards, among the twenty oldest national cemeteries, covering 70 acres on the Army page (66.8 acres in the Army history article — variance in Gaps) with approximately 23,000 to 24,000 markers representing 29,000 burials. A granite obelisk dedicated November 2, 2003 by a local chapter of the United Daughters of the Confederacy stands at the Confederate Cemetery's entrance, and four 12-pounder Confederate artillery monuments stand within it, on the VA page. The museum that interprets all of this is itself an artifact: opened July 4, 1905 as the Ordnance Museum, the Army's second-oldest museum after West Point, begun from fifteen boxes of ordnance materiel sent in 1903 by Chief of Ordnance William Crozier; closed and stored for both World Wars; reopened in May 1948 in its present home, Building 60; renamed for John M. Browning in 1959 and back to Rock Island Arsenal Museum on July 1, 1986; and reopened June 29, 2023 after a renovation the Army valued at $2.5 million (a contemporary auction-house account says $2 million — variance in Gaps), refocused on the Arsenal's people, processes and products, with more than 250 artifacts and hundreds of photographs on display from a collection of more than 7,000, including the M1903 Springfield rifle serial number 1 and lumber from the original Fort Armstrong. Its core exhibits now run from 1805 to the present and include the story of the 108th Infantry Regiment, U.S. Colored Troops, the African American regiment tasked with guarding the prison camp. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** Arsenal Island — originally Rock Island — is a 946-acre island in the Mississippi River between Davenport, Iowa, and Rock Island and Moline, Illinois, lying within Illinois. The prison barracks stood on the island's north-central ground in 1863–65; nothing of them survives above ground. The Confederate Cemetery stands on Rodman Avenue, about 2,500 yards northwest of Rock Island National Cemetery on the VA page's measure, and the museum stands in Building 60 at 3500 North Avenue. All of it is inside an active military installation, which is the controlling fact of the visit. [1, 3, 4]

**Lawful physical anchors.** (1) Rock Island Arsenal Museum, Building 60, 3500 North Ave., Rock Island, IL 61299 — staffed museum, free, Tuesday–Saturday 10–4, closed federal holidays and the day after Thanksgiving. (2) Rock Island Confederate Cemetery, Rodman Ave., Rock Island, IL 61299 — VA-administered burial ground, closed to new interments, administered through Rock Island National Cemetery (309-784-0000 on the VA Confederate Cemetery page; a second VA-derived listing gives 309-782-2094 — variance in Gaps). Both anchors are reached only through the Moline Gate visitor-pass process in the gate: enter via the Moline Gate off River Drive in Moline, obtain the pass at the Visitor Welcome Center at 23 Prospect Drive, and proceed directly to the museum and cemetery. No part of the Arsenal beyond the public museum, cemeteries and marked public routes is part of this visit; photography rules, vehicle search and all gate instructions govern, and privately owned weapons are not allowed on the installation. [3, 4, 8]

## 5. Public progression

Do the paperwork before the history: arrive at the Moline Gate Visitor Welcome Center at 23 Prospect Drive in visitor-center hours with a REAL ID (or two government IDs), take the background check, and get the pass before you think about the museum's 4 p.m. close — the gate, not the museum, sets this visit's real schedule. Drive the public route to Building 60 and take the museum in its own order, 1805 to the present. Linger at the prison-camp exhibits and at the 108th Infantry Regiment gallery: the men who guarded the barracks were themselves part of the war's transformation, African American soldiers in federal uniform guarding Confederate prisoners on an island in the Mississippi, and the renovated museum puts that story where it belongs, inside the camp's story rather than beside it. Note the Fort Armstrong lumber and the Springfield serial number 1 for what they are — the museum's habit of keeping the island's own fabric. Then drive Rodman Avenue to the Confederate Cemetery and slow all the way down. Walk the rows and read the markers as the Army page says they were cut: a name, a company, a unit, repeated 1,964 times. **Strongest story moment · The only tangible remains.** Of an 84-barracks prison that held 12,192 men, everything built is gone — barracks, hospital, stockade — and what remains is the burial ground and the names in it, kept by the same federal establishment that ran the camp. The museum two miles of island away now spends its renovated galleries on people, processes and products; the cemetery is the process's cost, in rows. Read some names aloud or not at all, keep the novel's version and the record's version separate as this dossier does, and leave by the Moline Gate in pass hours. There is no version of this visit that begins without the pass, and no lawful presence on the island outside the pass's terms. [1, 2, 3, 4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Landmark-designation year conflict, printed in full: the Army's installation history page states the original arsenal buildings were designated a National Historic Landmark in 1989. The Register record in wider circulation gives the National Historic Landmark District designation as June 7, 1988. The dossier asserts neither year; the NR listing itself (September 30, 1969, NRIS 69000057) is not in dispute in the sources checked this run. [1]
- Death-toll variance across official sources, printed in full and not merged: the Army installation page and Army history article give 1,964 prisoner deaths. The NPS Civil War series account gives 1,960 prisoners died and 171 Union guards died in the camp's twenty months. The VA cemetery history gives approximately 1,950 interments when burials in the Confederate Cemetery were discontinued July 11, 1865. Deaths and interments are not the same count, and the four-man spread between 1,960 and 1,964 is not explained in any source checked this run; the Story carries the Army's 1,964 as the operator's figure with this variance standing. [1, 2, 3, 7]
- National Cemetery acreage variance: 70 acres on the Army installation page; 66.8 acres in the Army history article. Both printed. [1, 2]
- Renovation-cost variance: $2.5 million in the museum's reference record and Army reopening coverage framing; $2 million in a contemporary (June 2023) auction-house account of the reopening, which also calls it the museum's first facelift in 80 years and a three-year closure (the Army says closed nearly three years, having shut a year early for COVID ahead of a planned March 2021 renovation closure). The dossier carries $2.5 million as the Army figure with this variance standing. [5, 6]
- Visitor-center hours conflict: current visitor/trail listings give the Moline Gate Visitor Welcome Center registration hours as 7 a.m.–3 p.m. daily; a MilitaryINSTALLATIONS listing gives Moline Gate Visitor Center hours Monday–Friday 6:00 a.m.–3:30 p.m. and Saturday–Sunday 7:30 a.m.–3:30 p.m. The gate uses the narrower 7–3 daily window; arrive inside it. [8]
- Cemetery grounds hours — blank, not invented: the VA Confederate Cemetery page states office hours (Monday–Friday 7:30–4:00, closed federal holidays except Memorial Day and Veterans Day) for the administering national cemetery, but states no public visiting hours for the cemetery grounds themselves. A VA-derived places listing gives Monday–Friday 7:30–4:00 for the cemetery, which appears to repeat the office hours. The dossier prints the blank and routes the cemetery visit inside the museum trip and daylight; it does not publish grounds hours it could not verify on the VA page. [3]

The museum's own general-inquiries number is 309-782-5021. The dossier prints the VA page's number in the anchor and the variance here. [3, 4]
- Museum address form: the museum's pages give both "1 Rock Island Arsenal, Bldg. 60 – 3500 North Ave., Rock Island, IL 61299" and "3500 North Avenue" — same building, two street-name spellings (Ave. / Avenue) across the operator's own pages. Not a substantive conflict; both forms printed in the gate as North Ave. [4, 5]
- "Andersonville of the North" — filed as reputation, not record: the phrase appears in January 1864 criticism (VA), in Danforth's antiwar campaign as characterized by the Army, and in *Gone with the Wind* (1936), whose three-fourths death claim the Army history states the records do not support (documented: 1,964 of 12,192, about 16%). The dossier keeps the documented conditions — unfinished camp, severe winter, smallpox and pneumonia, poor drainage — and the reputation in separate sentences throughout, and scores neither against Andersonville beyond the Army's own stated 27% figure for that camp. [2, 3]
- Indigenous island use: the Army-derived record states the Sauk used Rock Island as a summer camp site. No tribal source, date range or village record for the island was retrieved this run; the statement is carried exactly at that weight and no more is built on it. For the Sauk city ground in this same city, see the Black Hawk State Historic Site / Saukenuk dossier filed this run.
