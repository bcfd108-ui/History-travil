# HT-RES-NY-niagara-reservation — The falls fenced off, bought back, and wired for power
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A strip of riverbank, a wooded island, and the brink of the American Falls — ground New York bought in 1885 so the falls could be seen freely, on the same river whose falling water, a decade later, lit a city twenty miles away.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a state park wrapped around a waterfall: Niagara Falls State Park, the present name of the Niagara Reservation, in the city of Niagara Falls, Niagara County, New York. The ground is the American side of Niagara Falls — the eastern shore of the Niagara River centered on the American Falls, the islands above and between the falls including Goat Island, and the viewpoints at Prospect Point and Terrapin Point. The operator is the New York State Office of Parks, Recreation and Historic Preservation. The State Parks page for the park states the terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: the park is open year round, with attractions open seasonally; the park charges a vehicle use fee but "is always free to just walk in"; fees are collected in Parking Lot 1 and Parking Lot 2 at $10.00 daily for most of the year, and $15.00 Friday–Sunday from May 27 to October 9 (the day before Columbus Day), with the Empire Pass offered at $80. The park's address, as the State Parks page carries it, is 15 Goat Island Loop Road, Niagara Falls, NY 14303. State Parks calls it America's oldest state park. The Niagara Reservation was designated a National Historic Landmark on May 23, 1963, and added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000555, per the National Park Service's National Register record). The power ground is part of the same visit's story but not the same fence line: the surviving Adams Power Plant Transformer House stands off 15th Street near Buffalo Avenue, outside the park boundary, and the New York Power Authority's Niagara Power Project stands about 4.5 miles downstream in Lewiston — both are treated in this entry as the reservation's second story, not as park attractions.

## 2. The story
Before it was a park, the American shore at Niagara was a gauntlet. The National Register nomination for the Niagara Reservation records that as early as 1834 it was suggested the land near the falls be cleared of the factories, hotels, and signs so the falls might be viewed in a natural setting — and that for many years the idea received no support, while mills, fences, and paid viewpoints crowded the brink. By the 1870s the complaints had names attached: the nomination lists landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted, novelist Henry James, editor Charles Eliot Norton of *The Nation*, and painter Frederic Edwin Church among the prominent citizens who joined them. In 1879, at the New York legislature's direction, Olmsted and James T. Gardiner, head of the state survey, reported on the falls' condition; their report recommended the state purchase the lands around the falls so visitors could view them in a natural setting.

The campaign — remembered as the Free Niagara movement — took two legislative steps, and the dates matter because later summaries compress them. On **April 30, 1883**, Governor Grover Cleveland signed the act authorizing the selection, location, and appropriation of lands at Niagara Falls for a state reservation, and the first commissioners of the State Reservation at Niagara were appointed; the State Parks blog records their first meeting on June 9, 1883. In 1885 the legislature passed the Niagara Appropriations Bill, funding the purchase — the State Parks blog puts the authorized purchase at 412 acres, including Goat Island. On **July 15, 1885**, the State Reservation at Niagara opened to the public. Thomas Vincent Welch, the assemblyman who had pushed the legislation, became the park's first superintendent and served eighteen years. The nomination states the precedent in one sentence: in creating the reservation, New York became the first state to use its power of eminent domain to acquire land for aesthetic purposes.

What the state bought, it then un-built. Following the guidelines of the 1879 Olmsted and Gardiner report, the commissioners kept construction to what was necessary to preserve the natural scene and let visitors appreciate it. In 1887, the nomination records, Olmsted and his associate Calvert Vaux implemented the plan: the eastern shore and the islands were cleared, and the shore land's eastern boundary was planted to screen out the city. An administration building went up on the shore east of Prospect Point in 1900 and is still in use, per the nomination. The nomination also names the reservation's greatest intrusion: the Robert Moses Parkway, completed in 1960, a four-lane divided highway run through the middle of the reservation along the shore. The reservation was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1963 and entered on the National Register on October 15, 1966.

The river's second story is power, and it begins upstream of the brink. The nomination for the Adams Power Plant Transformer House records that in 1886 Thomas Evershed, the New York State Engineer, produced a plan for a hydraulic canal to use Niagara's waters to make electricity. Investors incorporated the Cataract Construction Company in 1889 to build it. Construction of the intake canal and a 7,500-foot brick-lined tunnel under the city, to return water to the gorge below the falls, began in 1890; work on the power plant itself waited until 1895 while an international advisory board's revisions were folded into the scheme. The architects were McKim, Mead and White, charged — in a specification that belongs beside the reservation's own founding logic — with designing a plant that was aesthetically pleasing because of public interest in conserving the scenic beauty of Niagara Falls.

Power House No. 1 and the northeast half of the Transformer House were completed in 1895, and the nomination dates the first delivery of power to **August 26, 1895**, to the Pittsburgh Reduction Company (later Alcoa) in Niagara Falls. The plant was a large-scale alternating-current central station — the technology of Nikola Tesla's patents, built by Westinghouse — and the nomination's significance statement, quoting Cornell engineering professor Paul M. Lincoln, calls it a plant that "marks an epoch in electricity." On **November 15, 1896**, per the nomination, the Niagara station began long-distance transmission to Buffalo, twenty-two miles away. For several years the Adams plant was the largest facility of its kind in the world. It was relegated to standby duty in the early 1920s, closed in 1961, and its two power houses were demolished in 1965; the limestone Transformer House is the only surviving structure of the plant.

The Schoellkopf stations at the gorge, fed by the older hydraulic canal, carried Niagara power into the twentieth century — until June 7, 1956, when the gorge wall behind the Schoellkopf Power Station gave way and two-thirds of the plant fell into the river. Congress answered with the Niagara Redevelopment Act of 1957, licensing the New York Power Authority to redevelop the river's power. NYPA's Niagara Power Project — intakes above the falls, conduits under the city, and the Robert Moses Niagara Power Plant in Lewiston, about 4.5 miles downstream — produced its first power in 1961, when, as NYPA states, it was the largest hydropower facility in the Western world. The power story and the park story are the same bargain struck twice: the falls kept as scenery in 1885, and the river above the brink tapped — under license, with the flow over the falls itself regulated — to light the region.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a shore crowded with factories, hotels, fences, and signs, the falls themselves approached through private ground and paid viewpoints, as the nomination describes the pre-1885 shore. **Now:** the reservation's strip of shore and islands kept as open parkland — Prospect Point, Goat Island, the Three Sisters Islands — screened by the planting Olmsted and Vaux set out in 1887, with the city held at the park's eastern edge.

**Then:** a color photochrom of about 1898 (see §7) shows the falls from Prospect Point thirteen years after the reservation opened — visitors massed on the point, the American Falls in the foreground, the Horseshoe in mist beyond, and wooded banks where the mills had stood. **Now:** the same point is the park's signature overlook, reached free on foot, in a park State Parks describes as open year round.

**Then:** a limestone transformer house flanked by two power houses, taking the river's water through a tunnel under the city and sending alternating current to Buffalo. **Now:** the power houses are gone — demolished in 1965 — and the Transformer House stands alone off 15th Street, the one built survivor of the first great Niagara power station, while generation has moved downstream to NYPA's Lewiston plant.

## 4. What's still there
- The reservation ground itself: the eastern-shore strip centered on the American Falls, Prospect Point, Goat Island, Green Island, and the Three Sisters Islands, within Niagara Falls State Park (nomination description)
- The American Falls, Bridal Veil Falls, and the Horseshoe Falls as viewed from the American side — Prospect Point and Terrapin Point are the park's brink viewpoints
- The 1900 administration building on the shore east of Prospect Point, still in use per the nomination
- The Adams Power Plant Transformer House (McKim, Mead and White; locally quarried limestone; about 40 by 180 feet per its nomination), off 15th Street near Buffalo Avenue — the only surviving structure of the Edward Dean Adams Power Plant, listed on the National Register in 1975 (reference no. 75001212)
- The Schoellkopf Power Station site at the gorge below the falls, within the park's downstream grounds, where the Maid of the Mist landing now operates at the base of the Observation Tower elevator
- NYPA's Niagara Power Project in Lewiston — the Robert Moses Niagara Power Plant and the Niagara Power Vista visitor center — about 4.5 miles downstream, per NYPA

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At Prospect Point:** Begin where the 1898 photochrom was made (§7) — the American Falls at the foreground, the Horseshoe in the distance — on ground that was a paid viewpoint before 1885 and has been free since.
2. **Across to Goat Island:** Take the reservation's wooded center, the island the 1885 purchase included, and walk its perimeter to Terrapin Point at the Horseshoe's brink.
3. **To the Three Sisters Islands:** The reservation's quiet upstream edge, where the river is still rapids and the 1887 planting logic — scenery first, structures only as needed — is easiest to read.
4. **Down to the gorge:** Ride or walk to the river level at the Schoellkopf site and the Maid of the Mist landing, where the gorge-wall power stations stood and where the 1956 collapse is written in the cleared ground.
5. **Off 15th Street, at the Transformer House:** End at the power story's survivor — the limestone transformer house, read from the street — then, if the trip extends downstream, NYPA's Power Vista in Lewiston for the 1961 plant that replaced them all.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The reservation's acreage is carried three ways by the sources, and all three print. The State Parks blog states the 1885 appropriations authorized the purchase of 412 acres, including Goat Island. The National Register nomination states the initial reservation was 418 acres and "has increased to 435 acres," and its geographical-data section gives the acreage of the nominated property as 435 acres. The difference between the 412-acre purchase figure and the nomination's 418-acre initial figure is not explained in the sources consulted; this draft prints the appropriation figure as an authorization and the nomination figures as the landmark's own record, and asserts no single "original acreage."

The founding date is framed two ways, because two different acts carry it. The authorizing act was signed April 30, 1883; the reservation was funded in 1885 and opened to the public July 15, 1885. The National Register record's significant year is 1885. Summaries that date the park simply "1883" or simply "1885" are each holding half the record; both halves print here.

The Adams plant's first power is split by one day. Its National Register nomination dates the first delivery of power to August 26, 1895 (to the Pittsburgh Reduction Company). A local history of Niagara power states the plant went into operation on August 25, 1895. The two may describe different moments — operation versus first delivery — but the sources do not say so, and the one-day spread is printed unresolved.

The Niagara Power Project's first power is also carried two ways. NYPA's own current materials state the project "produced its first power in 1961," and its 2026 anniversary release celebrates the 1961 first power without a day date. Contemporary and trade accounts split the day: first power on a trial basis on January 28, 1961, and the formal first-power ceremony — Governor Rockefeller closing a switch, with President Kennedy's recorded message — on February 10, 1961. Both dates print, labeled as they are carried.

The National Register record for the reservation carries no latitude or longitude, and the nomination's location is marked "NOT FOR PUBLICATION"; the nomination's handwritten UTM references are not legible in the filed copy consulted. The locator below therefore prints no coordinates — none are taken from lead-list sources.

Attraction-level prices inside the park (boat, trolley, Cave of the Winds) are not printed: the State Parks page consulted carries the park's vehicle fees and walk-in terms only, and no attraction fee is asserted from any other source.
