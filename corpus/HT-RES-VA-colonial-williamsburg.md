# HT-RES-VA-colonial-williamsburg — A capital that lost its job, kept its streets, and was rebuilt on its own foundations
Division VA seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Eighty-one years as Virginia's capital, a century and a half as a quiet college town, and then the largest restoration ever attempted in America: a whole 18th-century city brought back on its own street plan, with 88 original buildings still standing in the middle of it.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a restored city, not a single building: Colonial Williamsburg, the 301-acre Historic Area in Williamsburg, Virginia, operated by the Colonial Williamsburg Foundation, a private not-for-profit educational foundation. The Foundation's own tickets page states the terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: a Single-day Ticket is $37.50 for adults (13+) and $10.00 for youth (6–12); a Multiday Ticket, covering three days, is $55.00 for adults and $28.00 for youth; an Annual Pass is $75.00 for adults and $35.00 for youth. The same page states that service fees and taxes apply in the cart, and its notice reads: a 3% service fee applies to credit card payments (avoidable by paying on site with cash or check), and the City of Williamsburg charges a 10% admission tax on the first $10 of any admission product. The Foundation's Know Before You Go page states what the ticket is for: admission tickets are required to enter buildings and experience programming in the Historic Area, including interpreter-guided tours of the Capitol, the Governor's Palace, and the Courthouse, the working trade shops, and the Art Museums of Colonial Williamsburg; a building flying the Grand Union Flag is open. That page states the operating hours it lists: the Art Museums of Colonial Williamsburg are open 10:00AM–5:00PM (ticketing available 12:00–4:00PM), and the Lumber House Ticket Office and Visitor Center are open 9:30AM–5:00PM. The Historic Area corresponds to the Williamsburg Historic District, listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000925, per the NPS's own National Register record), and designated a National Historic Landmark District.

## 2. The story
Williamsburg began as Middle Plantation, an English settlement planted in the 1630s on the high ground between the James and York rivers, inland from Jamestown. In October 1693 the Virginia General Assembly designated Middle Plantation as the site for the College of William and Mary. Then, in October 1698, the statehouse at Jamestown burned — not for the first time — and the colony finally moved its government. In 1699 the Middle Plantation became the capital of Virginia, renamed Williamsburg in honor of King William III.

It was, from the start, a planned city. The City of Williamsburg's own history records that Williamsburg was one of America's first planned cities, laid out in 1699 under the supervision of Governor Francis Nicholson. The 1699 act of the General Assembly divided the city into half-acre lots, set houses back six feet from the main street — Duke of Gloucester Street — and called for a brick capitol building, the first in the American colonies, together with a brick prison. The Capitol was built between 1701 and 1705 at the east end of Duke of Gloucester Street; the Governor's Palace rose at the north end of Palace Green, ordered built in 1705 in the Library of Congress's caption for its 1943 photograph of the building, and constructed, in the Society of Architectural Historians' Archipedia account, in 1706–1709 under builder Henry Cary, then enriched by Lieutenant Governor Alexander Spotswood from about 1710 to 1720. Seven royal governors and two governors of the Commonwealth — Patrick Henry and Thomas Jefferson — occupied it.

For 81 years Williamsburg was the seat of Virginia's government and, in the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation's summary, the social and cultural center of the colony — the place where the General Assembly met, where the colony's courts sat, and where, in the 1760s and 1770s, the argument with Britain was conducted in speeches, resolves, and conventions before it was conducted in arms. In 1780, with the Revolution under way and Richmond judged safer from British attack, the capital moved up the James River to Richmond, where it remains.

Williamsburg then did the thing that saved it: almost nothing. The City's history puts it plainly — Williamsburg reverted to a quiet college town and rural county seat, and in retrospect its loss of capital status was its salvation, because so many 18th-century buildings survived into the early 20th century simply because nobody needed the ground badly enough to replace them. The colonial street plan survived with them.

The second founding began in 1926. The Reverend Dr. W. A. R. Goodwin, rector of Bruton Parish Church — the parish church of the colonial capital, itself an original building — had already restored his own church and dreamed of restoring the whole town. He had approached Henry Ford without success. In 1926, in the Colonial Williamsburg Foundation's own brief history, Goodwin approached John D. Rockefeller, Jr., about restoring the small town to its colonial past, and Rockefeller took it on: first buying property quietly, through Goodwin, then financing the restoration until his death in 1960. The reconstructed Raleigh Tavern opened in September 1932 as the first public exhibition building. The Capitol and the Governor's Palace — both long gone, the Palace burned in 1781 while serving as a hospital for American soldiers — were rebuilt on their excavated foundations; the Palace reconstruction, designed by Perry, Shaw and Hepburn, was built in 1931–1934, in the Archipedia account, working from the archaeological footprint, Thomas Jefferson's measured plans of 1779–1781, the so-called Frenchman's Map, and a 1730s Bodleian copperplate view of the building. Hundreds of later buildings were removed, 88 original 18th-century buildings were restored where they stood, and several hundred more were reconstructed, most on their original foundations.

The restoration's first decades told a partial story. The Foundation's own institutional histories record that only in 1979 did Colonial Williamsburg begin to incorporate African American history and culture into its research, programs, and site interpretation — this in a city where, in the 18th century, roughly half the population had been enslaved Black Virginians. Since 1979, in the Foundation's words, it has interpreted those complicated stories through African American programs supported by scholarship. The town the visitor walks today is therefore two artifacts at once: an 18th-century capital, and a 20th-century act of memory that is still being corrected.

The Williamsburg Historic District was designated a National Historic Landmark District and was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 — the Register record carrying both the colonial period and the restoration period (1925–1949) in its periods of significance, an official admission that the rebuilding is itself part of the history.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a planned capital laid out in 1699 — half-acre lots on Duke of Gloucester Street, a brick Capitol at one end, the Governor's Palace closing Palace Green at the other, the College and Bruton Parish Church anchoring the west. **Now:** the same plan, kept as a 301-acre Historic Area in which, in the Foundation's own count, 88 original buildings stand among several hundred reconstructed ones, most on their original foundations.

**Then:** the Governor's Palace, built 1706–1709, home to seven royal governors and to Henry and Jefferson, burned in 1781 while a military hospital. **Now:** the Palace rebuilt in 1931–1934 on its excavated cellars, from Jefferson's plans, the Frenchman's Map, and a 1730s copperplate — a reconstruction that has now stood far longer than the original did.

**Then:** a capital abandoned in 1780, its population falling by about a third, its public buildings left to a college town. **Now:** the world's best-known living-history museum, where the abandonment is the reason there was anything left to restore.

**Then:** a restoration, begun in 1926, that presented the planter elite's city and left the enslaved half of its population largely uninterpreted. **Now:** a Foundation that dates its own correction to 1979 and interprets slavery and freedom as the capital's central tension — the change is part of the site's documented history, not a footnote to it.

## 4. What's still there
- The 1699 street plan itself: Duke of Gloucester Street, Palace Green, and the half-acre lot pattern laid out under Governor Francis Nicholson (City of Williamsburg history)
- 88 original 18th-century buildings, restored in place (Colonial Williamsburg Foundation brief history)
- The reconstructed Capitol on its original site at the east end of Duke of Gloucester Street — the first Capitol built 1701–1705, burned 1747, rebuilt, and reconstructed again in the restoration (Foundation and City accounts)
- The reconstructed Governor's Palace (1931–1934, Perry, Shaw and Hepburn) on Palace Green, on its excavated original foundations (SAH Archipedia)
- Bruton Parish Church, the colonial parish church whose rector, W. A. R. Goodwin, started the restoration
- The Raleigh Tavern reconstruction — the first public exhibition building of the restoration, opened September 1932 (Foundation brief history)
- The Art Museums of Colonial Williamsburg, included in admission (Foundation Know Before You Go page)
- Working trade shops and interpreter-guided sites across the Historic Area, marked open by the Grand Union Flag (Foundation Know Before You Go page)

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Visitor Center:** Start where the Foundation starts its visitors — tickets, the shuttle, and the day's schedule of open sites (Visitor Center ticketing 9:30AM–5:00PM, per the Know Before You Go page).
2. **On Palace Green:** The Governor's Palace — first as the 1706–1709 seat of royal government, then as the 1931–1934 reconstruction built from Jefferson's plans and a copperplate. Hold both dates at once; the building asks for it.
3. **Down Duke of Gloucester Street:** The mile of the 1699 plan — original houses and reconstructed shops on half-acre lots, the street Nicholson laid out and the restoration cleared back to the 18th century.
4. **At the Capitol:** The east end of the street, where the General Assembly sat from 1705 to 1780 — the room where the colony argued itself into a revolution.
5. **At Bruton Parish Church:** The original church at the center of the plan, and Goodwin's starting point — the one building from which the whole restoration grew.
6. **At the Art Museums:** The objects the buildings cannot hold — the Foundation's decorative arts and folk art collections, included in the same admission.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The restoration's start year is carried two ways, and both print. The Colonial Williamsburg Foundation's own brief history dates the beginning to **1926**, when Goodwin approached Rockefeller. The NPS National Register record for the Williamsburg Historic District (66000925) lists its significant years as 1699, **1779**, and **1927** — putting the restoration's significant year a year later than the Foundation's own account, and carrying 1779, not the capital's removal year 1780, for the colonial period's end. This draft prints the Foundation's 1926 for the restoration narrative, as the operator's own history states it, and prints the Register record's years as that record carries them; the one-year differences are not reconciled here.

The Governor's Palace building dates also vary by source, in smaller ways. The Library of Congress caption for the 1943 FSA/OWI photograph states the Palace "was ordered built in 1705." SAH Archipedia dates construction to 1706–1709, with Spotswood's enrichment running to about 1720, a ballroom-wing addition in 1751–1752, and the reconstruction to 1931–1934. The Foundation's Google Arts & Culture restoration account adds that the Palace burned in 1781 and that a lost 18th-century copperplate of its facade was discovered in 1929. The sources agree on the sequence — ordered, built, burned 1781, rebuilt 1931–1934 — and differ only in which year of a multi-year construction each chooses to headline; the full ranges are printed in §2 rather than a single picked year.

No source consulted for this entry gives a single authoritative count of the Historic Area's buildings that all the others match. The Foundation's brief history gives 88 original buildings and "hundreds" reconstructed; the City of Williamsburg's 2013 Comprehensive Plan counts over 620 structures contributing to the National Historic Landmark District across the Historic Area and its surrounds, of which 88 are original. The two counts measure different boundaries — the Foundation's Historic Area against the larger landmark district — and are printed as such, not averaged.

The NPS National Register record for the district carries no latitude or longitude, and no NRHP nomination, NPS asset record, or HABS record consulted for this entry carries a district point. The locator below therefore prints no coordinates; coordinates appearing in lead-list sources were not used.
