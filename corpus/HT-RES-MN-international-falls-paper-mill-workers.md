# International Falls — The paper machine and the people who built it

*History Travel research draft · Minnesota OP06 · Independent Dash review pending.*

## 2. The story

### Turn the falls into power

The county history dates the start of dam construction to 1905 and delivery of partial power to December 1909. In June 1910, four paper machines began producing newsprint. Power, wood and paper were successive stages of one operation. The mills had another use for wood waste when engineer Carl Muench tested a small Insulite fiberboard machine in 1914 and changed the equipment in 1915. The production of newsprint, experimental fiberboard and kraft pulp required different machinery at different dates. The Oberholtzer nomination quotes Backus looking back in 1930: “Everything that is there today came directly or indirectly through us.....through the payrolls.” This is a transcribed owner’s recollection, as reproduced in the nomination. [5–6]

### Two meanings of a new machine

In 1989 Boise Cascade’s expansion put those who worked inside the mill and those who wanted to build the new plant on different sides of a decision. Company spokesman Bob Anderson later called it a “bittersweet situation.” He recalled established mill workers expecting years of work ahead while Boise was contracting with a firm that brought construction workers from outside. Local union leader Larry Baron described the trade workers’ anger in the same Minnesota Public Radio account: “people have to take a stand.” Boise told the reporter it had doubted enough union labor would be available on schedule. Local building trades called the choice union-busting. Those are attributed positions about the employer’s decision. [7]

### The families outside the gate

Leif Enger interviewed two sisters married to incoming construction workers. They described growing cautious about what they said and where they went in town. In the same report Carol Dalton, a local travel agent, remembered how painful it was for people to keep shopping in businesses they believed had profited while failing to support them. The reporting recounts damage to worker housing on September 9. It also distinguishes the completed machine’s output and employment from local economic growth that residents had expected to follow. Read the voices together. A factory can keep making paper after the people who lived through its construction stop speaking to each other. [7]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- The county history dates paper output June 1910. Wisconsin Historical Society’s photograph description dates the Boise-Payette plant to 1913. The federal Oberholtzer Historic District nomination supports 1910 for the original paper mill and dates that company’s establishment to 1916. These are potentially different companies, structures or milestones. Original incorporation papers and equipment logs were unavailable. [5–6,8]
- The Oberholtzer nomination places dam completion in late 1908. The county history gives 1909 in a caption and 1910 in its narrative, with partial power in December 1909. The commissioning or construction records needed to resolve those dates were unavailable. [5–6]
- The 1997 MPR web transcript supplies named recollections and brief exact quotations. The linked legacy audio was not independently retrieved. The Minnesota Historical Society newspaper hub was located, but no period account in the International Falls Press was retrieved for quotation. Crowd and employment counts in the retrospective remain attributed and are not used as verified figures here. [7,9]
- Rainy Lake’s visitor partner lists the museum winter hours as Monday–Friday 9–5. The museum’s site confirms the address and visitor contact but does not substantiate that winter schedule in the content retrieved. The paper mill operator does not advertise a public visit in the sources consulted. [2–4]
- The state permit document is an intent to reissue, useful for identifying the current process and operator. The final signed permit number and status were not checked. [10]
