# HT-RES-NY-womens-rights-seneca-falls — Five days' notice, a brick chapel, and a declaration signed in pencil-light
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A plain brick chapel on Fall Street, built in 1843 by a reform-minded Methodist congregation, is where some 300 people gathered on July 19–20, 1848, debated a declaration modeled on the Declaration of Independence, and put 100 names to the proposition that all men and women are created equal — the First Women's Rights Convention, and the ground Women's Rights National Historical Park now keeps.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a small park in two villages: Women's Rights National Historical Park, preserving the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York — site of the First Women's Rights Convention of July 19–20, 1848 — together with the Elizabeth Cady Stanton House in Seneca Falls and the organizers' houses in neighboring Waterloo. The operator is the National Park Service. The NPS's own fees page states the terms, printed here as that page carries them: the park is free, and an entrance pass is **not required** to access Women's Rights National Historical Park; the same page notes the park does not distribute America the Beautiful Interagency Passes. The NPS directs visitors to begin at the Visitor Center at 136 Fall Street, Seneca Falls. The park's hours listing warns that building hours change by season; Declaration Park, at the same address, is carried as open dawn to dusk, and park grounds as open sunrise to sunset. No single daily schedule is printed here, because the park's own pages carry none that is stable (see §6). The Wesleyan Methodist Church — the chapel — was added to the National Register of Historic Places on August 29, 1980 (NRHP reference no. 80000361, per the NPS's own National Register record), months before Congress authorized the park on December 28, 1980.

## 2. The story
The convention's roots run to London in 1840, where Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, both in Britain for the World Anti-Slavery Convention, were refused seats on the convention floor as women and consigned to a gallery. The National Archives' account records that the two resolved then that a convention on women's rights would someday be held. It took eight years and a move: in May 1847 Stanton came to Seneca Falls with her husband and three sons, taking the house at 32 Washington Street that she called "Grassmere" and the "Center of the Rebellion." Her father, Daniel Cady, deeded the property to her that June — a married woman holding property in her own name. The NPS Stanton House page records that the family lived there from 1847 to 1862, growing to seven children, in a large farm house with outbuildings, orchards, and gardens on two acres her father also gave her money to put in order.

In July 1848 Mott was visiting relatives in Waterloo, the next village west. Five women — Stanton, Mott, Mott's sister Martha Coffin Wright, Mary Ann M'Clintock, and Jane Hunt — met over tea at the Hunt house in Waterloo and decided, in Stanton's later telling, to call a convention at once, while Mott was still in the area (the sources give the tea two different dates; see §6). The drafting was done at the M'Clintock house in Waterloo, where the Declaration of Sentiments was composed on the model of the Declaration of Independence — its famous sentence made by changing one line: "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal." An unsigned notice announcing "a convention to discuss the social, civil, and religious condition and rights of woman" was placed in the *Seneca County Courier* (the date it first ran is disputed; see §6). The meeting place was the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls: a brick church, 44 by 64 feet with a gallery on three sides, completed in October 1843 by a reform-minded Wesleyan Methodist congregation, and already known, in the NPS's words, as a local haven for antislavery activity, political rallies, and free speech events. The *True Wesleyan*'s contemporary description, quoted by the NPS, is of a building "well finished, though, as it should be, it is plain."

The convention's own *Report* — printed in Rochester in 1848 by John Dick at the North Star Office, Frederick Douglass's printing office — is the controlling record of what happened inside. The convention assembled at 11 o'clock on the morning of **July 19, 1848**. Mary M'Clintock was appointed secretary; Stanton stated the object of the meeting; Mott urged the women present to join in the debates. Stanton then read the Declaration of Sentiments, and it was re-read by paragraph, discussed, and amended. The *Report* states the shape of the two days plainly: discussed throughout two entire days, the first day by women exclusively, the second with men participating. On the afternoon of the 19th, papers were circulated to obtain signatures. On Thursday morning, **July 20**, with James Mott of Philadelphia in the chair, Stanton read the Declaration again; it was freely discussed — by Mott, Ansel Bascom, S. E. Woodworth, Thomas and Mary Ann M'Clintock, Frederick Douglass, Amy Post, Catharine Stebbins, and Stanton, the *Report* names them — and was unanimously adopted. The resolutions were then taken up one by one. Most passed with little remark; the one that drew real debate was the ninth, resolving "That it is the duty of the women of this country to secure to themselves their sacred right to the elective franchise" — the demand for the vote. Douglass, the convention's most famous man present and, per the NPS's abolition history, a signer and abolitionist ally of the organizers, spoke for it, and it carried. His *North Star* printed the proceedings, and the *Report* tract was reprinted several times and circulated as a sales item at later conventions, in the NPS's description.

The names are the convention's most quoted number, and its most fragile (see §6). The *Report* prints the signers in two lists: 68 women, headed by Lucretia Mott, and 32 men, headed by Richard P. Hunt — 100 names in all, out of the roughly 300 people the NPS states met at the chapel. Among the women: Stanton herself, Eunice Newton Foote, Mary Ann M'Clintock, Martha C. Wright, Jane C. Hunt, and Charlotte Woodward, who alone of the 100, the National Archives notes, lived to see the Nineteenth Amendment ratified in 1920 — 72 years after she signed. Stanton died in 1902, eighteen years before that ratification, as the NPS park history puts it.

The chapel outlived its congregation's use of it and was repeatedly remade. The NPS place record states the original red brick church was sold by the congregation in 1871 and extensively altered by subsequent owners; the park's chapel history carries the succession — Johnson Hall, then the Johnson Opera House, and through the first half of the twentieth century a furniture store, movie theater, car garage and repair shop, and laundromat. The connection was not forgotten: Harriot Stanton Blatch, Stanton's daughter, returned in 1908 to place a commemorative plaque on the opera house wall. Congress authorized the park on **December 28, 1980**; the Stanton House, a National Historic Landmark since 1965, was purchased by the Elizabeth Cady Stanton Foundation and donated to the NPS in 1982, per the NPS Stanton House page. The chapel's modern rehabilitation, begun in August 2009 under the NPS's Wesleyan Chapel Rehabilitation Project, rebuilt the north and south walls and replaced doors, windows, and roofing — the form in which the building stands now.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a plain brick chapel, 44 by 64 feet, gallery on three sides, finished in October 1843 and filled on July 19–20, 1848, by some 300 people for two days of debate. **Now:** the rehabilitated Wesleyan Chapel on Fall Street, its surviving original fabric kept and highlighted in the NPS's display, standing beside Declaration Park and the Visitor Center at 136 Fall Street.

**Then:** a declaration drafted in Waterloo, read aloud, amended paragraph by paragraph, adopted unanimously on July 20, 1848, and signed by 100 of those present. **Now:** the Declaration's full text and all 100 signatures cut into Declaration Park's water wall between the Visitor Center and the chapel — the NPS notes the Declaration of Water Wall depicts all 100 signatures from the Declaration of Sentiments — while the signed original itself has never been found (see §6).

**Then:** Stanton's "Grassmere" at 32 Washington Street — a farm house with a north wing, an east kitchen wing, outbuildings, orchards, and two acres of gardens, home to a family of nine. **Now:** the Stanton House as the NPS holds it, its demolished wings gone and only their outlines visible, the lot long since split into parcels — in Harriot Stanton Blatch's words, quoted by the NPS, a house with "wings clipped and its acres of shaded lawn and gardens cut to meet the needs of a growing neighborhood."

## 4. What's still there
- The Wesleyan Chapel at Fall and Mynderse Streets, Seneca Falls — the 1848 convention ground, rehabilitated under the NPS project begun in August 2009 (north and south walls rebuilt; doors, windows, and roofing replaced), displaying the highlighted historic fabric of the original 1843 building (NPS place record and rehabilitation project page)
- Declaration Park, between the Visitor Center and the chapel, with the water wall carrying the Declaration of Sentiments and all 100 signatures (NPS park history)
- The Visitor Center at 136 Fall Street, with exhibits and the park film (NPS park brochure text)
- The Elizabeth Cady Stanton House, 32 Washington Street — Stanton's home 1847–1862, a National Historic Landmark since 1965, held by the NPS since 1982; the outlines of its demolished north and east wings remain visible (NPS Stanton House page)
- The M'Clintock House in Waterloo, where the Declaration was drafted, and the Hunt House in Waterloo, where the five organizers met over tea (NPS park history)
- The documentary record: the 1848 *Report of the Woman's Rights Convention* (North Star Office, Rochester), and the Library of Congress HABS surveys — HABS NY-6304 for the Wesleyan Chapel (10 measured drawings) and HABS NY-6103 for the Stanton House

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Visitor Center, 136 Fall Street:** Start where the NPS directs visitors to start — exhibits and the park film, in the building that shares its site with the chapel, free as the NPS fees page states.
2. **In Declaration Park:** Read the Declaration where it is cut into the water wall, all 100 signatures with it, on the grass between the Visitor Center and the chapel it was signed in.
3. **In the Wesleyan Chapel:** Stand in the room of July 19–20, 1848 — the 11 o'clock morning session, the paragraph-by-paragraph reading, the unanimous adoption on the 20th — in a building whose surviving 1843 fabric the rehabilitation kept visible.
4. **At the Stanton House, 32 Washington Street:** The convention's domestic ground — "Grassmere," the "Center of the Rebellion," where Stanton lived the fourteen years that began a year before the convention, with the lost wings' outlines still readable.
5. **In Waterloo:** The M'Clintock House, where the Declaration was drafted, and the Hunt House, where five women over tea decided to call the convention — the park's second village.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The signer count is printed here as the record carries it, with its weakness stated. The 1848 *Report* prints two lists — 68 women's names and 32 men's names, 100 in total — and the NPS park history and participants page both carry that count ("Only 100 people signed their names to the Declaration of Sentiments," of 300 who attended). But the signed original has never been recovered: the NPS's own abolition history states the original Declaration "has been lost to time," and the National Archives' account records that its location is unknown and it may not have survived. The 100 therefore rests on a printed tract, not on a surviving signed sheet that can be recounted. Later tellings drift from the *Report*'s arithmetic in both directions — some say "over 100" signed, some "nearly one hundred" — and Britannica's account notes that many signatories later withdrew their names under the ridicule that followed publication; how many withdrew, no source consulted states. Attendance is likewise an estimate: the *Report* gives no headcount, and the roughly 300 carried here is the NPS's figure, not a counted one.

The convention notice's date splits. The Library of Congress record for the convention call states the call "was published in the Seneca County courier, July 14, 1848, without any signatures," and Stanton's own reminiscence agrees with the 14th — "giving only five days' notice." Other accounts place the notice's first appearance on July 11 — the Smithsonian Magazine account and the Cornell University Press park history both carry July 11, the latter giving the organizers "only five days' advance notice" by a different reckoning and the former eight days to the convention. Both dates are printed; this draft does not choose. The tea at the Hunt house splits the same way: the NPS's early park brochure dates the five reformers' meeting to July 9, 1848, while the Cornell Press history dates the tea to July 13. The drafting session at the M'Clintock house is carried as July 16 by the Cornell history; no NPS source consulted fixes that day.

The chapel's sale year is carried two ways inside NPS-derived records: the NPS place record states the congregation sold the original church in 1871, while the chapel's narrative history has the Wesleyan Methodists moving to a new Fall Street church and the building passing to other uses from 1872, and the Register summary carries a sale in 1873. The years are printed as their sources carry them.

Hours are not printed as a single schedule in §1 because the operator's own pages do not agree on one: the park FAQ carries the Visitor Center as open daily except winter holidays, 9:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., with building tour schedules varying by season, while the park's current hours listing carries seasonal schedules that change through the year. The fee, by contrast, is unambiguous on the NPS fees page: free, no pass required.

The NPS National Register record for the Wesleyan Methodist Church (80000361) carries no latitude or longitude. The one NPS point found — the latitude/longitude on the park's NPGallery photo asset records (42.9025993347168, −76.8444976806641) — is repeated identically across the park's assets as a park-level point and does not agree with the chapel's Fall Street position as the Register record's own street address (126 Fall St.) places it; it is not a verified point for this ground, so the locator below prints no coordinates, and coordinates appearing in lead-list sources were not used.
