# Smith Robertson School through the eyes of Ruby Dawson

Labeled research draft · On Old Ground · Mississippi Regional March OP10 · November 21, 2026 Pacific

The city confirms the museum and its exhibit program but does not print museum-specific hours on the page checked. ## 2. Story

Ruby Dawson knew Smith Robertson from both sides of the classroom. Born in Jackson in 1904, she remembered attending in the third and fourth grades before going to Tougaloo. In Roy Welch’s 1978 interview she remembered Principal Lanier leading drills on the playground, geography as a favorite subject, and textbooks that families bought. Asked whether the school provided lunch she said, “We carried our lunch.” Her rough recollection of about 45 in her grade is a pupil’s estimate. The lunch and textbook details reveal some work the school day required of families. [4]

Dawson returned as a teacher, dating the return to 1928. She recalled starting with third grade, and the edited draft transcript places a first-grade transfer and subsequent work at other Jackson schools in her career. Her answer to the interviewer’s salary question includes an initial $35 and editorial marks. This is a memory of pay, not a confirmed payroll entry. She recalled the building had been remodeled between the years she knew it as a student and teacher. The interviewer’s manuscript preserves revisions. Listen for the changes Dawson chooses to remember without treating every phrase in a draft as settled chronology. [4]

The school’s name honors Smith Robertson, born enslaved in Alabama, who worked as a barber and served Jackson as a city alderman and school trustee. The National Register nomination quotes the 1894 bond ordinance for a school building for Black children. A frame building followed. It was named for Robertson in 1903 and burned in 1909. C.H. Carroll’s replacement reopened that September. The 1929 Hull and Malvaney scheme wrapped part of the 1909 school. The nomination reports that the earlier fabric could no longer be visually distinguished. Later wings and lunchroom work extended the school again. That succession explains why Dawson’s memories fit this address even when the wall before a modern visitor belongs to another building campaign. [2–5]

## 3. Then and now

The former school is now a city museum. The city lists a school-history exhibit, a Farish Street gallery and its version of the Smithsonian’s Field to Factory exhibition. The school’s three-bay entrance, low-relief accents and bands of classroom windows still give readers a way to see the building as a workplace. The galleries are a later use of those spaces. The nomination dates lunchroom construction to 1941 and 1952, after Dawson’s remembered years as a pupil. [1–5]

## 5. Public progression

From the Bloom Street sidewalk, read the frontispiece against the rhythm of windows that served classrooms. If the museum is open, begin with the school-history gallery and move to the Farish Street interpretation where staff direct. **Story landing — The pupil who came back to teach.** Dawson’s lunch, textbooks and credit for her teachers put household labor and classroom labor back into the building’s history. [1–5]

## 6. Uncertainty and disputes

The 1894 ordinance, early frame school, 1909 rebuilding and 1929 Art Deco work refer to different stages. The inventory’s single 1909 field should not be used to date the visible 1929 design. The state property inventory says 520 Bloom; the nomination and visitor bureau give 528 Bloom. Use 528 as the visitor address and confirm the posted entry. Telephone and holiday status remain open. The city-linked legacy museum website redirected to unrelated content during this check and provides no operator evidence. The original edited Dawson typescript is accessible, while the tape and pay records have yet to be checked. [1–5]
