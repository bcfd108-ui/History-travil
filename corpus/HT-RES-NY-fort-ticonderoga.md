# HT-RES-NY-fort-ticonderoga — Stone bastions above the narrows where two lakes meet
Division NY seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

A French star fort built in the 1750s on a promontory between Lake Champlain and the outlet of Lake George, fought over by three armies, blown up once by its own builders, captured at dawn in 1775 in the first American victory of the Revolution, and rebuilt in the twentieth century as one of the earliest great restoration projects in America — still private, still a museum, and still commanding the water it was built to close.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a reconstructed eighteenth-century fort and its battlefield landscape: Fort Ticonderoga, at 102 Fort Ti Road, Ticonderoga, New York 12883, in Essex County, on the New York shore of Lake Champlain near the south end of the lake. The operator is The Fort Ticonderoga Association, a 501(c)(3) non-profit educational organization; the Association's own FAQ states plainly that the site is not state or federally owned, and that it is owned and operated by the Association. The Association's FAQ states the visiting terms, and they are printed here as that page carries them: Fort Ticonderoga and Mount Defiance are open for visitation Tuesday–Sunday, May 2–October 25, 2026, from 9:30 a.m. until 5:00 p.m., with the last ticket sold and the gate to Mount Defiance closing at 4:30 p.m.; general admission tickets are valid for two consecutive days, and parking is included. The Association describes a 2,000-acre landscape, welcoming visitors since 1909, open for daily visitation May through October with special programs during Winter Quarters, November through April. The FAQ page consulted states no dollar admission amounts, and none are printed here (see §6). The fort and its associated ground were added to the National Register of Historic Places on October 15, 1966 (NRHP reference no. 66000519, per the NPS National Register record), as part of the Fort Ticonderoga / Mount Independence National Historic Landmark district, which crosses the lake into Vermont.

## 2. The story
The ground came first. Ticonderoga stands on a promontory above the narrows between Lake Champlain and Lake George — in the Association's press-kit phrasing, above the narrow choke-point between the two lakes, which controlled the major north–south inland water route between New York and Canada. Whoever held the promontory held the corridor.

France moved first in the French and Indian War. The Association's own research writing identifies the fort's birth certificate: orders from the Governor of Canada dated **September 20, 1755**, directing Michel Chartier de Lotbinière — a Canadian-born officer trained in military engineering in France, in the tradition of Vauban — to survey Fort Saint-Frédéric at Crown Point and then build a new fortification at the place already called Carillon, to cover Crown Point and block a British approach to New France. The work began in **1755** and went slowly; manpower, supplies, and the short working season all dragged on it, and Lotbinière's pace drew criticism from French officers at the time. The fort was first called Fort Vaudreuil, for the Canadian governor, and was more familiarly known as Fort Carillon; the English name that replaced both, Ticonderoga, comes from the Iroquoian word for the place. How long the building took is framed differently by the Association's own publications, and the difference is printed as a gap in §6 rather than smoothed over here.

In **1757** the fort was already a staging ground: the Association's timeline records that more than 8,000 French, Canadian, and Native forces left Carillon that year to attack the British Fort William Henry at the head of Lake George.

The fort's great test came the next summer. On **July 8, 1758**, a British army under General James Abercromby assaulted the French lines on the heights in front of the fort — a log breastwork fronted by felled trees, built in haste by the Marquis de Montcalm's much smaller French force. The British attacked frontally, repeatedly, without first bringing their artillery to bear, and were cut down in front of the lines. The Association's press kit calls it, in America's terms, the bloodiest battle until the Civil War, with nearly 16,000 British troops suffering almost 2,000 casualties assaulting the French positions; the Association's own battlefield trail guide gives the attacking army as nearly 15,000 and the loss the same way, nearly 2,000 killed and wounded. The two Association figures do not agree with each other, and §6 prints the disagreement rather than choosing between the operator's own publications. What no source consulted disputes is the shape of the day: a small French army behind fieldworks destroyed the largest army yet assembled in North America, in front of a fort the attackers never reached.

It bought Carillon one year. In **1759** a larger British advance under General Jeffrey Amherst forced the issue methodically, with siege batteries rather than a frontal rush. On the night of **July 25–26, 1759** — the Association's facts page dates the destruction to July 26 — the French abandoned the fort, blew up its powder magazine, and retreated north. The British took the ruin, repaired it, and renamed it Fort Ticonderoga. The Association's trail guide preserves a physical footnote to that siege: of the three artillery batteries the British built in July 1759, one that held six 24-pounders and two 12-pounders was ordered torn down afterward and never was, and remains a prominent feature on the landscape today.

The British then held a deteriorating post with a small garrison through the peace that followed the French and Indian War. The fort's second famous morning came sixteen years later. In May **1775**, weeks after Lexington and Concord and before any declaration of independence, Ethan Allen, Benedict Arnold, and the Green Mountain Boys crossed the lake in the dark and took the fort from its small, surprised British garrison at dawn on **May 10, 1775**. The Association calls the capture the first American victory of the Revolutionary War. Its material value was as large as its symbolic one: the fort's artillery train was the best available to the American cause, and in December 1775 Henry Knox began hauling the captured guns overland to Boston. Guns from Ticonderoga went onto Dorchester Heights, and the British evacuated Boston on March 17, 1776.

Americans held and massively refortified the Ticonderoga peninsula in **1776**, after the failed invasion of Canada fell back to it, adding miles of works across the lake on Mount Independence. In **1777** General John Burgoyne's advance from Canada reversed the position: British guns placed on Mount Defiance, the height above the fort that the Americans had left unfortified, made the whole complex untenable, and Ticonderoga fell to the British again that July without the storm its walls had survived in 1758. After Burgoyne's surrender at Saratoga that autumn, the British garrison abandoned Ticonderoga and returned to Canada, destroying what it could not carry. The fort was never used as a military post again.

The ruin became property, then a project. In 1820 William Ferris Pell purchased the ruins and the surrounding land to preserve them. His descendants Stephen and Sarah Pell began the restoration in the first decade of the twentieth century, and the restored fort **opened to the public in 1909**, an event the Association dates its visitor history from; the Association itself, as the private not-for-profit body that still runs the site, dates to 1931 in the older site histories the Association's materials reference. The restoration the Pells began is the fort the visitor walks today — a reconstruction on the original ground, a distinction the site itself now interprets openly, including where a century of scholarship has moved past the rebuild's details.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a French star fort of stone and timber on a wooded promontory, built from 1755 to close the water corridor between Lake George and Lake Champlain, fronted in July 1758 by a hasty log breastwork on the heights behind which some thousands of French troops waited. **Now:** the reconstructed fort on the same promontory, operated as a museum by The Fort Ticonderoga Association across a 2,000-acre landscape that the Association's press kit describes as including the Carillon Battlefield and the largest series of untouched Revolutionary War–era earthworks surviving in America.

**Then:** in July 1759, three British siege batteries dug in against Carillon, guns that never fired before the French blew the magazine and left. **Now:** one of those batteries still stands as a landscape feature on the Association's Carillon Battlefield trail, identified in the Association's own trail guide.

**Then:** a ruin through the nineteenth century, its stone walls standing roofless over the lake, bought in 1820 by William Ferris Pell to keep it from being quarried away. **Now:** a restored fort open since 1909 — barracks, bastions, guns, gardens, and a museum collection the Association describes as one of the most important collections of eighteenth-century militaria in North America, with a research center open to scholars by appointment.

## 4. What's still there
- The reconstructed fort itself on its original promontory — stone bastions, barracks ranges, and the place d'armes — documented by the Historic American Buildings Survey in 1934–35 (HABS NY-3212, Library of Congress) and photographed for this entry from that survey (see §7)
- The Carillon Battlefield, with the 1¾-mile Carillon Battlefield hiking trail (Association trail guide), including remains of the 1758 French Lines breastwork — a low hump and ditch on the low ground near the La Chute River — and ground the Americans rebuilt in 1776 into the Great Redoubt
- A surviving British artillery battery of July 1759, built for six 24-pounder and two 12-pounder cannon, never demolished after the siege (Association trail guide)
- Mount Defiance, included in general admission by token, with the summit view over Lake Champlain and Vermont's Green Mountains that the Association's FAQ describes — the height whose occupation by British guns in 1777 decided the fort's fate that year
- The King's Garden and the wider gardens, included in the admission the FAQ describes
- The museum and its collections, and the Thompson Pell Research Center, where the Association's FAQ invites researchers to schedule visits to collections it calls one of the most important holdings of eighteenth-century militaria in North America
- The *Carillon* boat cruise on Lake Champlain, offered by the Association as a combination ticket in season (FAQ: daily cruises May 22–October 25, 2026, as that page carries it)
- Mount Independence, across the strait in Vermont, the American side of the 1776–77 fortified complex, joined with the fort in the single National Historic Landmark district of the National Register record

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At the Log House Welcome Center:** Start where the Association starts visitors — admission, the day's itinerary of guided tours and demonstrations that the FAQ promises on arrival, and the museum store and café the FAQ places in the Log House.
2. **Inside the fort:** The place d'armes and the barracks ranges first, then the bastions — the ground the HABS photographers documented in 1934–35, guns on the bastion looking out over the narrows the fort was built to close.
3. **On the Carillon Battlefield trail:** The 1¾-mile loop the Association maps — the French Lines breastwork, the ground where the British army formed on the morning of July 8, 1758, the center of the attack, and the surviving 1759 British battery.
4. **In the King's Garden:** The walled garden the admission includes, the fort's domestic counterweight, reached by the King's Garden parking the FAQ directs drivers to.
5. **On Mount Defiance:** End above the whole position — the fort, the strait, Mount Independence, and the lake — reading the 1777 lesson from the height that taught it: ground left empty above a fort is part of the fort's story whether its defenders count it or not.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The 1758 casualty and army figures do not agree inside the operator's own publications, and the disagreement is printed rather than resolved. The Fort Ticonderoga Association's press kit (2022) states that on July 8, 1758, nearly 16,000 British troops suffered almost 2,000 casualties assaulting the French positions, and calls the battle America's bloodiest until the Civil War. The Association's own Carillon Battlefield trail guide states that nearly 15,000 British and American troops formed for the attack that morning, that nearly 2,000 British and American soldiers were killed and wounded at the center of the attack, and calls it the bloodiest battle on the continent until the 1840s. The attacking army is thus carried as nearly 16,000 by one Association publication and nearly 15,000 by another, and the "bloodiest until" framing is carried two ways as well. No French casualty figure is asserted in this draft: the Association sources consulted give the French force's size in general terms for 1757–58 but no agreed 1758 French loss figure, and none is imported from outside the primary sources consulted.

The fort's construction dates are likewise framed differently by the sources consulted. The Association's press-kit timeline enters the event as "1755 — French forces build Fort Carillon at Ticonderoga." The Association's facts page states that construction was begun in 1755 and continued until the fort was captured by the British in 1759 — a framing that runs the building period to the capture. Other framings in circulation close the construction period earlier, in 1757 or 1758. This draft prints the start year, 1755, on which the Association's own materials and Lotbinière's orders of September 20, 1755 agree, and prints the end of construction as unsettled between the sources rather than picking a closing year.

The Association's FAQ page consulted for this entry states the season, the days, and the hours, and states that tickets are valid for two consecutive days — but it states no dollar admission amounts. Following the rule that fees print only as the operator's own site carries them, no admission prices are printed in §1. A visitor should expect to pay admission — this is a privately operated museum, not a public park — and should take the current amounts from the Association's ticketing pages, which were not used as a source for figures in this draft.

No coordinates are printed in the locator below. The National Register nomination for the landmark district marks its location "not for publication," the NPS National Register record consulted carries no latitude or longitude, and the Library of Congress HABS record for the fort carries an empty latitude/longitude in its place field. Coordinates appearing in lead-list sources were not used.

The first photo's frame record (§7) carries no separate date field; its date lives in its title, as a range — 1934-35 — and it is printed as a range, not resolved to a single year.
