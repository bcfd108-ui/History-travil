# HT-RES-OH-edison-birthplace — The inventor's first address was a canal town's brick cottage, and the canal is still in the yard
Division OH seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Thomas Alva Edison was born in a one-and-a-half-story brick cottage in Milan, Ohio, a grain port that a three-mile canal had briefly made famous, and he left before he turned seven. What makes the ground worth the trip is how much of that first world survives in one small lot: the house his father designed, on the lot his mother bought, with the dry remnant of the Milan Canal still running along its north side — the water that built the town, outlasted by the boy who was born beside it.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a museum visit: the Thomas Edison Birthplace Museum, 9 N. Edison Dr., Milan, OH 44846, operated by the Edison Birthplace Association, Inc. As the operator's own Plan Your Visit page states it: the museum is open seasonally, February through December, with seasonal hours that visitors are told to check on the website before coming — the operator's page checked for this draft prints no fixed daily clock hours, and none are printed here. Guided tours of the Birthplace home are offered in English, run about one hour, run regularly through the day on a first-come, first-served basis, and need no reservation for standard visits. Admission as the operator states it: Preschool & Military — FREE; Children (18 and under), Students (with valid ID) & Seniors (60+) — $12; Adults (18+) — $17; Special Family Rate (2 Adults or Seniors + 2/3 Children) — $45. Access to the gift shop is free and does not require admission. The operator states plainly that the Birthplace home is not currently wheelchair accessible, and gives the contact number 419.499.2135 for planning help. The visit is to original fabric, not a reconstruction: the National Register nomination records that the house "has seen very few exterior or interior alterations and appears much as it did when the Edisons occupied it."

## 2. The story
The Edisons came to Milan because Milan was booming, and Milan was booming because of a canal. The Milan Canal was completed in **1839** — three miles of dug channel linking the village to the navigable Huron River and so to Lake Erie. The Ohio Historical Society's marker at the basin records what followed: fourteen warehouses around the basin, as many as twenty schooners a day at the height of it, and seventy-five vessels built in the basin across the port's life. A scholarly account in the Ohio Archaeological and Historical Society's journal makes the geographic point plainly: a rich triangular farming hinterland, a partly navigable river, and a short canal turned a small inland village into a lake port, and wagon roads from the whole region had to cross the sandy belt to reach it. Grain came to Milan because Milan was where the water was.

Samuel Edison arrived in that boom from political trouble. The National Register nomination recounts the family line: the inventor's grandfather served in the British forces in the War of 1812, and his father, Samuel Edison Jr., left Canada after taking part in the unsuccessful Papineau–Mackenzie Rebellion and settled in Milan. Samuel was a shingle maker by trade. In **1841**, his wife Nancy Elliott Edison purchased the lot on what is now Edison Drive, and Samuel designed and built the house on it himself — a gabled brick cottage of one and a half stories, sitting on the west side of the street a short distance from the canal basin and its shipyards (the construction year is disputed between two federal records; see §6).

In that house, on **February 11, 1847**, Thomas Alva Edison was born — the seventh and last child of Samuel and Nancy Edison. Dr. Lehman Galpin, whose own 1847 house still stands nearby, was the physician present at the birth, as the Ohio marker record for the Galpin house notes. Milan in 1847 was at its peak as a grain market; the boy's first memories, as he set them down late in life and as the state later reproduced them on a marker at the site, were of wheat elevators on the canal, the Gay shipyard, the launching of new boats with the whole town watching, and the square packed with farm teams.

The nomination is honest about how thin the Milan record of the boy himself is. What survives is recollection: watching the California-bound wagons pass, visiting the shipyards on the canal a short distance north of the house, sitting on a nest of goose eggs to see if he could hatch them, and burning down a barn. The nomination itself prints the caution alongside the anecdotes — one biographer called the barn fire Edison's first "spectacular experiment," while Henry Ford, Edison's friend and admirer, wrote that nothing appears to have happened at Milan particularly to impress him. Both readings print in the federal record; neither is provable from the ground.

In **1854**, with Milan's canal trade already fading as conditions changed, the family sold up and moved to Port Huron, Michigan. The boy was seven. The rest of the nomination's arc is the famous one, told here only to close the Milan chapter: self-education, telegraphy inventions by sixteen, a New York position in **1869**, the move to Menlo Park, New Jersey, in **1877**, the incandescent lamp in **1879**, the Pearl Street power plant in **1882**, a Congressional gold medal in **1928**, and death on **October 18, 1931**.

The house, though, came back to the family. Edison's sister Marion Edison Page, who had married in the house's parlor in 1849 and stayed in Milan when the family left, bought the home in **1894**. After her death it passed to Edison, who acquired it from his sister's family in **1906** and held it for the rest of his life. On **February 11, 1947** — the centennial of his birth — his daughter Madeleine Edison Sloane opened the house to the public as the Thomas Edison Birthplace Museum, and the Edison Birthplace Association has run it since. The house was designated a National Historic Landmark in **1965** and entered in the National Register of Historic Places on **October 15, 1966**. Federal surveyors had already documented it a generation earlier: the Historic American Buildings Survey photographed the house in **April 1934**, thirteen years before it opened as a museum, and that survey is this entry's first photograph (§7).

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a working canal basin a short walk north of the house — fourteen warehouses, schooners loading grain for lake and world ports, seventy-five vessels built in the basin over the port's life, and a boy watching launchings from the ridge the town called the Hogback. **Now:** the nomination records what remains on the birthplace lot itself — on the north side of the house, the remnant of the once famous Milan Canal, dry, no longer containing water, and heavily grown over with foliage. The port is gone; its ditch survives in the inventor's yard.

**Then:** April 1934, the HABS camera in the snow — a small brick cottage under bare trees, porch across the front, snow on the roofs, documented as a private house three years after Edison's death (§7). **Now:** the same house, on the same original lot, as a guided-tour museum — the nomination's measured judgment is that it appears much as it did when the Edisons occupied it, and the operator's tour still runs about an hour through the home Edison was born in.

**Then:** February 11, 1847, a seventh child born in the sitting room of a canal port at its peak, to a shingle maker who had fled a failed rebellion. **Now:** a National Historic Landmark (1965) and National Register listing (No. 66000608) whose entire nominated ground is less than one acre — a lot the nomination's boundary description measures at 66 feet by 115.5 feet.

## 4. What's still there
- The birthplace house itself — one-and-a-half-story gabled brick cottage designed by Samuel Edison, on the original lot on the west side of Edison Drive, with very few alterations per the National Register nomination
- The remnant of the Milan Canal on the north side of the house — dry and overgrown, as the nomination's boundary continuation sheet describes it
- The birth room and the adjoining display room described in the nomination — the sitting room where Edison was born, and the room west of it holding Edison inventions, letters, sketches, and memorabilia
- A date of 1847 at a corner of the house, the year of Edison's birth, noted in the nomination's description
- The National Historic Landmark plaque at the site, recording designation under the Historic Sites Act of August 21, 1935, dated 1965
- The museum complex the operator runs today — the Birthplace home, a gift shop open without admission, and guided tours of about one hour (operator's Plan Your Visit page)
- The Milan Canal Basin ground a short distance north, with the Ohio Historical Society's 1966 basin marker (Marker 4-22) recording the port's warehouses, schooners, and shipbuilding
- The HABS record itself — three photographs, two measured drawings, and data pages from the April 1934 survey, held by the Library of Congress as HABS OH-22-22

5. **The basin ground beyond the lot:** The Milan Canal Basin marker stands on its own ground a short distance away, with its own record; this entry claims only the remnant on the birthplace lot.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The construction year does not agree between the two federal records, and both print. The National Register nomination states the house "was constructed in 1841 on the lot purchased that year by Nancy Elliot Edison," and the HABS data page likewise reads "Built 1841 by Samuel Edison." The Library of Congress's own catalog metadata for the same HABS survey, however, carries "Building/structure dates: 1842 Initial Construction." No source checked resolves the one-year difference; 1841 is used in §2 because the nomination and the survey's own data page agree on it, but the LOC metadata's 1842 stands here unreconciled.

The street address carries three forms across the records, printed as found: the 1934 HABS survey titles the house "Front Street & Choate Avenue"; the nomination places it on "Edison Drive"; the operator's current address is 9 N. Edison Dr. These are successive namings of the same lot, not three houses, but no source checked dates the renamings, and that gap stands.

The landmark dates are three distinct layers, not one: National Historic Landmark designation in 1965 (the site's plaque), entry in the National Register on October 15, 1966 (NPS NPGallery record for No. 66000608), and the nomination form itself certified January 23, 1979, when the Historic Sites Survey prepared the landmark documentation. All three print; none is "the" listing date on its own.

The Milan childhood anecdotes — the goose eggs, the barn fire — rest on recollection, and the nomination says so itself, printing Henry Ford's skepticism beside the biographers' readings. They are carried in §2 as recollection, not as documented events. What Edison himself remembered (wheat elevators, the shipyard, launchings) comes from his own late-life account as the state reproduced it; it is his testimony, not a contemporary record.

The family's arrival year is not fixed by the records checked: Ohio Magazine's account has Samuel Edison coming to Milan in 1840 and the family purchasing the lot and building in 1841; the nomination fixes only the 1841 lot purchase and construction. No coordinates print from the nomination in latitude/longitude form — the form carries a UTM reference only, carried in §10 in its own form rather than converted.
