# HT-RES-MI-hartwick-pines — The stand the saws left standing

Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

Hartwick Pines State Park, northeast of Grayling in Crawford County, holds what the Michigan History Center calls the Lower Peninsula's largest remaining stand of old-growth white pine — a remnant of the primeval forest that Michigan's logging era otherwise took down. The park pairs that surviving stand with the Hartwick Pines Logging Museum, a Civilian Conservation Corps-built recreation of an 1890s logging camp, and a visitor center whose exhibit hall carries Michigan's forests from the Ice Age to the present. The forest is the primary document here: the museum explains an industry, but the 49 acres of standing pine are the record itself — the piece of the nineteenth-century woods that was deeded to the state in 1927, thinned by a single storm in 1940, and never cut.

## 1. Trip gate
- **Vehicle entry:** A Recreation Passport is required for entry into Hartwick Pines State Park (Michigan DNR). Admission to the museums themselves was free as the DNR stated it in 2015; that statement is dated, and this entry prints no current fee beyond it.
- **Seasons rule the visit.** The Logging Museum is open daily May 1 through Labor Day and on weekends from Labor Day through October 31; it is closed November 1 through April 30 (DNR visitor center page). The visitor center keeps weekend hours in the shoulder seasons and is closed November 15 through December 31. A winter trip gets the forest and the park, not the museum.
- **Park hours** are carried in the DNR's park listing as 8 a.m. to 10 p.m. daily.
- **Two addresses circulate** — 3612 State Park Drive in the DNR park listing and 2833 Monarch Drive on the DNR visitor center page. The conflict is printed in §6; a driver should confirm the entrance road (M-93, Hartwick Pines Road) rather than trust either number blindly.
- The Old Growth Foot Trail is paved and accessible (park interpretive panel), so the core of the visit — the stand itself — does not depend on rough-ground ability.

## 2. The story
From 1870 to 1900, Michigan was the national leader in lumber production (Michigan History Center). The white pine came down across the northern Lower Peninsula in those decades, camp by camp and river drive by river drive, and the country around Grayling was worked like the rest: the park's own interpretive panel records that the Salling-Hanson Company of Grayling logged most of the area between 1883 and 1925 and built logging camps wherever they were needed. What that work left behind is still legible in the park — the same panel points visitors to the stump fields, whole fields of stumps along the park's trails bearing witness to the cutting.

One tract was never cut. Edward E. Hartwick was a Michigan lumberman who went to two wars: lieutenant, 9th Cavalry, in the Spanish-American War, and major, 20th Engineers, in the World War. The State of Michigan's memorial tablet to him reads, in its heading, "Michigan 1871 • France 1918" — he died in France in 1918. The tablet's text gives the gift that made this park: "this remnant of the primeval pine forests which he loved is given by his wife Karen B. Hartwick to the people of this commonwealth." Karen Hartwick purchased the tract northeast of Grayling — 8,236 acres by the marker record — and gave it to the state in 1927 as a memorial to her husband, the old-growth pine at its heart.

The gift came with conditions, and the conditions built the park. The Michigan History Center's park historian, Hillary Pine, has described the deed's requirements: the state was to build a road to the park — present-day M-93, Hartwick Pines Road — build a Memorial Building dedicated to Edward, and construct a lumbering museum. The Memorial Building went up in 1929, built of logs brought down from the Lovells area under Karen Hartwick's direction; it was the first public structure in the park and served as the park's visitor center for over sixty years (park panel).

The museum condition was met by the Civilian Conservation Corps. A CCC camp operated in the park from 1933 to 1942, planting hundreds of thousands of trees, cutting trails and fire breaks, and building the museum buildings (park panel). The Logging Museum was built in 1934–35, its log buildings recreating the structures of an original 1890s logging camp: a bunkhouse, a mess hall, a company store, a blacksmith shop (panel; the Michigan History Center describes the same recreations and the camp world they depict). Around the buildings stand the outdoor exhibits of the trade's heavy iron — among them the Enterprise Mill, dated by the park panel to 1940, and a steam traction engine the panel identifies as a 1912 Port Huron. The sawmill is operated during the park's festivals every summer.

The stand itself took its one great wound in peacetime. The deed gift's old growth — 86 acres by the History Center historian's account of the deed — lost about a third of its trees in the Armistice Day Storm of November 11, 1940. Forty-nine acres of old growth remain, and the park panel gives the trees their measure plainly: "Towering 350 year-old white pine trees dominate the 49 acre old growth forest." Those trees were already old when the logging era began; they are the same forest the Salling-Hanson crews worked around, left standing first by a widow's purchase and then by the state's keeping.

Today the park is kept jointly in its two parts: the Michigan DNR runs the park, and the Logging Museum is jointly managed by the DNR and the Michigan Historical Center (park panel). The Michigan Forest Visitor Center carries the long telling — a 1,500-square-foot exhibit hall, "Michigan's Forests … Past, Present and Future," running from the Ice Age to the present, with an auditorium and a Friends of Hartwick Pines bookstore (DNR). In summer the museum adds costumed interpreters (Michigan History Center), and the park's festivals — Wood Shaving Days and Black Iron Days, named on the park panel — bring the demonstrations, the whittlers and blacksmiths, and the running sawmill.

## 3. Then / now
- **Then:** A working lumber landscape — Salling-Hanson camps and cuts across the Grayling country, 1883–1925, in the decades when Michigan led the nation in lumber (1870–1900). **Now:** Stump fields inside a state park, pointed out by the park's own panels as evidence, in second forest the CCC helped plant.
- **Then:** An uncut remnant of primeval white pine, the largest stand left in the Lower Peninsula, deeded to the state in 1927 as one man's memorial. **Now:** 49 acres of that stand, thinned by the 1940 Armistice Day Storm, walked on a paved, accessible trail.
- **Then:** The 1890s logging camp — bunkhouse, mess hall, company store, blacksmith shop — as a workplace that vanished with the era. **Now:** Those same building types standing again as the Logging Museum, built by the CCC in 1934–35 to the camp's pattern.
- **Then:** The Memorial Building of 1929, the park's first public structure and its visitor center for over sixty years. **Now:** The Michigan Forest Visitor Center carries the visitor function, with the exhibit hall's Ice Age-to-present telling; the Memorial Building stands as the deed's condition made log and stone.

## 4. What's still there
- **The old-growth stand:** 49 acres of white pine, trees the park panel ages at 350 years, reached by the paved Old Growth Foot Trail from the visitor center area. This is the entry's core — the surviving piece of the pre-logging forest.
- **The Logging Museum:** CCC-built log camp buildings of 1934–35 — bunkhouse, mess hall, company store, blacksmith shop — with exhibits and artifacts of the lumber boom years, jointly managed by the DNR and the Michigan History Center.
- **The mill ground:** the Enterprise Mill (1940, per the park panel), the steam traction engine (a 1912 Port Huron, per the panel), and the steam-powered sawmill the museum operates during its summer festivals.
- **The Memorial Building:** 1929, log-built, the deed's memorial condition and the park's first public structure.
- **The visitor center:** the "Michigan's Forests" exhibit hall, auditorium, and Friends bookstore.
- **The stump fields:** cutover evidence along the park's ski and mountain-bike trails, interpreted by the park's own panels as the mark of the 1883–1925 logging.
- **The memorial tablet:** the State of Michigan's tablet to Edward E. Hartwick, carrying the gift's own words.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Visitor center first.** The exhibit hall's Ice Age-to-present sequence sets the frame: what this forest was, what the industry did to it, what a remnant means. Check the day's museum hours here — the seasons in §1 are real.
2. **The Old Growth Foot Trail.** Walk the stand before the museum. The paved loop puts the visitor among the 350-year trees with nothing between the eye and the record; the scale of the trunks is the fact every later exhibit refers back to.
3. **The Logging Museum.** A short walk from the visitor center. Read the camp buildings as the workplace that took the rest of the forest down: bunkhouse, mess hall, company store, blacksmith shop — the whole economy of a camp in four log rooms.
4. **The mill ground.** The Enterprise Mill, the Port Huron traction engine, and the sawmill — if the visit lands on a festival day, the sawmill runs, and the sound is part of the record.
5. **The Memorial Building and the tablet.** End with the reason the stand exists: the 1929 building the deed required, and the state's tablet carrying Karen Hartwick's gift in its own words.
6. **The stump fields, on the way out.** The trails beyond the grove cross the cutover. The contrast between the stump fields and the standing 49 acres is the whole entry in one walk.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **The original grove's size does not reconcile.** The marker record's account of Karen Hartwick's purchase gives 80 acres of old-growth white pine in the gift. The Michigan History Center's park historian, describing the deed itself, gives 86 acres of old-growth forest in the original deed gift. An 85-acre figure circulates in secondary compilations. All three print; this entry does not choose. The surviving figure is not in dispute: 49 acres remain (park panel; History Center historian).
- **The park's total acreage does not reconcile.** A DNR press release of June 9, 2015 gives 9,600 acres of forest and hiking trails. Later compilations give 9,335 and 9,672 acres. No single current DNR figure was verified for this entry.
- **The sawmill machinery carries two datings.** The Michigan History Center's museum page describes "a 1914 steam engine and sawmill." The park's own interpretive panel dates the Enterprise Mill to 1940 and identifies the steam traction engine as a 1912 Port Huron. The sources may be describing different machines on the same ground; neither text says so, and the entry does not merge them.
- **The museum's build year is given two ways.** The park panel dates the Logging Museum to 1934–35; the History Center's park historian dates its construction to 1935. Both print.
- **The trees' age is a range in one source and a figure in another.** The park panel says 350 years; the History Center historian has been quoted at 350–400 years.
- **Two addresses stand in the state's own pages.** The DNR park listing gives 3612 State Park Drive, Grayling; the DNR visitor center page gives 2833 Monarch Drive, Grayling, MI 49738. The park also carries two phone numbers in the same split: 989-348-7068 (park listing) and 989-348-2537 (visitor center page).
- **The logging company's name is spelled three ways** across the sources — Salling-Hanson (park panel), Salling Hansen, and Sailing-Hanson. The panel's spelling is used in §2; the variants are noted, not resolved.
- **No National Register record was verified for this entry** in this pass, and no coordinate from a primary source was verified at its source page; §10 leaves both blank rather than borrow them.
