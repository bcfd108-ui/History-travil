## 2. The story

**One-minute hook:** a mining town depended on more than miners. Who washed clothes, prepared food and kept a household functioning while public memory concentrated on the people who became wealthy?

The **Rocky Mountain Irish Roots Collective** publishes a biography of **Rosa Sullivan**, identifying her as Irish-born in 1852. Its account describes work as a laundress at the household's laundry business and additional restaurant work as a waitress and dishwasher. The biography also records domestic abuse and her death in 1892. [2] This is a community-published historical account, not Rosa's own testimony. Its underlying newspaper and record citations are not individually linked in the entry, so the employment details require original-record confirmation before expanded publication.

Keep Rosa's labor in view without making her death a spectacle. No home location, crime reconstruction or asserted private feelings are needed to recognize that her household history belongs in the story of a mining economy.

A named contemporary community voice adds an interpretive frame. In the RMPBS *Colorado Experience* transcript, **Kathleen Fitzsimmons** says: **“In Leadville, our demographic has always been a community of immigrants.”** The program also gives space to **Gloria Perez** discussing family provision and access to basic resources in the contemporary immigrant community. [3] Those are distinct present-day perspectives, not nineteenth-century testimony or proof that every immigrant group had the same experience.

**Original interpretation:** the shift worth making is from a town populated by fortunes to a town sustained by work. A miner's wage and a household's care are connected but not interchangeable. Recovery of a woman's name should open questions about her work and agency, rather than make her another anonymous illustration of hardship.
