## 2. Story

**The dispatcher was a named person.** The Illinois Central’s May 1, 1927 official list identifies “C. G. ANDERSON, Chief Train Dispatcher — Cherokee”. Other entries identify W. E. Ausman as train master for Cherokee, Sioux Falls and Onawa districts, and list separate road supervisors. The station tables carry a distinct dispatch notation and register the Onawa and Sioux Falls branches at Cherokee. The source is a digitized original company directory, read through its searchable transcription. It gives names and functions, but no train-order transcript or evidence a particular person worked a specific shift or desk in the surviving depot. [5]

**Read the building as a set of workspaces.** The station’s nomination puts the depot in a network of rail property. Passenger service demanded tickets, baggage and waiting rooms. Freight movements called for a different kind of accountability. A state-hosted Iowa history article separates station agents, clerks and telegraph employees from train crews. Interpret the building as the interface between the town and a staffed railroad. The archive's photo label also identifies individual workers posing on and around the building in 1903. The listed names include William Shardlow and Dan Christensen. These are caption identifications, without independently read employment cards. [3, 4, 6, 7]

**Another kind of shift saved the depot.** The Depot Renovation account recalls Bill Sauer's local reporting, meetings called by newspaper editor Tom Miller and R. J. Baker, and Mick Samsel's open house. Organizers invited residents to see the condition of the property and raised funds to buy it from the city. The operator puts final payment in January 1992. This voice belongs to a preservation organization recalling the work, distinct from the railroad's 1927 staff roster. [1]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic place.** The Illinois Central Depot and associated rail property at Cherokee. The operator dates construction to 1896 and the end of passenger use to the 1971 Amtrak transition. The separate railroad yard and freight or express facilities are historically relevant, but their access boundaries cannot be taken from the historic district map. [1, 3, 4]

**Lawful public anchor.** The public South Fourth Street sidewalk at the approach to the depot’s civic address, 119 S 4th Street, Cherokee. Drive up to lawful parking and walk the signed public approach only if clear and passable. The operator confirms a railroad museum exists, but usable ordinary walk-in hours were not substantiated. The fall bake sale was a dated event at this address. No reliance on private appointments or event-only doors. [1, 2, 8, 9]

## 5. A short visit

Start at the public sidewalk and look at the exterior work building rather than following its tracks. Read the Cherokee district roster and notice which jobs belonged to town and which covered a wider territory. Land on **A dispatcher with three directions to watch**. Use the source remotely if the view cannot be reached lawfully. [1–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

The staff roster documents Anderson's role and city. Live room assignments, shift, payroll and actual train orders were not located. Photo-caption identifications are distinct from a railroad personnel list. The city/chamber listings, state travel entry and event coverage do not establish the museum's ordinary opening timetable. Regular winter parking and footpath clearance remain unverified. No discrepancy affecting the 1896 building date was established in the sources used here. [1–9]
