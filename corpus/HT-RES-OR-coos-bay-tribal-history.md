## 2. The story
Begin by keeping the names distinct. **CTCLUSI** describes its confederation as three Tribes and four bands: Hanis Coos and Miluk Coos, Lower Umpqua, and Siuslaw. Its own history emphasizes differences in language and history rather than an interchangeable coastal identity. [3] **The Coquille Indian Tribe is a separate sovereign Nation**, not another name for CTCLUSI. Its own heritage publication places its enduring identity in southwestern Oregon's lands and waters. [4] The Siletz confederation is a third named museum collaborator; this dossier does not replace its own history with either of the other Nations' accounts.

CTCLUSI's published history describes an unratified 1855 treaty, forced removal and confinement, continued community organization, and federal termination despite opposition. It records restoration of recognition on **October 17, 1984**, after sustained efforts. [3] These are selected elements of its account, not a complete history or visitor directions to places of suffering. Restoration is not the beginning of the people's existence.

For the Coquille account, read **Brenda Meade's statement, The Meaning of Sovereignty**, published by the Tribe and identifying her as chairperson. She says **“the Coquille people never gave up their sovereign rights.”** She links sovereignty to responsibilities for people, culture and land, and dates renewed federal recognition to **June 28, 1989**. [5] Her statement's office attribution is retained as printed; no independent claim about today's officeholder is needed. The two restoration dates belong to different Nations and must not be combined.

A second named voice ties this history directly to the museum. In **The Voice of CLUSI, November 2022, volume 23, issue 11, printed page 6**, Council member **Enna Helms** explains: **“Our goals around language revitalization are to create speech communities.”** [6] The article is contributed by the Culture and Natural Resources Department. It describes collaboration with the Coquille Indian Tribe and Siletz Nation in developing the language exhibit. This is an attributed statement in the Nation's own publication, not an anonymous museum paraphrase or a new interview.

That gives the visitor a purpose beyond collecting a few vocabulary words. The published aim is a community speaking and transmitting language. Our interpretation: the exhibit's strongest moment is hearing language as present work and future relationship, not as the last trace of a vanished people. Removal and termination deserve reflection, but the story should not end there.

## 3. Then / now
Then: government policies tried to sever recognition, land relationships and cultural transmission. The historical accounts above record survival through those policies. Now: different sovereign Nations speak for themselves and collaborate in public interpretation. That is continuity with change, not a claim that every historic practice or language has remained unaffected.

The museum's own institutional history describes collaboration with the Coquille Indian Tribe and CTCLUSI developing from an earlier basketry-study relationship, and dates opening at its present location to **fall 2015**. [7] The building is a modern public interpretation site, not a surviving ancestral village or community hall.

The comparison here is **who controls the telling and what the visitor hears**, not a historic photograph matched to a shoreline. No present image, exact viewpoint or ancestral location has been aligned. A place-name explanation in an exhibit is knowledge, not permission to travel to that place.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Suggested public-gallery sequence, not a tour booking:
1. **Identify the collaborators.** Read the Nation names before reading the interpretive story. Do not shorten them into one generic “Coos tribe.”
2. **Listen to the public interpretation actually available.** Use designated audio/visual interactives where operational. Do not record audio or assume the museum grants language-teaching or publication rights.
3. **Connect the named sources.** Carry Helms's stated goal and Meade's sovereignty statement into the reading. They belong to different Nations and different publications; neither speaks on behalf of all collaborators.
4. **Pause at loss without making it the whole story.** Reflect on the histories of removal and termination without seeking the affected cultural grounds or turning them into destinations.
5. **End in the present.** The strongest story moment is recognizing the distinction between displaying a word and sustaining a community that speaks it. This is an interpretive conclusion, not a claim that exhibit attendance proves language recovery outcomes.

Onsite reflection question: **Whose voice is speaking here, and what responsibility does listening place on the visitor?** No staff inquiry, interview or outreach was sent.
