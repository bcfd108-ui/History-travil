## 2. The story
**One-minute hook:** A federal date can mark a boundary without marking the beginning of a people. The way a Nation remembers that date is part of the history itself.

**Named Tribal-published testimony:** A **June 6, 2023** article published by the Mescalero Apache Tribe attributes commemorative remarks to **President Edward Martinez**. Within his discussion of survival, he described the three bands as having **“worked diligently to preserve our ancestral homelands, traditions and culture”**. This is a short excerpt from the Tribe's written publication, not a recording independently heard here. His title is retained as of that account, not asserted as his current office. [2]

The article frames commemoration of the 1873 reservation boundaries around resilience and future continuity rather than a founding of the people. It is a primary source for the leadership's public framing in 2023. It is not, by itself, independent corroboration of every nineteenth-century claim within the article. [2]

**Distinct institutional history:** The Tribe's *Our Culture* page identifies **Mescalero, Lipan and Chiricahua** as the three constituent groups within today's Mescalero Apache Tribe. It dates the reservation executive order to **May 29, 1873**, and describes continuing Tribal government. [3] Those identities are retained rather than collapsed into a generic “Apache” label.

This entry does not stand in for the separate history of Bosque Redondo, the White Mountain Apache Tribe, San Carlos Apache Tribe, Jicarilla Apache Nation or other sovereign communities. Neither a shared broad name nor historic movement authorizes one institution to represent all of them. No account of one group's captivity is substituted for another's.

**Interpretive synthesis:** The story is one of authority over public meaning. A museum visitor can read chronology while recognizing that the community decides which histories it makes public and how those histories connect to its present. Learning does not require reaching a sacred place, witnessing a ceremony or asking residents to perform identity for visitors.

## 3. Then / now
Keep three dates conceptually separate: the 1873 executive order, the museum's stated establishment in **1972**, and the dated 2023 commemorative remarks. [1–3] They mark different kinds of institutional history, not stages in a single simplistic story of disappearance and recovery.

The comparison proposed here is between a historical governmental boundary and a contemporary Nation's account of continuity. It is not a reenactment of removal or a matching-photo itinerary. No relocation trail, original camp footprint or sensitive historical location is supplied.

An exhibit can make a history publicly readable while much of community life remains outside the visitor's scope. That distinction is an interpretive result, not a gap to be filled by exploring beyond the museum.
