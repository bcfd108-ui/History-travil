## 2. The story
**The story is not that a people disappeared and their possessions remained. It is that visitors can mistake a museum's historical distance for the absence of living people.** That is this dossier's interpretive argument, not a quotation or a claim of Tribal endorsement.

The state operator explicitly identifies the historical inhabitants as ancestors of contemporary Pueblo peoples and gives an occupation span of AD 825–1225. It describes its educational purpose as including both ancestral and contemporary Native cultures. [1] The museum's interpretation can therefore begin with continuity, rather than a mystery of vanished people. Neither that broad chronology nor a museum label establishes a particular present-day Nation's exclusive relationship to every exhibited item.

A named, current Native-published voice supplies an independent frame. The Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition's July 24, 2026 article reproduces a statement by **Davina Smith-Idjesa (Diné)**, identified there as Coalition co-chair, from an interview with High Country News: **“You cannot erase us. We are still here.”** [4] This is a short excerpt republished by a Tribally constituted organization, not audio heard in this research, not a museum wall quotation, and not evidence that she authored or approved the museum's displays. Her point concerns continuing relationships with the regional landscape. Carry that present tense into the visit without converting it into permission to go onto cultural ground.

Government context matters. The Coalition describes its foundation by the **Hopi Tribe, Navajo Nation, Ute Mountain Ute Tribe, Pueblo of Zuni, and Ute Indian Tribe** in July 2015. Delegates are appointed by their respective Tribes; the organization describes a five-council memorandum that both empowers and limits its activities. These are distinct sovereign governments, not interchangeable names for one community. The Coalition's biography identifies Smith-Idjesa as a Navajo Nation member appointed to represent that Nation's perspectives. [5]

Utah State Parks operates the museum. The Coalition supplies a separately published Native political and cultural perspective. Neither source proves the other body's endorsement. The Coalition is also not interchangeable with the federal Bears Ears Commission: its July article reports the Commission's termination amid monument changes, while describing the Coalition's continuing work. [4] This dossier makes no independent legal determination about current monument boundaries or federal management. Those questions are not needed to enter this state museum.

A museum label can tell you when a pot was made. A Tribal publication can tell you why preserving the knowledge around it matters now. In the Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition’s proposal, Zuni Elder Octavius Seowtewa says, “It provides a link to our ancestors, from long ago.” He is speaking about the cultural record of the region. His statement does not identify the maker of any particular object displayed in Blanding. [10]

On March 22, 2016, the Hopi Tribal Council adopted Resolution H-035-2016 by a recorded vote of thirteen in favor, three opposed and one abstention. The Council supported permanent protection and a decision-making role for the five named Tribes. The tally preserves a deliberation among living people. Read the resolution beside the gallery interpretation without assigning a single shared opinion to every Hopi person or every Pueblo community. [11]

## 3. Then / now
The museum's official exhibit descriptions connect earlier farming, building and material work with later Pueblo migrations and continuing connections to the Four Corners region. They also describe interpretive illustrations of an earlier community. [2] Treat an illustration as an interpretation, not a photograph or a fully verified reconstruction of one person's daily life.

The present-day contrast is institutional: a state museum explains past lives through curated interpretation; a Native-published account insists on living relationships and agency. Reading both produces a better visit than using the first as proof that history ended. This comparison is editorial synthesis, not an asserted historical meeting between Smith-Idjesa and the people described in the exhibits.

No archival/current camera match has been established. No present interior or historical image was visually inspected for alignment. No item-level cultural attribution, provenance or repatriation conclusion is made here.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Proposed indoor interpretation, not an observed room-by-room route; allow roughly 45–60 minutes as an editorial planning allowance, not an operator tour duration.

1. **Before entry:** read the short named voice in section 2. Establish who speaks and for whom; do not turn the statement into a generic voice for all Native peoples.
2. **At the staffed public entrance:** confirm that day's indoor access and photography restrictions. The published visit question is: *Which current interpretation is authored or reviewed by descendant communities, and how is that attribution identified?* This is an optional visitor question, not outreach already performed.
3. **Inside public exhibits:** attend to wording, dates and authorship rather than searching for a prescribed object. Distinguish an archaeological inference, a museum reconstruction and a statement by a named Native person. If attribution is absent, record that as an unanswered question, not proof of exclusion or consent.
4. **End with the strongest story moment:** return to the contrast between past-tense labels and a living person's present-tense assertion. Ask privately: *Did I learn only what outsiders can classify, or also who maintains relationships and responsibility today?* There is no required answer, performance, collection, stamp or score. Leave through the ordinary public museum exit; do not add a cultural-ground excursion.
