# HT-RES-ME-fort-popham — The Half-Moon Fort at the Mouth of the Kennebec

Division ME seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

At Hunnewell Point in Phippsburg, Maine, where the Kennebec River narrows before it meets the sea, stands a half-moon wall of granite thirty feet high: Fort Popham, begun in 1861 to guard the river road to the Bath shipyards and the capital at Augusta, garrisoned in three wars, and never finished. The fort takes its name from George Popham and the English colony of 1607 — but the colony's own fort, St. George, stood on Sabino Head, about half a mile west. The two places share a name and a river mouth, and nothing else: not ground, not dates, not a single stone.

## 1. Trip gate

Fort Popham State Historic Site is operated by the Maine Bureau of Parks and Lands. The Bureau's park page gives the location as 219 Popham Road, fifteen miles from Bath on Maine Route 209: continue past Popham Beach State Park for two miles, and Route 209/Popham Road dead-ends at the parking for the fort.

The Bureau's stated season and hours are: open 9:00 a.m. to sunset daily from April 15th to October 30th, with a fee charged. Visitors may continue to enjoy the park during the off season by parking outside the gate and walking in during the same hours; facilities are closed during the off season. The Bureau posts condition alerts for the park on the page. Listed activities are beaches and fishing, and the listed facilities amount to a picnic area — this is a day-use historic site, not a developed park, and the visit is a walk through and around an unfinished granite fort.

The visitor boundary is the boundary of a masonry ruin kept as a historic site, open to the weather; Fort Baldwin State Historic Site, on the hill behind the fort, is administered alongside it.

## 2. The story

The point was defended long before the granite fort. The Bureau of Parks and Lands states the record plainly: historical records conclude that fortifications, probably wooden, existed here and protected the Kennebec settlements during the Revolutionary War and the War of 1812. The National Register nomination for neighboring Fort Baldwin adds that the Civil War fort rose on the site of an 1811 blockhouse.

The granite fort was commenced in 1861, under the direction of the Chief of the Bureau of Engineers, as Henry E. Dunnack, Maine's State Librarian, recorded in *Maine Forts* (1924). Its name was a deliberate act of memory: on a petition of John A. Poor and Ruel Williams dated November 18, 1861, Secretary of War Simon Cameron determined to associate the new fort with the name of Popham and the history of the Popham Colony. Its purpose, as the state's entrance marker puts it, was to protect the shipbuilding interests in the upriver city of Bath, as well as the state capital in Augusta. The granite came from Dix and Fox Islands, and the builders' leavings are still on the ground: Dunnack wrote that inside the wall enclosure are great blocks of granite strewn around just as the builders left them.

What they were building was a closed lunette — the National Register nomination's term — a half-moon work whose water batteries stood in a thick granite wall some thirty feet in height, with a perimeter of over 500 feet. Captain Henry W. Owen of Bath, stationed at Fort Baldwin during the First World War, left the fullest early description, printed by Dunnack: the wall was casemated for two tiers of guns on the convex, river side; on the flat landward side ran a curtain of lighter construction pierced at its center by the main gate and loopholed for musketry, the gate approached across a moat — spanned by a drawbridge — that severed the point from the mainland. The three faces of the main work held thirty-six guns of ten- and twelve-inch calibre commanding the channel; smaller casemated guns swept the beach and the Atkins Bay side against landing parties, and two more commanded the gate and moat. "The complete armament, therefore, consisted of forty-two cannon." Four powder magazines were built into the work; beneath the fort, almost the entire space is occupied by a great subterranean cistern. The fort was never completed — the nomination gives the reason in one sentence: it was never completed since improvements in fortification were taking place so rapidly. The earth parapet that was to crown the walls was never laid, and the work stopped where it stopped, with dressed stone still lying in the enclosure.

The fort was garrisoned in 1865–66, as both the nomination and Dunnack record. Its Civil War garrison is documented at first hand: the Maine State Library holds the inspection report of the 7th Company, Unassigned Maine Infantry, written by its captain, Augustin Thompson, regarding operations at Fort Popham in 1865. The guns never fired on an enemy here.

The Army returned twice more. In 1898 a battery with an emplacement for one eight-inch converted gun was built behind the caretaker's house, mounted and ready for use in the Spanish-American War; the gun was removed in 1900 and taken to Fort Levett. In the First World War the fort was garrisoned again; modern mine equipment was installed to protect the channel leading to Bath, the river above the fort was mined, and troops quartered nearby used the great cistern for washing and bathing. After the war the mine equipment was removed — the last improvement made by the United States at Fort Popham, in Dunnack's words.

On March 4, 1923, Congress authorized the sale of the old Maine forts, and on January 28, 1924, Maine purchased Fort Popham for $6,600. On October 1, 1969, the fort was listed in the National Register of Historic Places as Fort Popham Memorial. It is administered today by the Bureau of Parks and Lands as Fort Popham State Historic Site.

The name requires its own paragraph, because it is the point on which this entry turns. The fort is named for George Popham, president of the Popham Colony — the Virginia Company of Plymouth's attempt, in 1607, to plant England's first colony in New England. That colony did not stand here. Its fort, St. George, stood on Sabino Head, which the nomination describes as visible from the fort and marked with a tablet, half a mile west. The colony's story, in the nomination's telling from the contemporary accounts: the *Mary and John* and the *Gift of God* sailed from Plymouth on May 31, 1607, under Raleigh Gilbert and George Popham, with one hundred colonists; the settlers built a fort, "trencht and fortified yt with 12 pieces of ordnance," with houses, a church, and a storehouse, and framed "a pretty Pynnace of about some thirty tonne which they called the *Virginia*" — in the nomination's words, the forerunner of the Kennebec shipbuilding tradition the granite fort was later built to defend. The winter of 1607–08 was severe; in December all but forty-five colonists returned to England; in February President Popham died and Gilbert succeeded him; and in October 1608, Gilbert having learned of an inheritance awaiting him in England, they all embarked for home. Until 1890 it was generally thought that Fort Popham's point was the colony's site. Documents found in Spain, including John Hunt's 1607 draught of St. George's fort, established the true site on Sabino Head, and excavations led by Jeffrey Brain from 1994 have since confirmed it on the ground. The colony lasted fourteen months, built nothing of stone, and left nothing at Hunnewell Point.

## 3. Then / now

Then, the point was a construction yard that became a guard post: casemates rising in two tiers, a moat cutting the point off from the mainland, and two barracks standing within the inner grounds. A ship bound up the Kennebec — for the Bath yards or the capital — had to slow against the current and pass within point-blank reach of thirty-six heavy guns. In the First World War the channel above the fort was sown with mines controlled from the shore.

Now, the walls stand at the height the builders left them, roofless and gunless. The nomination records that the fort's guns, which once consisted of forty-two cannon, have all been sent to various places for historical purposes; the two barracks no longer exist; the mine equipment and the 1898 gun are long gone. What a visitor walks through is the granite armature itself — casemates, stair towers, magazines, cistern — held as a state historic site at the end of a dead-end road, with river traffic passing the point unchallenged. Half a mile west, Sabino Head carries the 1907 marker on the colony site, and the hill above it the concealed batteries of Fort Baldwin, built 1905–1912. The river mouth keeps all three works in sight of one another, and they are three different stories.

## 4. What's still there

The main work stands substantially as the nomination described it in 1969: the half-moon granite river front, some thirty feet high and over 500 feet around, casemated in two tiers, with the parade inside the arc and a spiral staircase at each end. Owen's 1924 account adds what a walk confirms: the four powder magazines with their grated doors still in position, the two spiral stairs of granite blocks set in lead, and the great cistern beneath the fort.

The landward side keeps its lighter curtain wall, loopholed for musketry, with the main gate at its center and the line of the moat that severed the point. Inside the enclosure, the unused granite blocks Dunnack described in 1924 still lie where the work stopped. The state's interpretive markers stand in and around the fort: the entrance marker erected by the Friends of Phippsburg and the Department of Conservation, and, on a lower casemate wall inside, the Department's "Fort Popham: Guardian of the Kennebec," which also records the separated Rodman battery whose remnants it says still exist.

What is gone is as definite. Both barracks no longer exist. Every gun is gone — the forty-two cannon dispersed for historical purposes, the 1898 eight-inch gun removed to Fort Levett in 1900, the mine plant taken out after the First World War. The earth parapet planned for the wall tops was never built at all. The fort is a finished fragment, empty of nearly everything meant to fill it.
## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression

The road shapes the visit: Route 209 runs fifteen miles from Bath, passes Popham Beach State Park, and ends at the fort's parking. Begin outside the work, at the entrance marker on the shore path south of the fort. Then walk the landward side first — the loopholed curtain, the central gate, the moat line — because the fort reads as a closed work, sealed against attack from the rear, not just a wall facing the river.

Pass through the gate to the parade. At ground level: the unused granite blocks in the grass, the well-like openings into the cistern underfoot, the ground where the two barracks stood. Then take the lower casemates along the river front in sequence, and find the "Guardian of the Kennebec" marker on the lower casemate wall. Climb one of the spiral stairs to the upper tier, and finish on top, where the nomination's promise holds: the top of the structure commands a sweeping view up the Kennebec in one direction and seaward in another.

From the top, place the other two stories before leaving. West, about half a mile, is Sabino Head — the colony ground of 1607, marked in 1907, where nothing the colonists built was ever stone. Behind and above is Sabino Hill, where Fort Baldwin's hidden batteries took over this fort's job in the twentieth century — a separate state historic site on the same road. Close at the shore, facing the channel where a Civil War warship would have had to slow and run the wall.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

The year construction began is printed two ways by the fort's own custodians. The National Register nomination states that construction on Fort Popham was started in 1861, the Register record carries 1861 as the significant year, Dunnack agrees, and the entrance marker reads "since construction commenced in 1861." Against these, the Bureau of Parks and Lands' current park page states that construction began in 1862, and the Department of Conservation's own "Guardian of the Kennebec" marker inside the fort calls it a fort "built by the U.S. War Department beginning in 1862." This dossier prints 1861 in §2 following the Register record; the disagreement is not resolved.

The designed armament is likewise two numbers. The nomination and Owen's description agree on forty-two cannon as the complete armament — Owen by arithmetic: thirty-six heavy guns in the three faces, four smaller guns in the south flank, two in the north flank, and two commanding the gate. The "Guardian of the Kennebec" marker states the fort "was designed to house a huge armament of 28 cannons" — fourteen 8-inch Rodmans, ten 100-pounder Parrott rifles, and four 20-pounder flank howitzers — with five additional 15-inch Rodmans in the separated platform.

The Civil War garrison's dates are narrower in the early records than in later retelling. The nomination and Dunnack both date the garrison to 1865–66, and Captain Thompson's 1865 inspection report documents his 7th Company of Unassigned Maine Infantry at the fort in that year. The more precise span often printed for the company — October 1864 to July 1865 — was not verified in the sources fetched for this dossier, and §2 does not rely on it. Captain Thompson's later fame as the inventor of Moxie is likewise widely repeated but rests on sources outside the primary record fetched here, and is not asserted.

No primary record fetched for this dossier states the year construction stopped. The frequently repeated date of 1869 appears in later park-history retellings but in neither the nomination nor Dunnack, and it is printed nowhere in §2.

The ground itself is located slightly differently by the two state-era records. The 1969 nomination fixes the property by a coordinate rectangle — 43° 45′ 19″–20″ N, 69° 47′ 3″–4″ W — while the Bureau's current park page carries the point printed in §10, a short distance east of the nomination's rectangle. The acreage also differs by era: Dunnack's 1924 figures describe the federal reservation as purchased; the Bureau's current page states no acreage.

Two smaller operator disagreements: the Bureau's park page says a fee is charged, but the Bureau's day-use fee schedule carries no Fort Popham line; and the park page gives the season as April 15 to October 30, while an older Bureau mini-guide gives Memorial Day to Labor Day. §1 follows the current park page.

Finally, the 1969 nomination carries one claim not repeated here as fact: that a boulder west of the fort bears markings read as a Viking inscription of the year 1018. It offers no evidence, and no later record fetched here supports it.

## 7. Photo record

**First photograph:** Doug Kerr's photograph of Fort Popham, taken from the landward side of the point looking along the fort. The loopholed granite curtain wall fills the right foreground, its coursed blocks weathered, weeds growing from the joints, and its upper courses stepping down in an unfinished line where the work stopped; driftwood lies at its base. In the foreground lie dressed granite blocks the builders never set — the leavings Dunnack described in 1924, still on the ground. Across the grass parade at the left, the casemated river front curves away, its two tiers of arched gun openings visible, with a flagpole before it flying the United States flag and the Maine state flag. The Kennebec and a wooded shore close the left edge of the frame. The frame shows the fort as it stands: original 1860s granite, unfinished at the top, nothing reconstructed.
