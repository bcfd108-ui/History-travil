# Madame John's Legacy — the last French colonial raised cottage of the Vieux Carré
HT-RES-LA-madame-johns-legacy · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

The name is a fiction, and the house is real. Madame John's Legacy takes its name from a New Orleans writer's invention: George Washington Cable's 1874 short story "'Tite Poulette," in which a character, Monsieur John, bequeaths a Dumaine Street house to his mistress, known as Madame John. The story's bequest is the legacy of the title. Nothing in the documentary record connects Cable's plot to the actual owners of 632 Dumaine Street. The building earned its National Historic Landmark designation on its architecture, not on its name — and the distinction matters, because the real story of the lot is older and stranger than the story that named it. [1, 4]

The lot is documented back to the first French settlement. Le Blond de la Tour's 1722 map shows the property as part of a large lot running to the corner of Dumaine and Royal Streets, and Ignace François Broutin's 1728 survey names sailing master Jean Pascal of Provence as its owner. Pascal died in the Natchez Indian massacre of 1729, and the property passed to his widow, Elizabeth Réal, who soon married her neighbor, ship captain François Marin. The couple ran an inn on the site, close to the river. A 1731 plan shows a building with side pavilions on the lot, similar in outline to the present house but without its front and rear galleries, and set closer to Royal Street than the house stands today. Widow Marin stayed on Dumaine Street for decades after her husband's death in 1740, divided the lot in 1776, and died in 1777, after which her grandson sold the remainder, then described as an "old house and a kitchen thereon." [4]

The house's eighteenth-century chain runs through legend and contract alike, and the two do not agree. In 1778 the purchaser was reputed pirate René (or Renaldo) Beluche, whom legend says hid stolen treasure in the house's depths; during his ownership he transferred eighteen feet of Dumaine Street frontage toward Royal Street to his father-in-law, a division that, measured against the 1731 map, would have cut through the house itself. The architect-historian Samuel Wilson Jr. assumed from this that Beluche either moved the circa 1730 house toward the river or built a new one, and noted that when the house was sold in 1783 it was no longer described as "old." Archaeologist Shannon Dawdy, who investigated the property extensively in 1998, questioned the absolute accuracy of the early map and conjectured instead that the circa 1730 dwelling stood on the portion of the lot Beluche retained, and that he substantially remodeled the "old house" before selling it to Captain Manuel de Lanzos in 1783. The two readings — moved or rebuilt, versus remodeled in place — remain in the record side by side, and the 1998 excavation did not settle them: it found a 1788 fire trash pit whose contents indicated the early eighteenth-century building had been damaged but not destroyed. [4]

The 1788 fire is what the standing house answers. Maps of the damage show 632 Dumaine Street on the fringe of the area destroyed by the Great New Orleans Fire of 1788, which burned roughly eighty percent of the city. Within days of the fire, Captain Manuel de Lanzos of the Spanish Fixed Regiment entered into a building contract with Robert Jones, an American master carpenter, for a house of six rooms with cabinets and a kitchen in the courtyard, with de Lanzos obliging himself to provide the brick and "all the ironwork which he has salvaged from his burned houses." The contract dates the work to 1788, and the National Register record gives 1788 as the significant year — but whether the result was a wholly new house or a reconstruction of the damaged earlier one, reusing its salvaged walls and ironwork, is not settled in the record. What is certain is the form it took, and the form is anachronistic: instead of the solid masonry, arcaded, stuccoed Spanish colonial style going up all around it — the Merieult House at 525–533 Royal, 722 Toulouse, the reconstructed Pontalba residence — de Lanzos's house looked back to the early French colonial days. It is a raised cottage: a solid brick basement, brick-between-posts construction above, wide galleries, and a steep double-pitched hipped roof, ventilated for subtropical heat and raised against the frequent flooding of delta ground. It is, in the state encyclopedia's words, the sole survivor in the Vieux Carré of a building form that emerged in the French colonial period (1721–1763) in response to local conditions — and the fact that it survived the second great fire, of 1794, makes it the oldest French colonial house form left in the Quarter. The National Register record gives the period of significance as 1750–1799 and the architectural style as colonial; the museum calls it one of the best examples of French colonial architecture in North America. [1, 2, 4]

The people who lived there after de Lanzos — he died in 1813 — show the city's upper classes crossing languages and empires. From 1813 to his bankruptcy in 1820, the Belgian-born lawyer Dominique Seghers and his wife lived and entertained lavishly in the cottage; the probate inventory made after her death in 1819 records the furnishing, and during their years the couple acquired the adjacent river-side property for a *parterre* garden in the French mode and added an open gallery, now gone, to overlook the view. The widow and family of upriver sugar planter Jacques Étienne Roman used the property as their city home between 1820 and 1836 — her son built the Oak Alley plantation house — and Madame Roman contracted for the extant detached two-story dependency at the rear. The next long-term owner was William C. C. Claiborne (1847–1896), son of Louisiana's first American governor, who married into a French Creole family and adopted its mores. The complex as the visitor sees it from the sidewalk today — main house, a kitchen with cook's quarters, and the two-story dependency — is the accumulation of those households, and behind the main building stands the rear dependency the National Register photo forms of 1975 call the "garçonnière or slave quarters," a reminder that the house's domestic life rested on enslaved labor as well as on the families named in the record. [1, 4, 5]

The Quarter's decline remade the house in turn. As the neighborhood became a slum of new immigrants in the late nineteenth century, the cottage passed from 1895 to 1925 to the Canepa family, Italian immigrants, and was parceled into tenement apartments. The revival of interest in the déclassé neighborhood among intellectuals and artists brought Stella Hirsch Lemann to acquire it in 1925 and establish a small art colony inside its walls; in 1947 she donated the landmark to the Louisiana State Museum. The museum operated it as a museum until 1965, when Hurricane Betsy struck New Orleans and the house was declared unsafe for visitors. Its architectural significance — National Register listing and National Historic Landmark designation both dated April 15, 1970 — drove the restoration that followed: a coalition of the Vieux Carré Commission and the Louisiana Council for the Vieux Carré lobbied successfully for state and federal funding, and the structure was restored in the early 1970s (the nomination record says work began in 1972 and was completed in the fall of 1974, under the direction of New Orleans architect F. Monroe Labouisse Jr.; the state encyclopedia gives the restoration as 1971–72) and reopened as a museum. In the 1980s it underwent a controversial paint analysis whose results may still be seen in its monochromatic color scheme. The house also passed through the camera: Lafcadio Hearn described it in 1883 as "the very peculiar house, half brick, half timber," artist William Woodward painted it in 1910, and parts of the films *Interview with the Vampire* and *12 Years a Slave* were filmed there. [2, 3, 4, 7]

The museum has been closed for at least nine years. The state's own page says only that it is closed for historic restoration, with major preservation funding secured by Lieutenant Governor Billy Nungesser and the Department of Culture, Recreation and Tourism from the State Office of Facility Planning and Control. The public record beyond the museum's page is a dispute, and this dossier reports it as a dispute rather than deciding it. On August 10, 2026, two Louisiana State Museum board members who also run the Friends of Madame John's Legacy — Kim Abramson and Kevin Kelly — visited the building and found wooden beams chopped into blocks and stacked against a wall, a shutter covered in sawdust, and painted stair pieces cut apart. State Museum Director Rebecca Mackie said the cut wood was material that could not be reused, that nothing was thrown away, and that removed items were relocated to collections storage at 1000 Chartres; architect Perrin Ehlinger, who recently worked on repairs, reviewed photographs and said the staff had cut only rotted wood and components of a 1970s staircase. Abramson and Kelly say they had been in talks with state officials about storing components in warehouses Kelly owns and were blindsided; other preservationists have split over the episode, and the director of the state's Division of Historic Preservation did not return press inquiries asking whether her office's guidelines were followed. The building is state-owned and therefore, in the Vieux Carré Commission executive director's words, outside the Commission's jurisdiction; the Commission will offer only non-binding recommendations. In the spring of 2026 the Legislature allocated $900,000 to the Friends nonprofit and $325,000 to Louisiana State Museums for repairs. A Historic Structures Report commissioned by the museum board in March 2023 has not yet been completed. No reopening date has been published. [1, 7, 8]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** 632 Dumaine Street, on the southwest side of the street between Royal and Chartres Streets, one block north of Jackson Square, in the French Quarter of New Orleans, Orleans Parish. The lot has been occupied continuously since the 1720s; the standing house is the 1788 de Lanzos construction, possibly incorporating fabric of the circa 1730 Marin house. [2, 4]

**Lawful physical anchor.** The public sidewalk on Dumaine Street, reading the street facade only. The museum is closed for restoration; the courtyard and interior are not open ground. Do not enter the property, do not approach the work site, and do not treat the restoration fencing or any open gate as an invitation. [1, 7]

## 5. Public progression

Stand on the Dumaine Street sidewalk opposite 632 and read the house as a survivor, not a postcard. Start at the raised brick basement — the building's first sentence, flood logic made visible — then the timber upper story and the steep hipped roof that mark it as French colonial rather than the Spanish colonial of its neighbors. Compare it, from the same sidewalk, with the solid masonry, arcaded facades down Royal and Chartres: this house is the older language, preserved because it happened to stand on the fringe of the 1788 fire's devastation and survived the 1794 fire as well. **Strongest story moment · The contract of 1788.** Within days of losing his house to fire, Manuel de Lanzos contracted to rebuild with salvaged brick and ironwork from his burned houses — a house made, in part, of the house that burned. The name it carries is fiction; the salvage is fact. End the visit at Jackson Square, one block south, where the Cabildo and the Presbytère — the Spanish-era rebuilding of the same fires — stand as the other half of the story. [1, 2, 4]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- **Built 1788, or rebuilt from a survivor?** The building contract of 1788, the National Register record (significant year 1788) and the museum page all date the house to 1788. Dawdy's 1998 excavation showed the circa 1730 building was damaged but not destroyed in the fire, and the anachronistic French colonial form suggests reused walls and material. Whether the standing house is a new 1788 build or a reconstruction of the earlier one is unresolved in the sources inspected, and this dossier prints both readings. [2, 4]
- **The Beluche transfer.** The state encyclopedia records that Beluche transferred eighteen feet of Dumaine frontage to his father-in-law, a division that would have cut through the house on the 1731 map. Wilson's assumption (the house was moved or rebuilt) and Dawdy's conjecture (the map is imprecise and the house was remodeled in place) disagree, and neither is corroborated by a deed or survey in the sources inspected. [4]
- **Second fire year.** The second great fire is dated 1794 in the National Register-era histories and in most accounts of the Quarter; the 64 Parishes entry once prints "the great fire of 1795." This dossier uses 1794 and flags the 1795 printing as an apparent slip in that entry. [4]
- **Restoration dates.** The National Register nomination record states restoration work began in 1972 and was completed in the fall of 1974, funded with $145,000 from the Louisiana legislature, $72,000 from state museum admission funds and $193,000 from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development. The 64 Parishes entry dates the restoration to 1971–72 under F. Monroe Labouisse Jr. Both are printed; no source reconciling them was found in this pass. [3, 4]
- **Current condition and reopening.** The museum page gives no reopening date and no condition report. The August 2026 dispute over cut material is reported in the press as a live disagreement between the museum director and two board members; the state Division of Historic Preservation has not publicly verified whether its guidelines were followed. The 2023 Historic Structures Report is not yet complete. Nothing in this dossier predicts a reopening date. [1, 7, 8]
- **The name.** "Madame John's Legacy" derives from Cable's 1874 fiction and refers to no documented owner of the lot. The house is sometimes written "Madam John's Legacy" in the National Register file itself (the 1970 nomination forms use "Madam"), a spelling variant printed in the record. [3, 5]
- The "slave quarters" identification of the rear dependency comes from the 1975 National Register photo forms' caption ("garconnière or slaves quarters"). No separate research into the identities or lives of the enslaved people who worked at the property was retrieved in this pass; the building is named for what it was, and the gap is printed. [5]
