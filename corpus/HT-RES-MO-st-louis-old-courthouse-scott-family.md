# Old Courthouse — Two petitions for freedom

Missouri OP01 · Draft for independent Dash review

## 2. Story

**Harriet Scott filed in her own name.** On April 6, 1846, her petition asked Judge John M. Krum to let her bring suit against Irene Emerson. It described being brought from Virginia, taken to Fort Snelling, taken to Fort Jessup and returned to Fort Snelling before being brought to St. Louis. In the archive’s transcription she was “entitled to freedom.” The record shows her mark on the petition and again in the sworn statement. This is the language of a legal pleading and the mark of the petitioner. The person who put the words on paper is a separate question. [3]

**Dred Scott’s petition traced a different itinerary.** His filing of the same day named residence in Illinois as well as at Fort Snelling. It later placed him at Jefferson Barracks, hired to Captain Bainbridge. The signed pleading as transcribed says “he is entitled to his freedom.” Read the two filings side by side. The particular places and movements were material to each claim and cannot be combined into a single trip by treating the Scotts as one litigant. [4, 5]

**A ruling in one case could bind the other.** Both filed again in July 1847. A written stipulation filed February 12, 1850 tied Harriet’s judgment to the outcome in Dred’s case. A later clerk’s note records the decision for the defendant and its effect under the agreement. The local jury found for Dred in 1850. The Missouri Supreme Court reversed in 1852, and the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against him in 1857 in the later federal suit. The state case and the federal case had different defendants. The renewed filings and the agreement are what connect Harriet’s independent legal act to the better-known federal case. [5–7, 16–18]

## 3. Then and now

The historic location is the St. Louis Circuit Court’s Old Courthouse block between Fourth and Broadway and Market and Chestnut. NPS dates the public opening to 1845. The distinctive dome a visitor sees now dates to 1862, after the initial petitions and trials. Look for the change from a courthouse under construction during the Scotts’ legal action to a completed nineteenth-century civic building, then a memorial and museum. [1, 8–10]

The lawful physical anchor is the **Old Courthouse public entrance and Scott exhibits**, 11 North Fourth Street. Approach on the signed public sidewalks and use a posted entrance. This is a short walk from the public street. The restored courtrooms are current interpretation. Exact petition filing rooms and trial-room fabric were not attributed to a particular exhibit room. [1, 2, 8]

## 5. A short visit

Start on the public Fourth Street approach. Notice the dome, then enter if the building is open. Read the Scott interpretation and the short excerpts from both separate petitions. Finish at **Two marks on two petitions**. Pause with the fact that Harriet had to seek freedom in her own name and that the legal process eventually tied her case to Dred’s. If the building is closed or sidewalks are unsafe, read the two records remotely. [1–8]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

The State Archives introductory paragraph broadly groups the free-residence arguments together, naming Rock Island and Fort Snelling. The petitions distribute the places differently. Harriet’s own filing names Fort Snelling and Fort Jessup, while Dred’s names Illinois and Fort Snelling. NPS’s landscape summary says “the Scotts” lost and later won at circuit level. The record of separate filings and the signed stipulation and clerk’s note are more precise for each person's procedural history. “Opened 1845” and “completed 1862” describe different construction milestones. [3–8, 16–17]
