# HT-RES-AK-skagway-1915 — Skagway in 1915: the Klondike gateway town in a panorama
Division AK seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 25, 2026

In 1897 Skagway went from a homestead at the mouth of the Skagway River to a boom town of several thousand stampeders almost overnight. The 1915 panorama at this entry's head shows what the rush left behind: a mature railroad town on a regular street grid, wooden commercial buildings along the waterfront, under the coastal mountains. Much of that grid survives inside Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park.

## 1. Trip gate
The historic core of Skagway is open year-round on public streets (Broadway and adjacent blocks). Much of the downtown historic district lies within Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park. Visitors walk the boardwalks and streets freely; some buildings are privately owned or seasonal. Confirm current NPS and municipal conditions.

## 2. The story
The Tlingit name for the area is often given as Skagua ("place where the north wind blows"). In 1887 Captain William Moore and his son Ben claimed a homestead at the mouth of the Skagway River, anticipating a route to the interior. When gold was discovered on Bonanza Creek in the Klondike in August 1896, word reached the outside world in 1897. Steamships began landing thousands of stampeders at Skagway and nearby Dyea. Within months the homestead became a tent-and-frame boom town of several thousand people, complete with saloons, outfitters, and the presence of confidence man Jefferson "Soapy" Smith until his death in a July 1898 shootout.

The White Pass & Yukon Route railway, completed to the Yukon in 1900, cemented Skagway's role as the principal gateway. Population fell sharply after the rush peaked; by 1910 it was under 1,000. The 1915 panorama captures the mature railroad town: a regular street grid, wooden commercial buildings, and the waterfront under the coastal mountains. Tourism, already present in 1898, became the town's mainstay in the twentieth century. Today the historic district is preserved within Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park.

## 3. Then / now
- 1887: Moore homestead claim.
- 1896–97: Klondike discovery; first large arrivals.
- 1898: peak boom, Soapy Smith killed.
- 1900: railway completed; city incorporated.
- 1910: population under 1,000.
- 1915: date of the panorama.
- 1976 onward: Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park established.
- Today: restored false-front streetscape, NPS visitor center, cruise-ship port.

## 4. What's still there
Many late-1890s and early-1900s wooden commercial and residential buildings remain, some restored, some still in use. The Arctic Brotherhood Hall, railroad depot area, and boardwalks are visible. The surrounding mountains and Lynn Canal setting match the backdrop of the 1915 view.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Arrive by road, ferry, or cruise ship. Begin at the Klondike Gold Rush National Historical Park visitor center for orientation. Walk Broadway and the side streets, comparing the present street grid and building footprints to the 1915 panorama. Interpretive signs and the restored Moore Cabin area mark early settlement. The waterfront and railroad corridor remain accessible.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
