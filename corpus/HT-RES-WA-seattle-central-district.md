## 2. The story
A museum is often presented as a finished civic gift. Here, the struggle to make Black history visible is part of the history itself.

NAAM's institutional timeline begins with a 1981 proposal and a 1984 task force. It names **Earl Debnam, Michael Greenwood, Charlie James and Omari Tahir Garrett** as the community activists who occupied the closed Colman School in 1985 to press for a museum. The timeline records the 2003 purchase, later museum leadership and opening on **March 8, 2008**. This is the institution's account of its own development, not proof that every participant agreed on the outcome. [4]

**Named primary voice:** In Lynne K. Varner's February 17, 1993 *Seattle Post-Intelligencer* report, Debnam spoke during the still-unresolved museum effort: **“We're confident this type of self-help attitude will attract a lot of support.”** The reporter connected that statement to a planned community fundraising enterprise. The report also records Debnam's frustration with official progress and the competing explanations offered by public officials. This is a contemporary reported statement, not a later invented first-person scene. [5]

The archived copy is a later printout of the newspaper's web archive, preserved by the African American Heritage Museum and Cultural Center website. Its article date and byline were read from the PDF; the original 1993 newsprint page and interview recording were not inspected. Debnam's confidence should not be converted into proof that the proposed fundraising venture operated or achieved its target. [5]

The interpretive point is authorship: community members sought the power to preserve and present their own history. A visitor should encounter creative work and daily community life, not reduce the Central District to either celebrated musicians or episodes of oppression.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** Colman was a school serving a changing neighborhood. The City's designation report traces freeway-related property acquisition and neighborhood disruption. It distinguishes closure as an elementary school in 1979 from subsequent use by Summit K–12 and the later closure. The building was not simply an empty shell waiting for a museum. [6]

**Now:** NAAM's history records a museum alongside affordable housing above it. The current visitor page explicitly protects resident parking. Museum admission therefore cannot be interpreted as residential access. [1,4]

Nearby Jimi Hendrix Park is a distinct commemorative public landscape. Its presence does not turn the school into Hendrix's childhood home or establish that any specific nearby residence is open to visitors. The park's current public interpretation can stand on its own without seeking homes or private music venues. [2–3]

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation, not a route or booked tour:
1. Check museum opening and admission; use its authorized entrance rather than a residential door.
2. Establish that Black community members worked to create this place of interpretation. Read the four occupiers' names without pretending they held one permanent, unanimous position.
3. Encounter currently available museum work on its own terms. No specific artwork or object is guaranteed by this dossier.
4. Read Debnam's 1993 statement with its date and publication context. Consider the difference between demanding an institution and determining what it becomes.
5. **Strongest ending:** Leave with the question of who gets to tell a community's history, while recognizing that the community is still present and creating, not only preserved in exhibits.

If using the adjacent public park, treat its interpretation as a separate optional reading setting, not a historical occupation reenactment. If the museum is closed, park access does not authorize entry; use the published history remotely.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- **Occupation start:** NAAM gives 1985. A 4Culture report specifies November 25, whereas other published accounts use November 24. Day-level precision is held open; this draft uses the year. [4,7]
- **Closure:** School-use changes and final vacancy are separate events, not necessarily conflicting dates. [6]
- **Competing institutional visions:** NAAM's own 2020 staff statement acknowledges a split and that the occupiers' full vision was not realized. That statement is dated institutional perspective, not an adjudication of ownership or present disputes. [8]
- **Voice limitation:** The 1993 quote is reported contemporaneously but mediated by a journalist and later archive printout. No interview with Debnam conducted. [5]
- **Current access:** Museum 2026 hours verified independently from park hours; actual closures, admission changes and accessible circulation still require checking. [1–3]
- **Regional scope:** This is a focused entry on Black institution-building, not a comprehensive Central District history or a repeat of the 1919 General Strike dossier.
- **Indigenous history:** No Tribe-published site history researched here; Black institutional history does not erase prior and continuing Indigenous presence.
