## 2. The story
**A museum may put material culture in the past tense. A government speaking for itself is evidence that public history also belongs to the present.** This is the interpretive thread of the dossier, not an assertion that every museum label makes that mistake.

A directly Tribal-published, named voice is available from **Shaun Chapoose**, identified as Ute Tribal Business Committee chairman in the Tribe's November 4, 2026 news release. His statement says: **“The Tribe should not have to continuously fight the County to protect our sovereignty over our own lands.”** [4] The release concerns a disputed October encounter between Tribal/federal officers and county authorities. The quoted view is the Tribe's own published account, not a neutral adjudication or this dossier's finding of legal liability. No checkpoint location, access route or operational detail is retained.

That source is valuable here because it makes the central category clear: the Ute Indian Tribe is a governing polity, not simply the historical subject of a county exhibition. The museum and the Tribe are separate institutions. A visitor should not treat a county narrative as a substitute for the Tribe's account, nor assume that a disagreement involving county authorities establishes anything about a particular museum employee or label.

The Tribe's own Business Committee page names **Uintah, Uncompahgre and Whiteriver** as its three bands. It describes elected representation and identifies Chapoose as an Uncompahgre representative. [5] These bands belong within the Ute Indian Tribe of the Uintah and Ouray Reservation. They must not be recast as interchangeable names for the separate Ute Mountain Ute Tribe or Southern Ute Indian Tribe. This dossier does not infer an individual's band from a surname.

A second historical primary voice shows the importance of that distinction over time. In his January 9, 2018 written congressional testimony, published by the Bears Ears Inter-Tribal Coalition, Chapoose objects to five sovereign Tribal governments sharing one hearing seat. His introductory argument stresses that each has its own relationship with the United States. [6] This is a dated, authored government-facing document, not a generic quotation about all Indigenous peoples. Its subject is Bears Ears, not the museum or a particular Uintah Basin site. It provides a framework for attentive reading; it does not move this visit into southern Utah.

The resulting story is a change in the visitor's perspective: from asking what remains of a historical people to asking who speaks, governs and interprets now. That shift can happen entirely inside an authorized museum visit and in public reading. It requires neither access to a living community nor disclosure of restricted knowledge.

## 3. Then / now
The museum's stated mission covers the Uinta Basin from its earliest peoples to the present. Its First Peoples exhibit is an operator-confirmed interpretation offering, but the public homepage does not supply a full label transcript, named Tribal authorship or consultation record. [1] Those remain questions, not grounds to presume either endorsement or exclusion.

Compare the 2018 testimony with the 2026 Tribal release as two dated acts of self-representation. [6,4] They are not interchangeable disputes, and the first does not adjudicate the second. Both permit a visitor to recognize political agency alongside whatever material history the museum interprets.

No ceremonial narrative, ancestral-site imagery, burial information or cultural-ground locator is reproduced. No historical photograph has been aligned with a current view. This is documentary comparison, not a reconstructed event or a claim that the named speaker appears in the museum.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Proposed 40–60-minute public-museum reflection sequence; this duration is editorial, not an operator tour length.

1. **Read before entering.** Establish the full Nation name and three-band distinction. Read the short, explicitly dated Tribal quotation as a present-day statement, not as a script for reenactment.
2. **Confirm the public gate.** Enter only during current opening hours and ask about exhibit access and photography rules. No after-hours or special collection access is assumed.
3. **Read interpretation critically and fairly.** If public labels identify particular peoples, authors, dates or uncertainties, attend to those distinctions. Do not treat silence on a webpage as proof that the actual exhibition lacks Native voices.
4. **Optional onsite question:** *Which parts of the First Peoples interpretation were written or reviewed by the Ute Indian Tribe or other named Native communities, and where is that attribution recorded?* This is a proposed visitor question, not outreach already conducted or a demand for confidential consultation records.
5. **End with the strongest story moment.** Hold the museum's historical narrative beside a named Tribal official speaking for a living government. Ask privately what changes when the subject is recognized as a present-day authority rather than only an object of study. There is no score, stamp, scavenger hunt or required answer.

No continuation to a cultural location, community event or private residence. If the museum is unavailable, the sequence remains a source-reading exercise.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- The current winter schedule and holiday exceptions are directly operator-published, but real-time closure, weather and equipment status were not checked onsite. [1–3]
- An external county-library branch listing displays 9–6 weekday hours, unlike the operator's winter schedule. Do not use that broader listing to extend a winter visit. [8]
- The November 2026 Tribal release is a primary account of a contested event, not an independently verified court disposition. No legal advice or route-right determination follows from it. [4]
- The 2018 testimony describes offices and institutions at that date. It is not evidence of present commission membership, monument boundaries or current law. [6]
- The Tribe's homepage and dated November release give different membership totals. This dossier avoids adopting either as a reconciled population figure. [4,9]
- Exact exhibit text, collection provenance, Tribal review process and photography policy were not retrieved. The museum's public invitation establishes a visitor gate, not cultural authority over every represented history.
- No comprehensive history of removal, treaties, allotment or schooling is attempted from these few sources. A more complete account would require further Nation-specific primary material rather than combining unrelated Ute histories.
