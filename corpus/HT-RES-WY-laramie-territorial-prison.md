# HT-RES-WY-laramie-territorial-prison — Who made the ordinary things?
Division WY OP03 • Local research draft • Checked November 19, 2026 UTC • REFLECTION ONLY

## 1. Trip gate
**Wyoming Territorial Prison State Historic Site, 975 Snowy Range Road, Laramie, WY 82070.** Current operator status confirms October–April admission Thursday–Saturday, 10 a.m.–3 p.m.; last ticket is sold one hour before closing, therefore **2 p.m.** Closed Thanksgiving Day, Christmas Day and New Year's Day. Adults/seniors $9; ages 12–17 $4.50; eleven and under free. [1]

**Interior gate:** self-guided visits are available during open hours. Guided tours are normally seasonal, late May–early September, not promised for winter. The operator warns that the prison interior can be colder than outside in winter. Do not treat this as a warm indoor refuge. Recheck exceptional closures, surface conditions and room availability before departure. [1]

This is the historic Laramie site, **not** the Wyoming Frontier Prison in Rawlins or a functioning correctional facility. A current opening schedule is not permission to enter closed rooms, manipulate equipment or explore behind barriers. No tour, admission purchase or contact was made.

## 2. The story
A broom is ordinary enough to hide the labor that made it. That is the interpretive starting point here, rather than a celebrity outlaw.

In **Matt Joyce's January 4, 2009 Associated Press report**, site curator **Teresa Sherwood** discussed prison-made broom labels that omitted the prison connection. Her explanation included **“supporting prison labor.”** This is a named curator's published interpretation of material evidence, not a prisoner's voice or proof of what every customer knew. The report concerns a restoration-era exhibit; it does not prove today's display or demonstration is unchanged. [2]

Labor was not incidental to the institution. A **University of Wyoming American Heritage Center** public-history segment, drawing on the **Lawrence A. Cardoso papers**, describes first warden **N. K. Boswell** arranging for incarcerated people to work at a local brickyard. The segment is an archival interpretation, not the underlying labor contract; that contract was not retrieved here. [3]

The **Alliance for Historic Wyoming** explains the Prison Industries Building in terms of revenue, management of incarcerated people and a claimed rehabilitative purpose. These purposes should not be collapsed into proof that the work was voluntary, beneficial or experienced uniformly. [4]

A second kind of record reveals how buildings can obscure their makers. Historical architect **Clayton Fraser's May 24, 1977 National Register nomination** identifies the warden's residence as built in 1875 with incarcerated labor. That record documents institutional fabric and its preservation assessment; it cannot tell us how a particular worker experienced the work. [5]

**Interpretive conclusion:** the useful tension is between the familiar finished object and the unequal conditions under which it was produced. The visitor should ask whose labor becomes visible in a label and whose experience remains missing. This is not a lesson in how to make prison goods, a celebration of punishment or a judgment about identifiable victims.

**Voice gap:** no authenticated firsthand Laramie prisoner account was recovered in this pass. Sherwood is explicitly a curator interpreting evidence, not a substitute narrator speaking for incarcerated people. Do not fill that silence with invented dialogue, composite characters or testimony from the different Rawlins prison.

## 3. Then / now
- **1872/1873:** distinguish construction from occupation. The nomination dates the first prisoners' arrival to **January 14, 1873**, after the 1872 building work. [5]
- **1875:** the nomination credits incarcerated labor for the warden's residence. This is building-specific, not a claim that every surviving structure has the same provenance. [5]
- **Later prison industry:** the preservation organization describes manufacturing as part of the institution's operations, not merely a craft display. [4]
- **2008–2009:** restoration reporting describes plans for interpretation. A planned historical demonstration is not current availability or an ethical instruction for this visitor experience. [2]
- **Now:** reading a site label is voluntary; historical confinement was not. A visitor cannot claim to have experienced imprisonment by standing in a cell.

## 4. What's still there
**Historic fabric and restoration:** the nomination records several construction phases and later agricultural alterations. It is a dated assessment, not a present condition survey. Its uncertain identification of one brick addition as a possible broom factory must not be treated as a definitive modern building label. [5]

**Public interpretation:** the historic site offers admitted self-guided access, but this draft does not guarantee entry to every building or a working demonstration. Current barriers and operator directions control. [1]

**Evidence outside the site:** the published curator interview, archive-based segment and nomination provide different perspectives. Reading them does not imply that their source materials are in the visitor center or available on demand.

**Excluded:** locked or staff-only areas, tunnels, archaeological investigation, equipment operation, graves, private land, active correctional facilities and any route based on a former escape or punishment location. No prisoner costumes, mugshot recreation, restraints, staged confinement or compulsory silence exercise.

**Permanent rule:** a historic road or district pin is not a public entrance.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
1. **Before travel:** check the winter ticket cutoff, closure exceptions and expected cold. Read the short archival account first.
2. **Admission:** establish which public interiors are open that day. A closed industrial building is not a reason to find another door.
3. **Read an available labor-related interpretation:** if such a label is currently accessible, distinguish object, original evidence and the museum's explanation. If it is not, use the published source without pretending an exhibit was encountered.
4. **End with the missing worker:** ask what evidence would be needed to understand the person's experience beyond the institution's account of revenue or reform.

The strongest moment is not a cell door closing. It is noticing the gap between a finished product and a worker who has been reduced to a category in a record. This single-site sequence can end early if cold or emotional discomfort makes continuing inappropriate. No outdoor detour or performance is necessary.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
- Secondary visitor listings sometimes give 1901 as the end of Laramie's prison use; the operator uses 1903. Transfer chronology needs its own records before claiming a precise final-prisoner date. [1, 6]
- A tourism listing prints “Apr 31,” an impossible date. Use the operator's October–April season, not that typo. [1, 6]
- The nomination's possible broom-factory identification is explicitly tentative. It should not be reconciled by guesswork with the restored Prison Industries Building. [5]
- The nomination's historical condition statements and later restoration reporting are not present structural-safety assurances.
- No primary prisoner diary, labor payroll, complete contract, exact artifact accession or current exhibit-label readback was secured. This limits the human account and remains a research priority, not a license to invent one.
- Published winter admission does not establish snow clearance, every interior's accessibility or current demonstration staffing.
