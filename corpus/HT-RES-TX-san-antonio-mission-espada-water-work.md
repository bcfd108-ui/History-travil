# Mission Espada — the system that moved water
Research draft · Texas OP02 · Fred → Dash

## 2 · Story

### A ditch that crossed a creek

The open land around Mission Espada tells only half the story. Fields depended on a managed flow: a diversion dam fed a gravity ditch, and an aqueduct carried it over Piedras Creek. The federal Historic American Engineering Record dates the aqueduct to between 1731 and 1745. It documents the unequal stone arches, lime accumulation that helped cement the masonry, and later interventions that kept water moving. Its account of these structures is based on engineering observation and historical records [3–4].

### Farmers took the ditch back into service

Water rights holders reorganized in 1895 after a period of disuse. They cleaned and altered the channel and repaired the diversion dam. HAER quotes an earlier report describing vegetables growing “in the greatest profusion.” That is the earlier observer’s phrase as reproduced in HAER; the original report was not independently read. The engineering survey also gives a reported acre of irrigated cotton yielding about one bale against about a quarter bale on nearby nonirrigated ground. These are reported historical comparisons, without enough detail to project them across the valley [3].

### The work had people behind it

NPS uses the Contact Station’s loom to explain everyday work. The park’s colonial records describe what the institution recorded. The Tāp Pīlam Coahuiltecan Nation’s own published account identifies its enduring connection to the San Antonio missions. Keep those sources visible side by side when reading about colonial labor and the lives of families associated with the missions [2, 4–5].

## 3 · Then / now

**Historic place.** Mission San Francisco de la Espada, the lands it served, and the separate supply works north of the compound. The river was subsequently rechanneled around the old dam’s reach [3–4].

**Lawful physical anchor.** NPS Mission Espada Contact Station and signed public approach, 10040 Espada Road, San Antonio. Drive-up and short walk along public NPS ways to the staffed interpretation. Stop at posted boundaries. The church, community, dam, private ditch banks and ceremonial spaces are outside this stop [1–2].

## 5 · Trip-shaped stop progression

1. Arrive at the signed NPS visitor entry and establish which rooms and paths are open [1–2].
2. Read the three parts of the irrigation system beside the public interpretation: intake, grade and crossing [3].
3. **Strongest story moment — A creek under a ditch.** At the Contact Station, follow the HAER description across two unequal stone arches. Stop with the idea that water could cross a creek while the planted land remained downstream of the system. Quiet reflection only [2–3].

## 6 · Honest gaps and conflicting dates

HAER assigns the aqueduct a 1731–1745 construction window [3]. The Library of Congress Highsmith catalog summary asserts 1731 [6]. The broad dating remains the engineering record’s assessment. The physical survey and the catalog caption have different evidentiary weight. The 1890s observer is available here only as quoted by HAER, and no daily flow or traversal of the canal has been verified [3].
