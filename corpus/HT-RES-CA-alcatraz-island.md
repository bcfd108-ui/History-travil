# HT-RES-CA-alcatraz-island — A fortress, a military prison, and a federal penitentiary on one bay island
Division prisons-asylums-tours-001 • Research draft • Checked November 22, 2026

Alcatraz is a rock in San Francisco Bay — about a third of a mile long, 525 feet across at its widest, and 140 feet high, in the National Register nomination's measurements — that the United States used in succession as a harbor fortress, its first official army prison, and, from 1934 to 1963, the federal penitentiary of last resort. After the prison closed, Native American activists occupied the island for some nineteen months in 1969–1971, and in 1972 it became part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area. The visit today is a ferry ride to the island and a walk through the whole layered ground: 1850s fortifications, the 1912 cellhouse, and the ruins the occupation left.

## 1. Trip gate
The visit is a National Park Service visit with a commercial ferry gate: Alcatraz Island, Golden Gate National Recreation Area, San Francisco, California, administered by the National Park Service. The NPS fees page states that the National Park Service does not charge an entrance fee to visit Alcatraz Island, but a ticket for ferry transportation by Alcatraz City Cruises is required; that Alcatraz City Cruises is the only NPS-authorized ferry provider and the only ferry permitted to dock at the island; and that ferry fees are not covered by annual passes or NPS fee-free days. Ferry tickets options and fees as the NPS page states them: Day Tour — children under 5, $0; children 5–11, $29.15; youth 12–17, $47.95; adults 18+, $47.95; seniors 62+, $45.15. Night Tour — adults 18+, $59.65 (full row on the NPS page). Behind the Scenes Tour — adults 18+, $104.65, including the Night Tour. Ranger tours are free; the cellhouse audio tour is included in the ticket. Tickets may be purchased up to 90 days in advance, and the NPS strongly recommends advance reservations. The NPS hours page states that departures are available about every half hour throughout the day beginning at 8:45 a.m., and that Alcatraz is open every day except Christmas, Thanksgiving, and New Year's Day; ferries depart from Pier 33, at the Embarcadero and Bay Street, San Francisco. The island has no street address; the ferry landing is the only public way on or off.

## 2. The story
The island's recorded name is Spanish. The Federal Bureau of Prisons (BOP) history records that in 1775 the Spanish explorer Juan Manuel de Ayala was the first to sail into what is now San Francisco Bay, that his expedition named one of its islands "Alcatraces," and that the name was Anglicized to Alcatraz; its exact meaning, the BOP notes, is still debated (see §6).

The United States took the island for defense. The BOP records that in 1850 a presidential order set aside the island for possible use as a military reservation, and that the Army built a citadel at the top of the island in the early 1850s, planning more than 100 cannons; together with Fort Point and Lime Point, Alcatraz formed a "triangle of defense" at the bay's entrance, and the island was also the site of the first operational lighthouse on the West Coast — the nomination dates the first U.S. lighthouse on the Pacific Coast to 1854. The nomination describes the fortifications begun in 1853 and expanded until 1874 as the earliest permanent U.S. fortifications on the Pacific Coast: batteries blasted into the cliffs, a switchback road from the wharf begun in the fall of 1853, a defensive wall, a guardhouse, and the brick citadel, a defensive barracks the nomination suggests may have been unique in American military architecture.

The prison use began inside the fortress. The BOP records that by the late 1850s the first military prisoners were being housed on the island; the nomination states that Alcatraz was first designated a military prison in 1861 and calls it the first official army prison in the nation. Through the Civil War and after, the island held military prisoners — and, as the NPS summary notes, some who were not soldiers at all, including Southern sympathizers during the Civil War, Native American prisoners, and, later, conscientious objectors of the First World War.

In 1909 the Army tore down the citadel, leaving its basement level to serve as the foundation for a new military prison. From 1909 through 1911, in the BOP's account, the military prisoners on Alcatraz built the new prison themselves; designated the Pacific Branch, U.S. Disciplinary Barracks, it is this building — the cellhouse, completed in 1912 — that later became famous as "The Rock." The Historic American Buildings Survey record for the cellhouse carries its layered construction plainly: building dates of 1853–1859 for the initial construction (the citadel it stands on) and 1909–1912 for the subsequent work.

The Army used the island from 1850 until 1933, when it was transferred to the U.S. Department of Justice for use by the Federal Bureau of Prisons. The federal government, in the BOP's words, had decided to open a maximum-security, minimum-privilege penitentiary to deal with the most incorrigible inmates in federal prisons, and to show the public that it was serious about stopping the rampant crime of the 1920s and 1930s. The penitentiary was deliberately small and monotonous. The BOP records an average population of only about 260–275 — it never once reached its capacity of 336, and held less than 1 percent of the federal prison population. A prisoner at Alcatraz had four rights: food, clothing, shelter, and medical care. Everything else — work, correspondence, family visits, the library, recreation — was a privilege to be earned. Several well-known criminals did time there — Al Capone, George "Machine-Gun" Kelly, Alvin Karpis, Arthur "Doc" Barker — but the BOP is plain that most of the men sent to Alcatraz were not famous gangsters; they were prisoners who refused to conform at other institutions, who were considered violent and dangerous, or who were considered escape risks. Over 29 years, more than 1,500 people were incarcerated on the island, per the NPS. Robert Stroud, the so-called "Birdman of Alcatraz," belongs to the record's corrections of its own legends: the BOP states plainly that Stroud never had any birds at Alcatraz — his bird studies were at Leavenworth — and that he spent his 17 years on the island, from 1942, in segregation in D Block and in the prison hospital.

Escape was the institution's defining pressure, and the BOP keeps the full count. Over the 29 years of federal operation, 36 men — including two who tried twice — were involved in 14 separate escape attempts. Of these, 23 were caught, 6 were shot and killed during their escape, and 2 drowned; five are listed to this day as "missing and presumed drowned," and officially, the BOP states, no one ever succeeded in escaping from Alcatraz. The most violent attempt was the "Battle of Alcatraz," May 2–4, 1946: six prisoners overpowered cellhouse officers and gained access to weapons and keys, but did not have the key to the recreation yard door; rather than surrender, they fought. Two correctional officers, William Miller and Harold Stites, were killed, about 18 officers were injured, U.S. Marines were called in, and the attempt ended on May 4 with the bodies of Bernard Coy, Joe Cretzer, and Marvin Hubbard found in the cellhouse. Sam Shockley and Miran Thompson were tried for the officers' deaths and executed at San Quentin in December 1948; Clarence Carnes, 19, received a second life sentence. The most famous attempt was the quietest: on June 11, 1962, Frank Morris and brothers John and Clarence Anglin vanished from their cells, leaving dummy heads in their beds, and are believed to have left on a raft made of prison-issued raincoats. No sign of the men was found; they are officially listed as missing and presumed drowned. That December, John Paul Scott bent the bars of a cellhouse basement kitchen window, reached the water, and was found alive on the rocks near Fort Point, suffering from shock and hypothermia — and was returned to Alcatraz.

The prison closed on cost, not on escape. On March 21, 1963, USP Alcatraz closed after 29 years of operation. The BOP states explicitly that it did not close because of the disappearance of Morris and the Anglins — the decision had been made long before — but because the institution was too expensive to continue operating: an estimated $3–5 million was needed just for restoration and maintenance, and in 1959 the daily per capita cost at Alcatraz was $10.10 against $3.00 at USP Atlanta. Isolation made everything cost boat fare: the island had no source of fresh water, and nearly one million gallons had to be barged to the island each week.

After the closure the island stood basically abandoned, and proposals came and went. In November 1969 a group of Native American activists, Indians of All Tribes, claimed Alcatraz as Indian land, hoping to create a Native American cultural center and education complex, and using the occupation, in the BOP's summary, to illustrate the troubles faced by Native Americans. The NPS dates the occupation November 1969 through June 1971 and calls it, with the brief 1964 landing before it, the period that galvanized the Red Power movement. Support was initially strong and thousands visited; over the months, a fire destroyed the lighthouse keeper's home, the Warden's home, and the Officers' Club. In June 1971 federal marshals removed the remaining occupiers. In 1972 Congress created the Golden Gate National Recreation Area with Alcatraz included, and the island opened to the public in the fall of 1973. The BOP's current figure is more than one million visitors a year.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a working fortress-prison on a bare rock — sandstone thinly covered, in the nomination's description, with the guano of seabirds — where everything and everyone arrived and left by boat, and where a man's whole entitlement was food, clothing, shelter, and medical care. **Now:** a National Historic Landmark district (National Register No. 76000209, listed 1976; designated a National Historic Landmark in 1986) inside a national recreation area, reached by a scheduled ferry, where the cellhouse audio tour is included in the ferry ticket and ranger tours are free.
**Then:** on May 2, 1946, six men with seized weapons holding a cellhouse they could not get out of, because one yard-door key was missing. **Now:** that same cellhouse walked daily by visitors, its corridors documented tier by tier in the Historic American Buildings Survey.

## 4. What's still there
- The cellhouse (1912) on its citadel-basement foundation — the cell rows of A, B, C, and D Blocks, Broadway between B and C, the dining hall, and the isolation cells of D Block, documented in HABS survey CA-1792-A- The fortification layer: the switchback road from the dock begun in 1853, the defensive wall with its granite coping, guardhouse and battery remains, the citadel basement inside the cellhouse, and the lighthouse — successor to the 1854 light, the first U.S. lighthouse on the Pacific Coast, still serving (per the nomination)- The prison-era support buildings: the New Industries and Model Industries Buildings, the recreation yard and its guard houses, the water tower, and the dock and Building No. 64
- The ruins of the domestic island: the Warden's House and Officers' Club, burned during the 1969–1971 occupation and standing as ruins

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route:

1. **At Pier 33, and on the water:** The gate is part of the record — the island had no other way in or out, for garrison, prisoners, or water barged in weekly. Read the crossing as the prison's first wall.
2. **At the dock and the switchback road:** Start with the oldest built layer — the road blasted from the wharf in the fall of 1853, the defensive wall, and the guardhouse.
3. **In the cellhouse:** Broadway first, between B and C Blocks, then a typical cell, then D Block's isolation cells — the penitentiary's whole argument in one building: one man to a cell, four rights, everything else earned.
4. **At the yard, the industries buildings, and the Warden's House ruin:** Close with the island whole, and with 1969–1971, the occupation that ended the island's seclusion and made the public visit possible.

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The island's name will not settle. The National Register nomination states that Alcatraz "is ancient Spanish for 'cormorant,' although commonly translated as 'pelican.'" The Bureau of Prisons states that the exact meaning "is still debated" and is "usually defined as meaning 'pelican' or 'strange bird.'" Both readings print.

The island's size is given two ways inside the nomination itself. Its description measures the island at approximately one third of a mile long, 525 feet across at its widest, 140 feet high, and 22.5 acres; a continuation sheet in the same file describes it as "about a half mile long." Its geographical data gives the acreage of the nominated property as 47 — a figure the verbal boundary description explains: the district boundary encompasses "all of the island and immediately surrounding waters." The 22.5 acres is the island; the 47 is the district including water. The two lengths are not reconciled in the form and both print.

When Alcatraz became a military prison depends on which act is counted. The nomination states the island "was first designated a military prison in 1861." The BOP records only that "by the late 1850s, the first military prisoners were being housed on the island." The NPS's own island summary dates the designation as a disciplinary barrack to 1868. All three print.

The cellhouse construction span varies by source and by what is being dated: the BOP gives 1909–1911 for the prisoners' construction of the new prison; the Conservancy's timeline gives the cellhouse's completion and the prisoners' move-in as 1912; the HABS record carries 1909–1912 as the subsequent-work dates, on an 1853–1859 initial construction (the citadel). This entry prints 1912 for the completed cellhouse and the range where the process is meant.

The occupation's length is rounded two ways: the BOP describes the public visiting "over the next 18 months," while the NPS calls it a "19-month occupation." The dated bounds in the records are November 1969 to June 1971, which is where this entry rests the fact.

The fate of Frank Morris and John and Clarence Anglin is unresolved in the official record and is printed as unresolved: the BOP lists them, with Theodore Cole and Ralph Roe of the 1937 attempt, as "missing and presumed drowned," states that officially no one ever succeeded in escaping, and notes that whether anyone succeeded "depends on the definition of 'successful escape.'"

The trip-gate records are of different vintages: the NPS fees page was last updated January 21, 2026, and the NPS hours page April 27, 2021. The fees in §1 are from the 2026 page; the departure pattern (about every half hour from 8:45 a.m.) is from the 2021 page and prints as the NPS states it.

## 8. Standing on the ground
What a visitor can check against the record, on site:

- From the dock, walk the switchback road and check it against the nomination: begun by blasting from the wharf in the fall of 1853, flanked by a brick defensive wall 20 to 25 feet high with much of its granite coping still in place.
- At the cellhouse's south front, compare the facade in front of you with the 1986 HABS frame in §7 — same entrance steps, same barred window grid, the lighthouse shadow's source standing behind you.
- Inside the cellhouse, stand on Broadway between B and C Blocks and check the one-man cells against the BOP's account of the regime: four rights, everything else a privilege.
- In the cellhouse's lower level, look for the citadel the Army tore down in 1909: the BOP records that its basement was left as the new prison's foundation.
- At the Warden's House ruin, check the burned domestic island against the record: the BOP states that the fire during the 1969–1971 occupation destroyed the lighthouse keeper's home, the Warden's home, and the Officers' Club.
