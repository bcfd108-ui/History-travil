# HT-RES — Bunker Hill / Kellogg: two conflicts, not one explosion
Research revision R01 • Checked 2026-11-19 • Research draft for Dash QC; not published or field verified.

## 1. Editorial finding and visit proposition
The useful question is not merely who blew up a mill. It is who could work, who could organize, who could summon the state, and who could be imprisoned without the visitor confusing allegation with conviction. Tell 1892 and 1899 separately. Kellogg is an interpretation base, not a verified walkable battlefield. Never present a later museum building as the destroyed concentrator.

**Strongest moment, editorial recommendation:** put the union newspaper's description of destruction beside the state's work-permit requirements, then ask what control over employment meant. Do this in the museum when open, or through the reader while legally parked at an ordinary public stop. No trespass is necessary to encounter the conflict.

## 2. What happened: evidence-led narrative
Mine owners closed operations in January 1892 amid prices and freight costs. After the freight dispute, wage reductions and imported nonunion labor produced a new confrontation. The Idaho State Historical Society's 1968 account connects the exposure of a Pinkerton agent in the union with escalation and describes arrests, martial law, and the subsequent formation of the Western Federation of Miners. It is a retrospective state account, not a participant's testimony. [B1]

**July 11, 1892:** distinguish the Frisco mill explosion in the Gem/Burke Canyon area from Bunker Hill at Wardner. The University of Idaho's cataloged historical photograph identifies the wrecked Frisco mill and dates it July 11. The image establishes physical destruction; the catalog's attribution to union strikers is an archival description, not something an image alone proves. Its geographic metadata uses both Gem and Burke, so do not use its map point as a surveyed footprint. The July 12, 1892 Daily Eugene Guard dispatch separately named the Frisco mill and Gem mine; contemporaneous reporting reinforces the distinction but is not neutral proof of every accusation. [B2, B10]

**April 29, 1899:** men commandeered a train and destroyed the Bunker Hill and Sullivan concentrator. The Library of Congress guide records the event separately from 1892 and traces subsequent federal proceedings. Wage and recognition disputes, armed coercion, destruction, and state repression must each remain visible rather than collapsing the story into a heroic adventure. [B3]

Brigadier General H. C. Merriam's July 31 report, printed as Senate Document 24 on December 11, 1899, supplies primary evidence of the military response. His table records troops arriving from May 2 onward; this is more precise than saying every unit arrived May 4. His report reproduces work-permit conditions requiring denials of participation and repudiation of specified union associations. These are evidence of the state's conditions, not proof each prisoner committed a crime. Appendix A reproduces the May 3 Idaho State Tribune, the WFM newspaper; Appendix B reproduces the May 6 Wardner News. Their juxtaposition exposes opposing positions, but both are selected through Merriam's framing. [B4, printed pp. 8–9, 14–15 and appendices]

### Voices, attribution, and unequal survival
- **Union press:** Appendix A's headline, “BUNKER HILL DESTROYED,” is the union newspaper's contemporary presentation. Its celebratory framing should be identified, not adopted by the app. [B4, p.15]
- **Military authority:** Merriam characterizes events as crimes and defends troop conduct. The report is both evidence and an institutional justification. [B4]
- **Management archive:** UIdaho's Bunker Hill Labor Collection contains 463 digitized letters. Its description explicitly notes missing 1893 correspondence and missing M–Z material for 1898–1900. This archive is unusually valuable for owners/managers, but not a complete record of workers' experience. [B5]
- **Verified individual catalog item:** Frederick Burbidge to F. W. Bradley, July 23, 1899 [01], MG367_85_029, concerns water issues. Do not advertise it as a strike eyewitness letter just because it falls in 1899. [B6]
- **Missing voice work:** obtain original union paper scans, named workers' testimony, family experiences, and soldiers' own accounts before claiming a definitive multi-voice history. No invented diary or composite speaker.

## 3. Conflicting labels and dates: keep visible
1. ISHS Reference Series 447 calls the 1892 destroyed plant the **Gem mill**. UIdaho's historical photograph catalog calls it **Frisco Mill-Explosion, Gem**. Use Frisco as the working identification, with this disagreement retained. Gem can be a place name; the error must not generate a second invented explosion. [B1–B2]
2. LOC's guide has a July 15 **1893** timeline entry amid 1892 events. Do not silently use it to date the 1892 roundup. It needs checking against original issues. [B3]
3. The 1899 destruction date is April 29, not a generic May date inferred from a photo catalog or later news publication. Publication and event dates are distinct. [B1, B3–B4]
4. The museum's 1906 house cannot be evidence that an 1899 building survived. It was moved in 1940, rotated, and altered. [B7]

## 4. Then / now and runnable stop order
**1. Before departure:** check the museum's own website. As read November 19, it closed September 28, 2026 and plans to reopen Memorial Day weekend 2027, Wednesday–Sunday 11–4. Special tours are possible by arrangement, not already booked. Address: 820 McKinley Avenue, Kellogg; heritage record adds W. Do not promise indoor access now. [B8]

**2. Museum when confirmed open:** Shoshone County Mining and Smelting Museum, Bunker Hill Staff House. The house was built in 1906 for manager Stanley A. Easton and Estelle, later accommodated staff/guests, and became a museum after its 1980s closure. Its management domestic setting is itself interpretive evidence, but it is not the destroyed mill. Suggested dwell 45–75 minutes is planning allowance, not a published tour duration. [B7]

**3. Public streetscape/context only:** from a lawful public location, discuss the town's industrial landscape without claiming a particular visible structure is an 1899 remnant. Exact mill/bullpen footprints, accessible viewing spots, parking legality, and sightline are not verified in this revision. No mine-property, rail-right-of-way, waste-pile or adit excursion. A trail add-on needs a manager-verified trailhead and current condition check before routing.

**Winter fallback:** if no special museum opening is confirmed, make Kellogg an optional interpretation pause, not an advertised indoor destination. Do not force a detour merely to photograph a closed door.

### Contamination and public access
Idaho's basin agencies warn about lead-bearing dirt/dust, including recreation exposures. Stay in designated public areas, heed signs, avoid bare mine/mill soil, bring clean water, wash before food, and remove tracked dirt. Hand sanitizer does not remove lead. Do not collect “historic ore” or invite children to handle industrial debris. These precautions are not a claim any particular unassessed parcel is safe. [B9]
