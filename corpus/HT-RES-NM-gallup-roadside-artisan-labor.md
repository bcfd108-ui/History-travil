## 2. The story

**Named first-person artisan: Raymond C. Yazzie**, whose interview text is published by Northern Arizona University's Special Collections. He introduces his own clan relationships and discusses his Navajo family, mother Elsie, brother Lee and wife Colina. His mother encouraged workmanship and education; he remembers starting in a shop with cleanup work before apprenticeship. His wife contributed substantially to developing the business. [5]

His own succinct line is: **“I can never say to myself that I could have done all of this by myself.”** The web transcript was read; the separate full-text link failed, and the audio was not listened to. Interview date and accession number were not established. [5]

**Attribution boundary:** This is one Diné/Navajo artist's published personal account, not a statement by the Navajo Nation government or a universal account of Native commerce. The university is the repository/publisher, not the sovereign cultural authority. His family testimony is used as personal experience, without extracting ceremonial knowledge or reproducing private-location descriptions.

**Original interpretive voice:** A visitor sees a completed object. The maker sees years of learning, family help, uncertainty and decisions about what that work deserves. Roadside commerce can make those years disappear behind a price tag. This entry restores the people to the object without turning a living community into an attraction or a shop into a museum.

## 3. Then / now

Yazzie's retrospective account concerns learning and earning, not a current price list or invitation to visit his business. His views of trading relationships are personal and varied; they do not establish that every artist was treated alike. [5]

The present cultural center names the Storyteller Museum and galleries among its public offerings. Its own staff page provides another first-person voice: **Brian Antonio**, who identifies his origin as Laguna Pueblo and describes contributing to the Storyteller Museum's design and implementation. This is a contemporary worker's self-description, distinct from Yazzie's historical recollection and not an official statement for Laguna Pueblo. [1,2]

No current display of a Yazzie-family work, interview or personal artifact was verified. The public anchor and historical evidence are deliberately linked as research context, not falsely merged into an exhibition claim.

**One public institution, not a shopping or historic-road route.** Allow a provisional 30–60 minutes, an editorial estimate rather than a promised guided-tour duration.
