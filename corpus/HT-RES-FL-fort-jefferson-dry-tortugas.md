## 2. Story

**Fort Jefferson / Dry Tortugas · Reflection · Research draft · Fred**

The government tried to build a self-contained fortress on a small island where it had to import nearly everything. Army engineers began construction in 1846. The HABS addendum identifies Norton and Parker’s unsuccessful early supply contract, Andrew B. Vennard’s successor contract, and Pensacola firm Bacon and Abercrombie as a leading prewar brick supplier. Materials came from both northern and southern ports. Enslaved workers from Key West and St. Augustine made up much of the early labor force. The HABS history records military prisoners gradually taking over much of that work during the Civil War. NPS describes the same shift without giving a precise start year. Named contractors survive more readily in these accounts than the names of the workers. [5–7]

Emily Holder lived at the post with her husband, an Army surgeon. In her 1892 published account, reproduced by the University of South Florida, she remembered the arrival of “one hundred and twenty-five workmen” on one vessel and brick on another. They appear in her account beside ships carrying animals and other supplies. This is a resident’s retrospective testimony, limited to what she saw or recalled. It makes the supply chain tangible. Her broader account describes heat and sickness inside a crowded post. It does not establish the legal status of the workers in that passage. [8]

Army custody outlasted the fort’s usefulness as designed. The Union garrison held it through the war. After the Lincoln assassination military proceedings, the government sent physician Samuel Mudd and other convicted prisoners to Fort Jefferson. During the 1867 yellow-fever outbreak Mudd helped care for the sick. The well-known episode fits a much larger community of soldiers, prisoners, workers and families whose lives depended on the same confined place and its distant supplies. The NPS history dates the Army’s formal abandonment to 1874. The HABS history describes roughly three decades of work with some later activity. Fort Jefferson was never completed. [5–8]

## 3. Then and now

HABS documents an immense brick perimeter, six bastions, repeated casemate arches and an adjoining moat. The NPS cultural landscape inventory distinguishes historic fabric, ruins and later service infrastructure. The Key West museum is a separate building with an interpretive model. Read the model for the shape and scale of the fort. Read HABS and the landscape inventory for surviving material and for later changes. [1, 5–6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- HABS credits changes in weapons and foundation settlement among the reasons the fort did not reach its designed form. NPS adds logistical and structural decisions, including reducing the load on the second level. Formal Army abandonment in 1874 and the period in which work tapered represent different milestones. [5, 7]
- The seaport’s older free Fort Jefferson Museum listing and the current Sails to Rails admission page both describe a model at 901 Caroline Street. Use the current operator’s hours and admission. Treat the NPS Bight and concession pages as evidence of the exhibit’s interpretation, with no independent assertion of a second currently free room. NOAA directly says its Eco-Discovery Center is temporarily closed for roof evaluation, superseding the hours shown on the NPS park page. [1–4, 9–10]
- Named laborers’ primary records and confirmed independent coordinate pairs remain open. The official Fort Jefferson listing describes island historic ground. It supplies no locator for the current museum. [5–6, 11]
