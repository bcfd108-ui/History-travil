## 2. The story

Begin with the document, then let a woman answer it. **Council Bill 70**, approved **December 10, 1869**, is transcribed by the Wyoming State Library. Its first section permits resident women aged twenty-one and over to vote and aligns their franchise and officeholding with the territory's election laws. This is a specific primary legislative text reproduced by a public repository, not a modern slogan about equality. [4]

**Amalia Post** makes the right active rather than ceremonial. D. Claudia Thompson's archival-based history quotes her letter to her sister: **“I am intending to vote this next election”**. The account documents jury service and her 1871 national suffrage-convention participation. It treats her appeal against repeal as credited or probable, not proof that she alone changed the governor's mind. [5]

A second primary text is Post's **1890 statehood-celebration speech**, reproduced by Wyoming State Library from the **Cheyenne Daily Sun, July 24, 1890, page 5**. She thanks the convention for constitutional protection of women's enfranchisement, calling it **“woman’s Magna Carta”**. This is a named woman's public political argument. Its rhetoric also invokes hierarchies of civilization and stereotypes; present her as a historical actor, not a flawless modern spokesperson. The transcript was read; the newspaper pixels and linked modern audio were not inspected or heard. [6]

Women were not merely the audience for William Bright's bill. They exercised the vote, served in institutions, organized, and pressed officials. Their actions gave a legislative experiment consequences beyond its sponsors' intentions.

## 3. Then / now

Three milestones must stay separate: the **1869 territorial act**, the **1871 attempted repeal and gubernatorial veto**, and the **1889 constitution/1890 statehood**. The State Library supplies the act, discusses the repeal contest, and identifies the constitution's suffrage clause. [4] This stop is about maintaining and using political rights, not another South Pass labor itinerary.

Participation was broader than a white-pioneer celebration, but not universal. Jennifer Helton's source-based study reproduces officials' accounts of **Black women voting in 1870**, while noting that their individual identities are often not recoverable. She distinguishes that from the exclusion of most Indigenous women through citizenship rules. Do not turn 1869 into a claim that every woman could vote, or 1924 into proof that all later barriers vanished. [7]

Helton also documents **Lucy Phillips and other Black women** opposing school segregation in Cheyenne in 1885 through a newspaper protest and the threat of electoral accountability. Phillips's own participation in the 1870 election remains **unconfirmed**. This distinction keeps a known political action from being replaced by an appealing but unproved “first.” [7]

Today a public museum and archives make it possible to examine how the state tells its story. They do not recreate the original legislature or establish access to every historic venue.
