# Caruthersville — The river’s height and a household’s winter
HT-RES-MO-caruthersville-1937-flood-households · Draft for independent Dash review · Quiet witness

## 2. Story

The gauge at Caruthersville registered 46 feet during the winter flood of 1937. In its published account of daily stages for 1984, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers records that earlier maximum on February 5–7. Those numbers measure the river against a gauge datum. The same number cannot supply a water depth inside a house or establish who had already left town. The National Weather Service’s retrospective lists February 5, and its separate service assessment gives February 3. The date discrepancy matters if a newspaper report or household letter is used to say when people made a decision. [3–5]

The city had built defenses in stages. The Corps describes the earlier wall and an approximately 3,000-foot passage of wall built in 1932, after the 1927 flood and the 1928 Flood Control Act. In a July 10, 1997 letter reproduced in his oral history transcript, Clyde Southern called the wall “part of the levee system.” He described openings and closable timber barriers and later remembered a district of workers, cotton labor and unequal property ownership near it. The letter and its context help attach people and streets to the concrete, while leaving specific 1937 household experiences open. [6, 7]

At Charleston, in a separate part of southeastern Missouri, journalist Mildred Wallhausen remembered visiting reporters using her newspaper office and asking about the Birds Point–New Madrid floodway as if someone were draining a tub. “Well, there was no plug!” she told interviewers. On the next transcript page, an interviewer reads the caption of a January 28, 1937 newspaper photograph of refugees near Wyatt, taken by A. H. Wallhausen. The voices are regional evidence for how flood displacement made news and how poorly a national news desk could understand the local system. That story belongs to Charleston, Wyatt and the floodway. A Caruthersville household account still has to be found locally. [8]

## 3. Then and now

**Historic location.** The flood stage belongs to the Caruthersville Mississippi River gauge record. The defenses described in the Corps history are historically separate from the courthouse. The Ward Avenue courthouse is an ordinary civic point from which to read the documented gauges and oral histories. [1, 3, 6–8]

**Lawful physical anchor.** Pemiscot County Courthouse, 610 Ward Ave., Caruthersville. Drive up to the courthouse area, park lawfully and use the open public sidewalk on Ward Avenue. The route ends on the civic block. Residential parcels, docks, gates, flood defense structures and water’s edge are outside it. [1, 2, 6]

## 6. Gaps and conflicts

- Army Corps daily stages list 46.0 feet February 5–7, 1937. NWS Memphis gives February 5 and a separate NOAA assessment table gives February 3. All three list the same height. Keep the original gauge sheet and datum reconciliation open. The separate 47.75-foot 1937 reading on the same NWS page is under Tiptonville, Tennessee. [3–5]
- The Corps floodwall page’s direct text and browser retrieval each returned HTTP 403. A search-index copy supplied the 1932 length and location. Those details carry that limitation. Current work limits remain unverified and outside the civic walk. [6]
- Southern is local, but the reproduced 1997 letter describes the wall and later neighborhoods. Wallhausen is a regional eyewitness through a published oral history transcription. The county historical society lists local sources. A named Caruthersville household’s direct 1937 diary, letter, relief record or published statement remains unlocated. [7–9]
