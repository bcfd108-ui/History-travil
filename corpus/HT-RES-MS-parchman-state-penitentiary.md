## 2. The story

Following the Civil War, Mississippi leased state convicts to private business contractors (`1867–1894`), a system notorious for forced labor, extreme mortality, and political corruption. In **1894**, the legislature appropriated $125,000 to purchase state farm tracts in Rankin, Hinds (Oakley), and Holmes counties; in **1900**, it appropriated **$80,000** to buy the **3,789-acre Parchman Plantation** in Sunflower County, whose patriarch **J. M. Parchman** stayed on as first warden. Four stockades were built and prisoners transferred in **1901** to clear bottomland timber and plant crops, earning the unfenced penal colony the nickname **"the prison without walls."** [1, 2, 4]

In **1904**, Governor **James K. Vardaman** made Parchman the centerpiece of Mississippi's penal policy—promoting state-owned penal cotton agriculture to replace private convict leasing while explicitly grounding the system in Jim Crow racial hierarchy and segregated forced labor. By **1914**, Parchman had expanded across five penal farms and two lime plants totaling **23,910 acres** (today ~18,000 acres), worked largely by predominantly Black prisoners guarded by armed inmate trusties ("trusty shooters") and organized into numbered agricultural camps (`Camp 1`, `Camp 2`, etc.). [1, 4, 5]

Conditions at Parchman drew federal intervention in the landmark civil-rights lawsuit ***Gates v. Collier***, filed February 8, 1971, in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi. In **1972**, Chief Judge **William C. Keady** ruled conditions unconstitutional under the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments—ordering the end of the trusty guard system, racial segregation, and physical abuses. [1]

Within the prison grounds lies a **two-acre pauper's cemetery** where inmates whose families could not claim or afford burial are interred. MDOC burial records show the cemetery's first recorded interment was inmate **Columbus Story** on **September 22, 1930**, with **273 inmates** (**264 men and 9 women**) buried there through **April 30, 2015** (`Gerald Peterson`). [2, 3]

### Haunted history / lore

No haunted-place folklore is carried here. Parchman's documented record—convict leasing, Jim Crow penal labor, federal court reform in *Gates v. Collier*, capital punishment at Unit 17, and the 273 recorded unclaimed burials in Parchman Cemetery—is treated solely on primary historical and legal evidence. [1, 2, 3]

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Founding date (`1900` vs. `1901` vs. `1904`):** MDOC cites `1900` land appropriation and `1901` prisoner arrival/stockades as opening; *Mississippi Encyclopedia* notes `1904` as Gov. Vardaman's political transformation of Parchman Farm into the consolidated state system. All three dates reflect distinct milestones and are preserved. [1, 2, 4]
- **Bed capacity (`3,560` vs. `5,768`):** Current MDOC facility profile lists `3,560` beds across 7 housing units (`58` support buildings), whereas an earlier *Mississippi Encyclopedia* entry cited `5,768` across 18 units prior to unit closures/consolidations. [2, 4]
- **Pre-1930 burials:** MDOC records date Parchman Cemetery's first burial to **September 22, 1930**; whether earlier 1901–1929 prisoner deaths were buried in unrecorded farm plots elsewhere on the 23,910-acre tract was not resolved in sources reviewed this run. [2, 3]
