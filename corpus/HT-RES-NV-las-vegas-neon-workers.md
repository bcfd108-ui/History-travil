## 2. The story
**Named firsthand voice: Betty Willis, commercial artist and sign designer.** Miriam Di Nunzio's Sun-Times article expressly republishes her **March 13, 2005 interview**, conducted through email and phone exchanges. Willis recalls her work and responds to the surprise that a woman designed the famous Welcome sign: **“It was a man’s business back then.”** This is a journalist's published primary interview with the maker, not an unedited transcript or audio recording. [3]

The museum's historical account places Willis at Western Neon when she designed the Welcome sign in **1959**, following commercial-art work in newspaper advertising. It also attributes the Moulin Rouge lettering to her. This institutional history supplies object context; it does not mean the original Welcome sign stands in the museum. [4]

**Original interpretation:** the public remembers a city's giant name before it remembers the person paid to shape the letters. The useful reversal here is from spectacle to authorship: look at a surviving sign as someone's work before treating it as a selfie background. That opens a labor story without pretending one designer alone represents all the electricians, fabricators, installers, cleaners and later conservators.

**One-minute hook, original:** You know the city's greeting. Now meet a woman who helped give the city its visual voice. A sign is also a work record, even when the maker's name is not written across it.

**Deep layer:** UNLV catalogs *The Story of Classic Las Vegas Oral History Interviews*, **MS-01075**, which includes Willis material. The catalog is directly read, but its actual interview files were not accessed; digital access requires advance arrangements. This is a documented research lead, not a second interview claimed as heard. [5]

## 3. Then / now
Then, signs competed for attention as commercial commissions. Now, museum interpretation can recover a maker's name after the advertised business has changed or disappeared. This is an original analytical frame, not a claim that all signs were saved or all contributors are known.

The museum identifies itself as founded in **1996** and describes a Visitors' Center in the former **La Concha Motel lobby**, with an outdoor main collection and a separate North Gallery. These are museum spaces, not a remaining historic sign factory. [6]

Keep commercial intent and public meaning distinct. Willis's interview is evidence of how she remembered her work; the citywide recognition that followed does not erase its paid craft context. Her personal statements are not a blanket rights ruling for photographs, other designs or museum reproductions.
