# HT-RES-MI-fort-wilkins — An 1844 Army post at the top of the Keweenaw, garrisoned five years and kept for a hundred more
Division MI seed-catalog • Research draft • Checked November 26, 2026

The United States Army built a fort in 1844 on a narrow strip of land between Copper Harbor and Lake Fanny Hooe, at the tip of Michigan's Keweenaw Peninsula, to keep the peace in a copper rush it could not otherwise police — two companies of infantry shipped 600 miles from Detroit, 22 buildings raised in about five months for $15,000, on a shore where the government's mineral land office was already drowning in permit requests. The soldiers were needed for five years in total, across two garrisons, and the post was permanently abandoned in 1870. The buildings never left: bought by two counties in 1921, a state park since 1923, restored by the WPA, the fort stands today with 19 buildings, 12 of them original structures from the 1840s.

## 1. Trip gate
Fort Wilkins Historic State Park is a Michigan state park, operated by the Michigan Department of Natural Resources (DNR). The National Park Service's place page for the site — the fort is a Keweenaw Heritage Site within the orbit of Keweenaw National Historical Park — gives the ground in one paragraph: built in 1844, permanently abandoned in 1870, a state park since 1923, with 19 buildings surviving including 12 original structures from 1844, museum exhibits, copper mine sites, lighthouses, and outdoor interpretive trails. The Michigan History Center places the fort a mile east of the village of Copper Harbor in Keweenaw County, and the National Park Service's Keweenaw park guide prints the park's address as 15223 U.S. Highway 41, Copper Harbor — the fort near the top of the peninsula, at the end of the road north.

Vehicle entry is under the statewide Michigan state parks rule: a Recreation Passport is required for a motor vehicle to enter. No dollar figure and no park hours are printed in this entry, because no primary source read in this pass states them (see §6). The visit is the fort complex itself — the buildings around the parade ground, entered as the park opens them — inside a modern state park whose wider ground carries a campground and the Copper Harbor shore. Nothing in this entry directs a visitor into a closed building or anywhere the park itself closes.

## 2. The story
The fort exists because of a survey. The National Park Service's cultural landscape research for the Keweenaw records that Douglass Houghton completed a geological survey of the Keweenaw Peninsula in 1840 and published his report in 1841 — and that the report set off "copper fever." The Treaty of La Pointe went into effect in 1843, a mineral land office opened at Keweenaw Point, and the rush came: the Service counts at least 300 mining operations launched in the region during the 1840s through the 1860s. The permit system meant to order the rush did not hold. The Park Service's account is blunt: by 1845 the land office had received over 700 requests for mineral-exploration permits, the permit system was not patrolled, and speculation and corruption were widespread.

The Army was the government's answer. The National Park Service's own lesson plan on the fort carries the sequence: a Mineral Land Agency was established at Copper Harbor in 1843, and in May 1844 two companies of federal troops were dispatched from Detroit to keep the peace among the miners and to deter any problems with Native Americans, should any occur. In the lesson plan's numbers, 150 soldiers made the 600-mile journey on the wooden sailing ship John Jacob Astor, and Fort Wilkins rose on the narrow strip of land between Copper Harbor and Lake Fanny Hooe. The Michigan History Center's summary of the same founding is a sentence long: built in 1844, the fort "was intended to keep law and order during Michigan's copper rush."

The post went up fast and cheap. The Park Service's lesson plan prices it: Fort Wilkins cost $15,000 and took about five months to build, and 19 of its 22 buildings were completed in the first season. The inventory the lesson plan lists is a self-contained village — officers' quarters, enlisted men's quarters, and married soldiers' quarters; company kitchens and mess rooms; a hospital; a sutler's store; a powder magazine; a guardhouse; storehouses; a cattle yard, stables, and a slaughterhouse. And then the peace held without the garrison. The National Park Service's place page compresses the whole experiment: the fort was built to keep the peace in Copper Country — "However, this was not necessary."

The Army's use of the place came in two short watches. The first garrison ran from 1844 to 1846, when the fort closed with the onset of the Mexican War. What followed was a caretaker's tenure the lesson plan remembers by name: Ordnance Sergeant William B. Wright stayed on as caretaker for nine years, until his discharge in 1855, keeping a post with no garrison. The Army regarrisoned the fort in 1867, after the Civil War — the lesson plan's own span for the second garrison is 1867 to 1870 — and permanently abandoned it in August 1870. The Michigan History Center counts the whole military career in one line: garrisoned for just five years, 1844 to 1846 and 1867 to 1870.

The fort's second life began as a ruin with a view. After the soldiers left, the History Center records, the grounds became a popular picnic and camping spot — and a slow salvage yard: doors and hardware were taken for use elsewhere, glass was broken, wooden stockade pickets were scavenged for firewood, and rain and snowmelt rotted what remained. In 1921, Houghton and Keweenaw counties, answering local petitions, bought the old garrison for $2,000. In July 1923 the State of Michigan acquired it as a state park — the History Center's centennial account marks 2023 as the fort's hundredth year as a state park, 53 years after the Army closed the post. The Department of Conservation, under P. J. Hoffmaster, moved to preserve it, and the fort got its first superintendent: Charles Beatty, a former surface foreman at the Ahmeek copper mine, who lived year-round in the east officer's quarters — a miner keeping the soldiers' fort.

The rescue was completed by the Works Progress Administration. Between 1939 and 1942, WPA work at the fort stabilized sagging chimneys and stone foundations, replaced roofs, rotted timbers, siding, and porches, repaired plaster and floors, and painted the buildings inside and out; the stockade pickets once scavenged for firewood once again enclosed the fort. Fort Wilkins was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on July 8, 1970. The result stands in the History Center's count and the Park Service's: a complex of 19 buildings, 12 of them original structures from the 1840s — the Army's five-year errand kept, as of this writing, for more than a century of park years.

## 3. Then / now
**Then:** a frontier post built in about five months for $15,000, 19 of 22 buildings finished in the first season (National Park Service lesson plan), raised to police a permit rush its own land office could not — over 700 permit requests by 1845, the system unpatrolled (National Park Service cultural landscape report). **Now:** 19 buildings stand, 12 of them original structures from the 1840s (Michigan History Center; National Park Service place page).

**Then:** soldiers on the ground for five years in total — 1844 to 1846 and 1867 to 1870 (Michigan History Center) — and a caretaker alone for nine of the years between. **Now:** a state park for over a century, since July 1923 (Michigan History Center), with the Army's buildings as the exhibit.

**Then:** after abandonment, a picnic ground being picked apart — stockade pickets burned for firewood, hardware carried off, roofs rotting (Michigan History Center). **Now:** the WPA-restored post of 1939–1942 (Michigan History Center), stockade re-enclosed, buildings roofed and painted, kept as a museum ground.

## 5. Trip-shaped stop progression
Conditional interpretation only, not an activated route — the fort is walked as the park lays it out:

1. **At the park entrance on U.S. 41:** Read the geography first. The fort sits on a narrow strip between a harbor and an inland lake, a mile east of the village (National Park Service lesson plan; Michigan History Center) — a post placed to watch a harbor at the top of a copper peninsula.
2. **On the parade ground:** Take the post's plan whole — quarters, barracks, mess rooms, hospital, store, magazine, and guardhouse around one square (National Park Service lesson plan inventory). Twenty-two buildings in five months for $15,000: the plan is the speed.
3. **At the east barracks and mess hall:** The §7 photograph's buildings — the enlisted men's working end of the post, kitchens and mess rooms beside the barracks, at the edge of the parade ground.
4. **At the married soldiers' quarters:** The fort's families. This was a village with wives and children inside the stockade, not only a barracks — the quarters rows are the evidence (National Park Service lesson plan inventory).
5. **At the powder magazine and guardhouse:** The peace that did not need keeping. The Park Service's own verdict on the post's purpose is the stop's text: the fort was built to keep the peace in Copper Country, and "this was not necessary" (National Park Service place page).

## 6. Honest gaps and conflicting dates
The National Park Service's lesson plan contradicts itself on the second garrison, and the conflict prints rather than being smoothed over. In one sentence the page states: "The U.S. Army regarrisoned the fort in 1861, after the Civil War" — a sequence that cannot stand, since 1861 precedes the Civil War's end. The same page, and the Michigan History Center with it, dates the second garrison 1867 to 1870, and §2 prints 1867 on that agreement; the lesson plan's "1861" is recorded here as an error in the source, unreconciled. The lesson plan's reason for the regarrisoning — a shortage of barracks space — prints in that page without corroboration in the other primary sources read and is not carried in §2.

The troop count for May 1844 differs between the sources in hand. The National Park Service's lesson plan carries 150 soldiers on the John Jacob Astor; a Michigan Technological University account of the fort carries 105 officers and soldiers in two companies. Section 2 prints the Park Service's 150 and flags the difference here rather than splitting it. The units' identities — Companies A and B of the 5th Infantry in 1844, and Company E of the 43rd Infantry, then Company K of the 1st Infantry, in the second garrison — rest on university and secondary accounts, not on the primary sources read in this pass, and are recorded here as reported detail only.

Building counts are three different counts, not one number gone wrong: the lesson plan's 22 buildings of the 1844 construction program (19 finished the first season); secondary lists of the historic district that run to 27 structures, counting stables, the slaughterhouse, and later structures; and the 19 buildings standing today, 12 of them original (Michigan History Center; National Park Service). Section 2 uses each count in its own place.

The fort's name is not explained in §2 because no primary source read in this pass states the naming. That the post was named for William Wilkins, Secretary of War in 1844, is carried universally in secondary accounts; it is recorded here as unverified against a primary source in this pass rather than asserted. The same discipline applies to the park's acreage — a figure of 987 acres circulates in secondary listings, no DNR or NPS source read in this pass states an acreage, and none is printed — and to the Copper Harbor Lighthouse's construction date of 1866, which secondary accounts carry and which is therefore not dated in §4.

Visitor specifics are limited by the same gate as the park's hours: no primary source read in this pass states the fort's opening hours, living-history season, or fees, so none are printed; the Recreation Passport rule in §1 is the statewide vehicle-entry rule for Michigan state parks, printed without a price. The photograph in §7 carries a camera position in its Commons file record; it is the photographer's position, not a verified point for the fort or its entrance, and is not carried into §10.
