# Pierce: the discovery that opened — and trespassed — the Clearwater

Research draft for Dash QC • 2026-11-18 • local only, no upload or outreach.

**Trip gate:** The Pierce Courthouse exterior and its NPS wayside can be read any day from the public street in Pierce (Idaho Highway 11 / Gold Rush Historic Byway). The interior is NOT a walk-in museum: the National Park Service states the building is operated by volunteers of the adjacent J. Howard Bradbury Memorial Logging Museum under a cooperative agreement with the Idaho State Historical Society and is "open on request and on summer weekends." In November, plan an exterior stop unless the museum confirms an opening. The NPS page carrying that statement was last updated January 13, 2018 — recheck before promising an interior. Local time: Pacific. [P3, P4]

## 1. The story

Pierce begins with a trespass, and the state's own reference history does not soften that. The Idaho State Historical Society's Reference Series No. 8 records that E. D. Pierce spent eight years trying to enter "the forbidden lands of the Nez Perce Indians," and that on February 20, 1860, having smuggled a prospecting outfit into the camp of the Nez Perce Wislanaeqa, he "went out panning in the North Fork of the Clearwater with Wislanaeqa and found gold." Pierce then tried, and failed, to interest the Nez Perce in allowing white development of the mines. The party he finally led out of Walla Walla on August 12, 1860 numbered only twelve men, and they "had to sneak across the reservation (from which they were excluded by law and by treaty) by a difficult, untraveled route on which they had to spend six weeks to make a one-week trip." [P1]

On September 30, 1860, the party reached Canal Gulch and found "very promising placers on Oro Fino Creek." The roadside marker at the discovery site assigns the actual strike to a member of the party: "The fabulous Gold Rush days of Idaho began on September 30, 1860, when W. F. Bassett struck gold just about here. E. D. Pierce, who knew the country, had led twelve prospectors, including Bassett, out from Walla Walla in August." Pierce organized and led; Bassett, on the marker's account, struck. Both statements belong in the entry; neither should swallow the other. [P7]

A second party founded the town of Pierce on December 3, 1860. By June 1861 "several thousand unemployed gold hunters" were waiting around Pierce for the season to open; Oro Fino City sprang up two miles away near strikes on Rhodes Creek and lasted only briefly, while Pierce, seat of the newly established Shoshone County, "proved to be a permanent community." Shoshone County cast the largest vote in Washington Territory in the congressional election of July 8, 1861. "Several million dollars in gold eventually came out of the Pierce area," and after most white miners left, "hundreds of Chinese came in to continue mining in Idaho's oldest camp for years." The Moore Gulch Chinese Mining Site near Pierce (National Register, address restricted) is the registered remnant of that second mining generation — it is evidence in the story, not a visitor destination. [P1, P8]

The rush needed a river port, and that is Lewiston's origin. The steamer *Colonel Wright* first landed at the confluence of the Snake and Clearwater in May 1861, and Lewiston was founded that month as "a supply center for the mines at the head of the steamboat navigation" — a tent city that became Idaho Territory's first capital in 1863. The two sources below disagree on the day (May 13 vs. May 19); see Section 5. [P9, P10]

The courthouse is the rush made permanent. The National Register nomination records that on May 26, 1862 the Shoshone County commissioners authorized construction of a courthouse and jail; it was completed in August 1862 at a cost of $3,700 — a two-story building of hewn logs, thirty-six feet by twenty, on sixteen-foot posts. When the county seat later moved, the building was sold to Ed Hammond for $50. The state marker calls it what it is: "Shoshone County's original courthouse — and Idaho's earliest public building — still stands in Pierce." [P2, P6]

A caution on color: a romantic origin tale — a Nez Perce companion, a glittering "eye of the Great Spirit" shown to Pierce — circulates in later popular histories. It is tradition, retold at third hand, not a documented event, and no Nez Perce source in this pass tells it. The entry may name it as a legend miners told; it must not tell it as what happened. The documented Nez Perce presence in this story is Wislanaeqa's camp, the treaty boundary, and the refusal — that is the record, and it is enough. [P1]

## 2. Then / now

Then, Pierce was the supply-starved center of Idaho's first gold rush: thousands of men, a county government organized in a mining camp, and a sister city (Oro Fino City) that boomed and vanished two miles away. Now, Pierce is a small living town on Highway 11 where the 1862 courthouse still stands one block off Main Street, and the industry that succeeded gold — logging — keeps the adjacent museum. The NPS notes the courthouse wayside "summarizes the impact of gold mining on the Nez Perce" and eight interior panels detail the area's history: the federal interpretation itself pairs the discovery story with its cost to the Tribe. [P1, P3]

Oro Fino City is the then/now counterweight: a community larger than Pierce in the summer of 1861 that "lasted only briefly." No verified surviving fabric or public site treatment for Oro Fino City was located in this pass; do not route visitors to a pinpoint for it. [P1]

## 3. Surviving features and access classification

- **Pierce Courthouse (1862):** National Register-listed (ref. 72000100), a component site of Nez Perce National Historical Park, owned/operated under the Idaho State Historical Society with Bradbury Museum volunteers. Exterior: public street viewing year-round. Interior: on request and summer weekends only, per NPS (statement dated 2018 — verify). [P3, P4, P5]
- **J. Howard Bradbury Memorial Logging Museum:** adjacent, in a historic 1920s log cabin; a Lewis & Clark Trail listing gives hours of 12:00–4:00 p.m. without stating a season in the text retrieved — treat hours as unverified until the museum's own current listing is read. It is the practical keyholder for courthouse interiors. [P11]
- **Discovery-site marker (Canal Gulch / Oro Fino Creek):** Idaho roadside marker program; HMdb indexes a "Gold Rush" discovery marker for the Bassett strike (m=121518) and a Pierce party marker (m=121490). Exact present-day pullout condition and legibility: not field-verified in this pass. [P7]
- **Moore Gulch Chinese Mining Site (10-CW-159):** National Register-listed 1983 (ref. 83000285), ADDRESS RESTRICTED. Story evidence only. No routing, no coordinates, no find task. [P8]
- **Lewiston (downstream pair stop):** founded May 1861 as the mines' steamboat port; its own National Register historic district. A separate entry's subject; included here only as the rush's river door. [P10]

## 4. Trip-shaped stop progression

**1. Start at the discovery marker on the Gold Rush Historic Byway (Highway 11).** Read the Bassett/Pierce wording closely — the marker itself splits credit between the man who led and the man who struck. That split is the first interpretive act of the stop. Pullout condition unverified; if the marker cannot be safely read, do not improvise a stop on the highway shoulder. [P7]

**2. Pierce Courthouse, exterior.** Stand on Court Street and read the building before the panels: hewn horizontal logs, two stories, built in a single summer of a boom that was already leaking away to Elk City and Florence. If the Bradbury Museum is open, ask for the courthouse interior and its eight panels; if not, the NPS wayside outside carries the Nez Perce impact summary. [P2, P3]

**3. Bradbury Logging Museum.** The second industry explains why Pierce survived when Oro Fino City did not: timber outlasted placer gold. This is the stop that keeps the entry from being a one-season story. [P11]

**4. Close with the trespass, stated plainly at the courthouse.** The discovery was made on land from which the discoverers were "excluded by law and by treaty," and the government that followed was organized in the building in front of the visitor. No ceremony, no reenactment — the fact, in the place.

**Strongest story moment:** at the courthouse door. A $3,700 log building, sold off for $50 when the county seat moved, is now the oldest public building in Idaho — the entire arc of the rush (trespass, boom, government, abandonment, preservation) is legible in one small structure. The $50 sale price is from the National Register nomination; quote it there, not from memory. [P2]

Inter-town driving (Lewiston–Pierce via Highway 11/Greer Grade) belongs in the regional trip plan, not in an invented estimate here.

## 5. Honest gaps and conflicting dates

- **Courthouse date, 1861 vs. 1862:** the Idaho State Historical Society's Pierce–Weippe site report (Reference Series No. 194) calls it "the Pierce Courthouse (1861)." The National Register nomination is specific and documentary: authorized May 26, 1862, completed August 1862. Print both; the nomination's paper trail is the stronger claim, but the discrepancy is not this dossier's to erase. [P7, P2]
- **Lewiston's founding day, May 13 vs. May 19, 1861:** ISHS Reference Series No. 334 gives May 13, 1861; the Lewiston Historic District nomination gives May 19, 1861. Both agree on month, year, and cause. Print the disagreement wherever a day is stated. [P9, P10]
- **Who struck first:** the marker credits W. F. Bassett's strike at Canal Gulch on September 30, 1860; ISHS No. 8 credits the party collectively ("discovered some very promising placers") and separately records Pierce's own February 20, 1860 find with Wislanaeqa on the North Fork — an earlier, quieter discovery that started nothing because it was not announced. Three distinct events; keep them distinct. [P7, P1]
- **No verified words of E. D. Pierce himself** were located in this pass. ISHS No. 8 is a state historian's narrative, not a Pierce memoir. Do not put quotation marks in Pierce's mouth until a letter or published statement of his is read directly.
- **The "great jewel / eye of the Great Spirit" tale** is later tradition (it appears in Hailey-era retellings), unverified and not a Nez Perce published account. Legend status only.
- **Current interior access** rests on an NPS page last updated in 2018 and a museum listing without a stated season. A same-season confirmation from the Bradbury Museum is a release gate for any interior promise.
- **No verified decimal coordinates from a primary register document** were adopted. HMdb prints a marker location (below); the courthouse's own coordinates are left blank rather than copied from an aggregator.
